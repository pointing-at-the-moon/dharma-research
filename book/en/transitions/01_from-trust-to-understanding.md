## Transition ① · From Trust to Understanding

The last chapter of Part I ended on a single line: "In Part II, we'll meet you there." The line is spoken, and the page turns.

This middle page is a breath.

A transition is not a new chapter, not the chapter zero of the next part, not the eleventh chapter of the part before. It is a space left open on purpose between two parts—**a place for the reader to pause, and for the book to pause too.** Move too fast and the weight of the part just finished has not yet settled before the new material of the next part is already bearing down; neither side can catch the other. Pause, and recognize one thing: the road we just walked—where did it bring us? And the road ahead—why is it not simply a continuation of the same road, but another way of walking altogether?

---

By the time the part on trust is finished, you are holding three things: **a direction, a set of instruments, and a map.**

The direction is the aspiration—you know which way you mean to move. The instruments are the implements of mind—the receiving surface of the mind has been cleared once through. The map is the thirty-seven factors of awakening—the one prepared and ready two thousand five hundred years ago. With these three in hand, someone who wants to practice is no longer the kind who turns in baffled circles on the spot.

Is that enough?

In the language of the part on trust, it is enough. But at the very moment you take these three things in hand and prepare to move—something appears that **the language of trust has no way to handle.**

It goes like this. When a person begins to generate the aspiration, begins to practice the thirty-seven factors, begins to put in the work, every moment of right mindfulness, every stretch of effort, every stage along the factors of awakening is quietly caught by something that has not yet been seen—and that something is called "I." "I" am generating the aspiration; "I" am practicing; "I" am making the effort; "I" am putting in the work. The harder the practice, the more solid that "I" grows.

This is not a matter of anyone going astray. It is the natural structure of how mind-consciousness works. But if it is never pointed out, it will keep running, and it will turn the whole of one's practice around and feed it into another kind of grasping—**the grasping at the very fact that "I am practicing."**

Ten years on the cushion; the meditation hall has become a place one can truly sit in; even the temper looks softened—yet inside, the "I" is more solid than it was ten years ago. On the surface, practice; underneath, fortification. This is the most hidden, most common, and hardest-to-see-for-oneself of all the dead ends.

To avoid this dead end, one needs something that does not yet exist within the language of trust.

---

This something is called **understanding.**

But the word "understanding" is easily read wrong, and a correction has to be made first.

⚠️ Many people, on first meeting the prajñā scriptures, give rise to one immediate misreading: "If all things are empty, then surely cause and effect, karma and its ripening, the generating of the aspiration—everything taught before—has been emptied out too?"

No. **Understanding does not cancel trust; understanding keeps trust from hardening into another block of stone.**

Cause and effect are real. The working of the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness—is real. The thirty-seven factors of awakening genuinely work. And at the same time—not one of these real things possesses an independent self-nature. To lack an independent self-nature does not mean they do not exist; it does not mean they do not function; still less does it mean that taking them up to practice is wasted practice. It means one thing only: **do not seize them as "my attainment," as "the truth I hold in hand," as "a component fitted to me, the practitioner."**

This layer of distinction is something the language of trust cannot say. It needs another language—a language made specifically for "seeing" rather than "doing."

Change the image. Trust gives you a toolbox; understanding gives you a mirror. A toolbox handles problems; a mirror does not handle anything—a mirror only shows what is, as it is. A person who practices needs both. The tools in the left hand, the mirror in the right; the left hand does the work, the right hand sees the work. These two hands cannot be merged into one—merge them, and you get either blind motion that does without seeing, or empty talk that sees without doing.

Trust gave the left hand. What understanding is here to give is the right.

---

The Buddha taught prajñā for twenty-two years. At the end He condensed this whole language of "seeing" into a single, shortest scripture—two hundred and sixty characters.

How those two hundred and sixty characters do this, Part II will walk through chapter by chapter. But a transition does not preview the content; it leaves only a direction—

The Heart Sūtra does not ask the reader to forget the previous ten chapters. It asks the reader to **see those ten chapters again, with a different pair of eyes.** By the end of Part II, the direction is still that same direction, the instruments still those same instruments, the thirty-seven factors of awakening still those same thirty-seven—only the hand that holds them has loosened.

And what is recognized in the moment of that loosening is neither loosening nor tightening—neither holding nor letting go. It is that which has been watching this hand from beginning to end. It never held anything, so it has nothing to release; it never grasped at anything, so it has nothing to be freed from. This "that which has been watching all along"—belonging to no part, no chapter, no stage of the whole business of practice—is the **original awareness** that the part on understanding is here to let you recognize.

It is not at the end of the road. It is the very pair of eyes by which that road is walked.

Part II begins with the title of the Heart Sūtra.
