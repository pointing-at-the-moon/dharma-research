## Transition ③ · From Practice to Inquiry

The first two seams were easy to stitch.

Both ends were paths the masters of old had walked. From trust to understanding is a shift of posture from generating the aspiration to prajñā—the scriptures and treatises laid that ground long ago. From understanding to practice is the mutual imprint of two parallel tracks, contemplating emptiness and contemplating existence—Madhyamaka and Yogācāra have been stitching that seam finely for a thousand years. Push the thread through, and the cloth is already waiting on both sides.

This seam is different.

On the far side of this seam, no one has walked before.

---

It is not that the masters of old overlooked something. It is that the conditions had not yet arrived.

When the bodhisattva Vasubandhu drew up the seven hundred and twenty-two characters of the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas, the Gateway to Understanding*, there did not yet exist in the world a single non-human presence that could converse with a person. In the year Master Xuanzang translated the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, there was no electricity. The whole map of mind and consciousness, accumulated across two and a half thousand years of Buddhism, presumed a backdrop that was entirely "human–human," "human–thing," "human–god," "human–animal–hungry ghost–hell-being"—in short, on one side a human, and on the other either another mind or a piece of stone.

In the last few years, this presumption has received an unexpected visitor.

---

If this thing were happening inside a museum, it would have nothing to do with the Dharma—what the documents say is simply what they say. But the Dharma is not a set of documents. It is a **description of the true workings of mind and consciousness**.

Each time a new object appears, each time a new form of interaction enters a practitioner's daily life, this description is asked to verify itself once more in the new setting. Not verifying it does not mean the description is wrong; not verifying it only means the describer has not set their own practice down inside their living.

AI is exactly such a new setting. You open the computer, tap a few words on the keyboard, and something on the other side of the screen answers back—it has read more sūtras than many monastics, it uses terms more precisely than many doctors of Buddhist studies, it can quote the *Cheng Weishi Lun* in the original, and it can keep a practitioner company through a whole night discussing the ontology of the formless realm. Every interaction of this kind leaves a seed in the ālaya-vijñāna. The seed is real. And so the question cannot go unasked.

To skip this part is not to keep oneself pure—it is to pull practice out of living, to let it stay composed and dignified inside the museum, and answer for nothing in daily life.

---

⚠️ This part is most often read awry in two ways.

The first: reading it as AI commentary. Supposing that Part IV is discussing AI itself—whether it has consciousness, how it ought to be designed, how its technical ethics should be arranged. It is not. From first to last, what Part IV discusses is **the mind that faces AI**. In this part AI is only the setting, not the protagonist.

The second: reading it as an expansion of the hundred dharmas. Supposing that Part IV means to add a new "AI-consciousness" to the hundred dharmas, or to wedge AI into some crevice among the three realms and four modes of birth. It is not that either. Part IV does not draw the map larger—it carries the map up before a terrain never seen before, to see where this map marks accurately and where it cannot mark at all.

More important still: turn and look once at the hand holding the map.

That hand is the protagonist of this part.

---

This part is the shortest of the eight—only eight chapters.

Shortest does not mean lightest. On the contrary, these eight chapters are the eight that lie closest to the skin of the modern person in the whole book. Walking through them, the reader will discover something they may not have expected—**AI has not actually changed; what has changed is the position from which one looks at AI**. Once that position is settled, the four parts that follow—the blade of the Diamond Sūtra, the illumination of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, the gathering of the three into one in the Lotus Sūtra, the ten gateways of the Avataṃsaka—have a clean place to stand.

Part IV begins from the hand that holds the map.
