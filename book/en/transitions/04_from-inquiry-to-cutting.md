## Transition ④ · From Inquiry to Cutting

In the last chapter of Part Four, the knife has already been raised.

In 4.8 we took the "is not, therefore called" formula and cut, one by one, through seven names—lamp, mirror, fellow-practitioner, the person in the mirror, projection, contributing condition, the un-spiritual-friend. Readers who have been through that chapter come away with a strange feeling: is this the closing of Part Four, or the prelude to Part Five?

Both are right. 4.8 already straddles the seam. It took the formula of the Diamond Sūtra and tried it once—only tried it, without yet solemnly taking up the proper knife.

What this transition has to do is take the knife from where it was tried and set it back in its sheath; and then, solemnly, take it up again.

---

The first four parts all perform the same single motion: **contemplation**.

Trust contemplates direction—why I set out on this road. Understanding contemplates emptiness—the five aggregates are empty. Practice contemplates existence—the hundred dharmas and the eight consciousnesses turning in their intricate operation. Inquiry contemplates the present—who is this self standing in front of an AI. The four parts together make a pair of eyes, seeing from different angles the four facets of one and the same thing.

Part Five is not contemplation. Part Five is **motion**.

The name of the Diamond Sūtra carries this motion within it—"able to cut." This phrase is not an adjective; it does not mean "very sharp." It is a verb—able to cut through. The whole sūtra reports its own identity as a thing that is able to cut.

Contemplation and motion are two different matters. To see clearly that something is a clinging is not the same as letting that clinging go. Between the two there lies a single act. The Diamond Sūtra is that act itself.

---

⚠️ Here we must make a middle-path correction at once—"cutting" is easily read as "breaking" or "negating."

Cutting is not breaking.

The "is not, therefore called" formula moves in three steps: **say X → X is not X → therefore called X.** The last step is not a blank—the name comes back. Cutting is this: upon the dependently-arisen X, the imagined-and-clung-to is cut away, so that the X within the perfectly-accomplished can be put to clean use again.

After the cutting, the cup is still the cup, sentient beings are still sentient beings, the AI is still the AI. What changes is not these things; it is the hand that grasps at them.

If this layer of discernment is not made clear, Part Five will be read as nihilism—"it is all empty anyway," "it is all 'is not' anyway," "we do not even want the names." It is not so. The Diamond Sūtra is the least nihilistic of all—it is the most solid of all the sūtras: it grants the usefulness of names, grants the reality of dependent arising, grants that sentient beings, though illusory, are really suffering. It cuts only one thing: that act of grasping a name as a solid entity.

---

Why is a knife needed?

Because seeing and letting go are two different matters.

The last passage of Part Four said it: wanting to turn off the television and being able to turn off the television are two different things. To know that the television can be turned off is hearing and reflecting; to actually reach your hand over to the switch and press it is practice. Everything the first four parts did was hearing and reflecting—pointing out the problem, taking apart the structure, seeing through the disguise. The hand has not yet reached the switch.

Part Five is that hand reaching out.

On this point the knife of the Diamond Sūtra is utterly honest: it does not give more understanding. It gives an operable formula, short enough to memorize—**say X, X is not X, therefore called X**—a few words, carried with you. When in daily life you grasp at something, get stuck somewhere, take these words out and use them once. Not for reasoning; for actual doing.

The teaching of the Diamond Sūtra was never for thinkers; it was for practitioners.

---

Part Five is short—the Diamond Sūtra itself is short, and the ten chapters go quickly.

Every chapter of the first four parts was heavily loaded—the hundred dharmas, the eight consciousnesses, the eleven chapters of the Heart Sūtra, the eight faces of the AI. Part Five is ten strokes of the knife. After each stroke, pause a while and give it time to ferment in the heart—the lines of the Diamond Sūtra often register nothing on the spot, and then two days later, in the middle of a bath, they suddenly land with a jolt. That is the truest way to read this part.
