## 5.8 One Cannot See the Tathāgata Through Marks — Where Is the True Buddha?

### §26 — The Passage Where Subhūti Answered Wrong

The last chapter closed on a sharp question. By the end of the first half of the teaching, the reader has learned "do not abide in marks," learned "all marks whatsoever are illusory," learned "generate the mind without abiding anywhere." So then, after all these marks have been cut away, where is the true Buddha?

The Diamond Sūtra puts this question squarely on the table in §26. The Buddha asks Subhūti:

> "Subhūti, what do you think — can one observe the Tathāgata through the thirty-two marks?"

This is not the first time the question appears in the Diamond Sūtra. In §13 the Buddha asked the same thing, and Subhūti answered "No" — correctly. By §26 the very same question comes around again, and this time Subhūti answers:

> "Yes, yes — through the thirty-two marks one observes the Tathāgata."

Wrong.

A great arhat who has listened to twenty-five sections of teaching — why does he stumble at this exact juncture? The Buddha breaks it open at once with a single line:

> "Subhūti, if one could observe the Tathāgata through the thirty-two marks, then a wheel-turning sage king would be a Tathāgata."

This is a reductio. If the proposition "one can observe the Tathāgata through the thirty-two marks" holds, then a wheel-turning sage king — who also has the thirty-two marks — would be a Tathāgata too. The conclusion is absurd, so the premise is false.

Subhūti has not regressed. It is that the cut the Diamond Sūtra makes in §26 is finer than the one in §13. §13 cuts the coarse thing — an ordinary person sees the Buddha's thirty-two marks and straightaway takes those thirty-two marks to be the Buddha. That is a simple attachment, easy to break. §26 cuts something different — it cuts the back door a practitioner who has already understood the first half of the teaching may still secretly leave open in their heart: "I know the thirty-two marks do not equal the Tathāgata, but I can use the thirty-two marks to *infer* what the Tathāgata is like."

Not taking marks for the Buddha. Using marks to *deduce* the Buddha. This layer of attachment is harder to spot than the previous one, because it looks so reasonable — the Buddha has thirty-two marks, doesn't the scripture say so?

The Diamond Sūtra says: no. Not even this back door.

### The Image in the Mirror Is Not the Mirror

In his commentary on the Diamond Sūtra, Kuiji left a very sharp analogy:

> "The thirty-two marks are nothing but reflections manifested upon mirror-wisdom. Since they fall within the category of the conditioned, they are subject to the four marks of impermanence."

The thirty-two marks are reflections projected by great mirror-wisdom — one facet of the Buddha's perfect wisdom. A reflection is a conditioned phenomenon; it arises and ceases, and it shifts according to causes and conditions. To different beings the Buddha may display the thirty-two marks, or may not; the marks displayed have to do with the occasion for teaching, and bear no direct relation to the dharma-body itself.

This is like the image in a mirror. The image depends on what is placed before the mirror — a person walks up, there is a person in the mirror; a flower is set there, there is a flower in the mirror. The images are always changing, but the mirror itself does not change.

To know the nature of the mirror, one key move must be done right — not staring at the image in the belief that understanding the image equals understanding the mirror. The image is the mirror's **function**, not the mirror's **essence**. Understanding the image and understanding the mirror are two different things.

Kuiji pushes the mirror analogy one step further:

> "It is like a mirror that reflects the image of a person along with other objects — not only where the person's image is absent is it the mirror; where the other objects appear, too, it is all the mirror."

The mirror is not the mirror only where there is no image — where there is an image, the mirror is there too. This one line blocks an easy misunderstanding in advance: to understand "one cannot see the Tathāgata through marks" is not to strip all marks away, not to look for some place with no image at all in order to find the dharma-body. The dharma-body is present where there are marks as well.

The problem is not whether marks are present or absent. It is the eye that looks at the marks.

### The Dharma-Body Is Neither Form nor Mind

What is the dharma-body, after all?

The *Cheng weishi lun* gives a precise definition — the dharma-body is "apart from marks, quiescent, beyond all discursive elaboration," and moreover "cannot be spoken of as form, mind, or any such thing."

This carries great weight. The *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* divides all dharmas into five classes: form-dharmas (eleven), mind-dharmas (the eight consciousnesses), mental factors (fifty-one), factors not associated with mind (twenty-four), and unconditioned dharmas (six). The dharma-body is in none of the first four classes — not a form-dharma, not a mind-dharma, not a mental factor, not a factor not associated with mind. Is it then an unconditioned dharma? Yes, and yet not in the sense of those items among the six unconditioned dharmas taken as conceptual labels — it is the "truly pure dharma-realm," containing the boundless merits of the four wisdoms.

To put it plainly: the dharma-body is not a thing that can be seen, nor a thing that can be thought. It cannot be reached by the senses, nor grasped by concepts.

This does not mean the dharma-body is empty. The *Cheng weishi lun* stresses precisely this — the uncontaminated realm "contains boundless, rare, great merits." The dharma-body is full; it is only that this fullness does not exist in the mode of marks.

Yogācāra divides the Buddha's body into three levels —

**Dharma-body**: apart from marks, quiescent, unconditioned, invisible. This is the true Tathāgata.
**Enjoyment-body**: the pure form-body displayed once wisdom is complete — invisible to ordinary beings, visible to the bodhisattvas in the pure lands.
**Transformation-body**: the various forms displayed for teaching sentient beings; the thirty-two marks belong to this class.

The "Buddha" the reader meets in the various sūtras — walking, teaching, going on alms-round, entering nirvāṇa — is the transformation-body. It is the form the Buddha displays in order to meet sentient beings where they are. The dharma-body is not at this level.

Then why does the Buddha still display a transformation-body? Because the faculties of sentient beings are still at the level of marks — they need an object they can see, can hear, can bow to, can question. The transformation-body is an expedient, not the ultimate. To take the transformation-body for the ultimate is like taking the raft for the far shore — the raft is to be used, but one cannot stay on the raft.

### If One Sees That All Marks Are Non-Marks, One Sees the Tathāgata

The Diamond Sūtra does not stop at negation. Back in §5, well before §26, it had already planted a positive thesis:

> "All marks whatsoever are illusory. If one sees that all marks are non-marks, then one sees the Tathāgata."

Read the two clauses together. The first breaks — all marks are illusory, not the way you take them to be. The second establishes — if, within all marks, one can see the "non-mark" of the marks, then one has seen the Tathāgata.

Notice the precision of this line. It is not "do not see marks" — not shutting your eyes to see nothing. It is "see that marks are non-marks" — in the very act of seeing, seeing that the mark is not the inherently real mark you grasp it to be.

Kuiji's reading of this line: "If discrimination does not arise, marks naturally do not arise… what remains is nothing but the awakened nature — this is called 'seeing the Tathāgata.'" Not annihilating marks — but stopping the discriminating act in the mind that labels marks and reifies them. Once that act stops, marks return on their own to their original dependent nature — illusory arising, illusory ceasing. What remains is the awakened nature itself — which is the Tathāgata.

So "seeing the Tathāgata" is not using the eyes to "see" an image of a Buddha, nor using the intellect to "infer" what the dharma-body looks like. It is a fundamentally different kind of "knowing" — prajñā realization. It is wisdom itself.

There is a line in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra worth setting alongside this:

> "When seeing sees seeing, that seeing is not seeing. Seeing is still apart from seeing — seeing cannot reach it."

When the mind attempts to "see" seeing itself, the "seeing" it sees is already not the seeing that can see — it has been objectified, turned into yet another thing seen. The true seeing-nature always withdraws one step behind, on the side of what can see, not on the side of what is seen.

The dharma-body is the same. If a person says "I have seen the dharma-body" — then the thing they saw is already not the dharma-body, but one more image of the dharma-body after it has been objectified. The true dharma-body is never on the side of what is seen.

This sounds as though it pushes the dharma-body somewhere unreachable. It does not. It pushes it somewhere **that cannot be grasped**. Cannot be grasped does not equal does not exist; cannot be grasped is, on the contrary, the dharma-body's most fundamental mode of being.

### Bhikṣuṇī Utpalavarṇā and Subhūti

The commentary on the Diamond Sūtra records a small story that puts the point of this chapter most clearly.

Once the Buddha ascended to Trāyastriṃśa heaven to teach his mother, and after three months descended again to the human world. Bhikṣuṇī Utpalavarṇā very much wanted to be the first to see the Buddha — she even transformed herself into a wheel-turning sage king with a full retinue and went forth in ceremony to meet him. The Buddha saw it and rebuked her.

At that same moment, Subhūti was sitting in meditation in the mountains. He too had thought of going to see the Buddha, but then on second thought: "The principle of emptiness and marklessness is the true dharma-body. What need is there to see a form-body?" And he went on sitting, and did not go.

Two people, two acts. Bhikṣuṇī Utpalavarṇā saw the Buddha through marks — only on seeing the Buddha's form-body did she count it as having seen the Buddha. Subhūti saw the Buddha through prajñā — within the empty, markless dharma-nature, the Buddha was already there.

This does not mean that seeing the form-body must be wrong — the teaching function of the transformation-body cannot be replaced; bowing to the Buddha, circumambulating the stūpa, gazing upon a sacred image all have their value, and these are the proper use of the dependent nature. But if a practitioner stops at the level of marks, thinking that only seeing the form-body counts as seeing the Buddha, then they are just like Bhikṣuṇī Utpalavarṇā.

⚠️ A necessary correction: saying here that "Subhūti's act is deeper" is not to belittle Bhikṣuṇī Utpalavarṇā. Nor is the Diamond Sūtra teaching people not to see the Buddha, not to bow to the Buddha, not to contemplate images and recollect the Buddha. What it cuts is the grasping — taking the mark one sees for the ultimate Buddha. Marks may be used; they may not be grasped.

There is one more way to go astray that must be blocked in advance: if, having read this chapter, the thought arises in your heart, "Then from now on I won't bow to the Buddha, won't contemplate images, I'll just empty out all marks directly" — this is turning the negation into a new posture. The Diamond Sūtra already warned of this in §14 — "Departing from all marks — that is called 'all Buddhas'" — and this "departing" is not "absence," it is "non-grasping." Bow to the Buddha as you should; only, while bowing, know in your heart: what is bowed to is the transformation-body, what is seen is an image, and the true Tathāgata is in the reality of these images — not in the images themselves, nor outside the images.

### The Analogy of a Piece of Music

Put the principle another way.

When a piece of music is performed, every single note is real — it can be heard, recorded, analyzed one by one. But where is "the music" itself?

In no single note. Listen to the first note alone — it is not the piece. Listen to the second — still not. List out all the notes one by one, write them on paper — still not. Music needs notes, but music is not the sum of the notes.

If someone asks, "What is good about this piece?" people begin to enumerate — melody, rhythm, harmony, emotional tension. And after the enumeration is done, there is a feeling in the heart: none of the things enumerated is "the music" itself. Music is more real than any feature that can be enumerated — and yet it simply cannot be turned into a thing you can point at and say "this is it."

This is the everyday structure of "one cannot see the Tathāgata through marks." The thirty-two marks are like those notes — real, analyzable, but not the music. The dharma-body is like the music — it needs notes to be presented, but it does not equal any one note, nor the sum of all the notes.

No one says "music does not exist" just because they cannot point to which note the music is in. By the same principle, one cannot say "the dharma-body is empty, there is nothing there" just because one cannot point to which mark the dharma-body is in.

The dharma-body is in all marks, and yet is not any one mark. This is no abstruse speculation — this is the way everything that truly matters exists.

---

§26 makes the finest cut in the second half of the Diamond Sūtra: not only are marks not the Tathāgata, even using marks to infer the Tathāgata fails. But this is still not the last cut. The verse that closes §32 pushes the whole sūtra's negation to its limit — "All conditioned phenomena are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, like dew and like lightning — one should observe them thus."

It is not only the Buddha's thirty-two marks that are images. All conditioned phenomena — what you see, what you hear, what you think, what you have lived through, what you have grasped at — all of it.
