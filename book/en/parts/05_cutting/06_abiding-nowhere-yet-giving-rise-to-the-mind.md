## 5.6 Abiding Nowhere, Yet Giving Rise to the Mind — Non-Abiding and Mind-Arising at Once

### Ten Characters

Of the Diamond Sūtra's more than five thousand characters, one line is the most famous of all:

> One should give rise to a mind that abides nowhere.

Ten characters in the Chinese. Even people who have never read the Diamond Sūtra have, for the most part, heard this line. The most dramatic awakening story in the whole history of Chinese Buddhism turns on exactly these words—we already saw it in chapter two. The Sixth Patriarch Huineng was pounding rice in the threshing shed when, in the middle of the night, he was summoned to the Fifth Patriarch's room. The Fifth Patriarch did not explain the Diamond Sūtra to him. He simply read it aloud. And when he reached these ten characters—**Huineng awakened on the spot.**

But why these ten characters?

Every line in the Diamond Sūtra carries the "is not, therefore called" structure; every line could become a public case. "All marks are illusory" is a public case. "Even the Dharma should be let go, how much more so what is not the Dharma" is a public case. So why is it precisely these ten characters that have come down to us as the loudest, the ones the most people carry on their lips?

The answer is this: these ten characters are the single hardest operating instruction in the whole Diamond Sūtra.

"Abide nowhere"—do not come to rest anywhere at all.
"Yet give rise to the mind"—and at the very same time, still let the mind arise.

Two actions, to be done at once. The difficulty lies entirely in that "at once." Most people read it as two steps: first achieve non-abiding, then give rise to the mind. Read that way, it is completely wrong—and yet almost everyone reads it that way the first time through.

What this chapter sets out to do is to make clear the "at once" that joins non-abiding and giving rise to the mind.

### "Yet" Means Simultaneous, Not Sequential

The whole problem gets stuck on a single connecting word—the Chinese 而, which we are translating here as "yet."

In Chinese, 而 has several uses. "Learn, and then practice it in season"—here it marks sequence: first learn, then practice. "Learning without thinking is perilous"—here it marks contrast: learn, but do not think. "Broadly learned and firmly resolved"—here it marks parallel coordination: broadly learned and at the same time firmly resolved. One and the same word, different in meaning depending on its context.

In "abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind," the 而 can only be parallel coordination, simultaneous—never sequence.

If you read it as sequence, you immediately run into two problems.

The first problem: if "non-abiding" is the prerequisite and "giving rise to the mind" is the result, then what is the mind doing during the stage of "achieving non-abiding"? Not giving rise to anything? A mind that gives rise to nothing is dead ash; it is annihilation. The Dharma has never taught anyone to cultivate a mind like cold dead ash.

The second problem: if "giving rise to the mind" is an independent action, then what kind of mind is it that arises? If the mind that arises abides somewhere, then it is just the ordinary mind—no different from before you began to practise. And if the mind that arises already abides nowhere, then "non-abiding" and "giving rise to the mind" are in fact two faces of a single event, not two sequential steps.

The Diamond Sūtra itself, in §14, seals this logic shut with one line:

> If the mind has something it abides in, that is precisely non-abiding.

Once the mind "abides," that abiding is not genuine abiding. What does this mean? If someone supposes they have reached "non-abiding"—"there, I am now abiding nowhere"—the very thought "I suppose I am abiding nowhere" is itself a place of abiding. Genuine non-abiding is not a state you can arrive at. It is the manner in which the mind operates.

Kuiji, in his commentary, gave this an exceptionally precise formulation:

> The earlier instruction to not abide in form and so on negates existence; the later instruction to give rise to the mind negates nothingness. Free of both existence and nothingness is precisely the Middle Way.

"Negating existence" and "negating nothingness" are two negations completed at the same instant. **Non-abiding = negating existence** (not grasping things as truly existent); **giving rise to the mind = negating nothingness** (not collapsing into annihilationist void). Falling off neither edge at the same time—that is the Middle Way.

So "abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind" is not two steps. It is two-faced description of one and the same action. Just as when water flows, "flowing" and "wetness" are not two sequential steps—the very moment the water flows, it is wet; flowing and wetness are the same thing.

### Not Abiding in Form, Not Abiding in Sound

"Abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind" is not an isolated maxim. The complete passage in §10 reads:

> The bodhisattva-mahāsattvas should give rise to a pure mind in just this way. They should not give rise to a mind abiding in form; they should not give rise to a mind abiding in sound, smell, taste, touch, or dharmas. They should give rise to a mind that abides nowhere.

The Buddha lists the six sense-fields one by one—form, sound, smell, taste, touch, dharmas—the six channels of cognition. Only then does He sum it all up with "abiding nowhere."

This is no casual list. These six fields correspond to the objects taken in by the six consciousnesses—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—the entire set of entrances through which a person cognizes the world. The Buddha's meaning is this: **every channel of cognition can become an entry point for abiding, and every one of them must be watched.**

What is the "abiding" in "giving rise to a mind abiding in form"? In the language of the previous chapter: it is laying a layer of the imagined nature over the dependently-arisen activity of cognition. The eye sees red (the dependently-arisen—a real cognitive event), and then the mind instantly produces the judgment "this is a real, independent red flower" (the imagined nature—a reifying grasp laid on top). That is "abiding in form."

"Not giving rise to a mind abiding in form" does not mean not looking; it does not mean closing your eyes. The eye still sees, cognition still operates (giving rise to the mind), but onto that cognition no further layer of "there is a truly existent red flower" is laid (non-abiding).

§10 sets this passage in a particular position. Immediately before it stands a line in the "is not" structure:

> One who adorns buddha-lands is not adorning; that is called adorning.

"One who adorns buddha-lands" is the dependently-arisen (the bodhisattva is indeed doing the work of adorning buddha-lands). "Is not adorning" negates the imagined nature (there is no substantial "adornment" that a bodhisattva can add to a buddha-land). "Is called adorning" is the perfected nature (seeing, truthfully, that adornment is illusory yet present).

This three-part "is not" formula and "abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind" are the same thing said another way. The very moment a bodhisattva adorns a buddha-land is the very moment of non-abiding (not grasping the adornment as truly existent) together with giving rise to the mind (truthfully doing the work of adorning the buddha-land). It is not first cultivating non-abiding and then going off to adorn the buddha-land—it is adorning the buddha-land and abiding nowhere, both at once.

The Vimalakīrti Sūtra puts it even more directly:

> If a bodhisattva wishes to obtain a pure land, they should purify their mind; as the mind is pure, so the buddha-land is pure.

A pure land is not built up out there on the outside; it is what is seen once the mind has been purified. And how is the mind purified? Exactly by abiding nowhere while giving rise to the mind—not grasping form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or dharmas as truly existent, while at the same time operating truthfully within them all.

### The Mind Has Never Abided Anywhere

The Diamond Sūtra says "one should abide nowhere"—and this is a practice instruction. But the Śūraṅgama Sūtra looks at the same matter from another angle, and its conclusion is more thoroughgoing still: **it is not that you ought to abide nowhere; it is that your mind has never abided anywhere at all.**

In fascicle one of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, the Buddha asks Ānanda a question: where is the mind?

Ānanda gives seven answers in a row—the mind is inside the body; the mind is outside the body; the mind is hidden behind the eye-faculty; the mind is the thing that sees darkness; the mind is present wherever the body makes contact; the mind is in between; the mind is just this matter of "non-attachment to everything."

Seven answers, and seven times the Buddha breaks them down: there is no such place.

The seventh answer is the one most worth noticing. At the end, Ānanda says: "Non-attachment to everything—that is what I call mind." This is almost the literal sense of "abiding nowhere." And yet the Buddha breaks even this: if "non-attachment" is the definition of mind, then "non-attachment" itself has become a mark that can be held, and the person is once again "abiding"—now abiding in "non-attachment."

The conclusion of the sevenfold examination of the mind is not "the mind is in an eighth place." The conclusion is that **the question "where is the mind?" is itself the wrong question.** The mind has never been an object that can be located, named, or set up anywhere.

This and the Diamond Sūtra's "abiding nowhere" are saying the same thing, only from opposite directions. The Diamond Sūtra speaks from the side of practice: one should abide nowhere. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra speaks from the side of how things originally are: the mind's very nature is non-abiding; it has never once abided.

Put the two together, and "abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind" is no longer a demand that is hard to meet—it is **a pointing-out of the mind's own native way of working.** The mind is non-abiding to begin with; affliction is the extra layer laid on top. Strip away that added layer, and the mind returns to its native operation: abiding nowhere, and at the same time giving rise to the mind.

### Where Does the Mind Abide When You Drive?

Let us bring this chapter down into ordinary life.

You are driving on the highway. Both hands on the wheel, eyes fixed ahead, foot on the accelerator and the brake. You judge distances, watch the traffic, react—every one of these actions is the mind operating; every one is giving rise to the mind.

Throughout all of this, where does your mind abide?

Not in the steering wheel—you hold it, but you do not cling to it. Not in the speed—you control the speed, but you do not fixate on some number. Not in the thought "I am a person who is driving"—you are simply driving.

This is the everyday version of "abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind": **fully engaged, and yet not clinging.** The mind is operating at full tilt, but there is no extra thought of "I am the one doing this" laid on top of it.

But this state is fragile. You drive along, and a thought arises: "Wow, I am really focused right now." That single thought, and you have become distracted. Your attention has shifted from the road to self-evaluation.

This is the subtle entry point of "abiding." Abiding does not necessarily settle on the outer object; more often it settles on "my own state." The self-awareness of "I am really abiding nowhere right now" is precisely a new place of abiding.

⚠️ The driving image is only a structural pointer, not an equivalence. The everyday "focus without distraction" is still a temporary condition of the defiled mind, whereas the "non-abiding, mind-arising" the sūtras speak of is the natural operation of the whole cognizing mind after its basis has been transformed—that is permanent, not occasional. The image points the way and stops there; do not take it for a proof of the Dharma.

### "I Have Already Achieved Non-Abiding"

We have to deal with the most hidden misreading of all.

Having finished this chapter, a thought may arise in your mind: "Good, I've got it—non-abiding and giving rise to the mind happen at the same time." The next time someone talks about practice, you will say "well, one should abide nowhere"—with the tone of someone who has understood.

And here is the trouble. Under the sentence "I have already understood non-abiding," a new "abiding" is taking shape. You are no longer abiding in form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or dharmas—you are abiding in "I have understood non-abiding."

This is the hardest layer the Diamond Sūtra has to deal with: **even "non-abiding" itself must not be grasped as truly existent.** The Śūraṅgama Sūtra's seventh examination already diagnosed this illness clearly: Ānanda said "non-attachment to everything is what I call mind," and the Buddha broke it as "there is no such place." To take "non-attachment" as a definition that can be held is itself a form of attachment.

Genuine non-abiding is not a state you can **reach.** It is the look that naturally appears once the mind has been restored to its native way of working—not something you produce, but what shows itself once the added layer of affliction has been removed. For exactly this reason, the thought "I am going to achieve non-abiding" can never achieve non-abiding—because "I am going to achieve it" is itself a place of abiding.

It is like trying to fall asleep. The harder you try to sleep, the less you can. Genuine sleep is not produced by effort; it happens once the body relaxes. "Abiding nowhere, yet giving rise to the mind" has a similar structure: it is not a goal reached through effort, but the way of working that naturally appears once practice has matured to a certain point.

But this does not mean you should not practise. Quite the opposite—without long practice, you could not even see the fact that "I am abiding in 'non-abiding.'" Practice is not the cultivating of "non-abiding" as a state; it is the cultivating of **the capacity to see, each time, that you have quietly come to rest somewhere again.** Once you see it, that abiding loosens a little. Again and again.
