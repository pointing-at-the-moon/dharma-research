## 5.9 All Conditioned Phenomena Are Like Dreams, Illusions, Bubbles, and Shadows — Illusory Does Not Mean Empty Nothingness

### The Final Cut Falls

The previous chapter said that the Tathāgata cannot be seen through the thirty-two marks — that was the sharpest cut in the second half of the Diamond Sūtra, but it was not yet the final cut. The verse that closes §32 pushes the entire sūtra's negation to its extreme:

> "All conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, shadows, like dew and like lightning — one should contemplate them thus."

The scope suddenly opens out. It is no longer only the Buddha's physical body that is like a shadow — it is **all** conditioned phenomena. Every phenomenon born of causes and conditions — what you have seen, heard, thought, lived through, clung to — all of it falls within these six similes.

For most people, the first thought that arises upon reading this line is: "So everything is fake, nothing matters."

That very thought is one of the core attachments the whole Diamond Sūtra sets out to dismantle — annihilationism.

The six similes are not saying "everything is fake." What this chapter sets out to do is to take the six similes apart one by one — to see exactly what they negate, and exactly what they leave standing. Because what the Diamond Sūtra truly means to teach is not to make you despair of the world, but to let you see the way the world actually exists.

### Six Similes

Why did the Buddha use six similes rather than one? Because each simile illuminates a different facet of conditioned phenomena.

**Dream** — the illusoriness of subjective experience. While you dream, everything in the dream is real: what you see, hear, and feel is, in the moment, one hundred percent experience. Fear makes your heart race; joy makes you smile. Only upon waking do you realize none of it was substantially real. But "not substantially real" does not equal "never happened" — the emotions in a dream, the fright in a dream, genuinely did occur.

**Illusion** — appearing as existent without any self-substance. An illusionist of old would conjure elephants, horses, and armies; the spectators took them for real, yet they had no substance. Nāgārjuna puts it precisely: "Although magical transformations are truly empty, they can cause sentient beings to give rise to sorrow, anger, joy, and delusion." The illusion has no self-substance, yet it genuinely produces emotional reactions. That is the precise meaning of "illusion" — not fake, but appearing as existent without any self-substance.

**Bubble** — the fragility of conditioned aggregation. Foam on the water's surface arises when water and air meet, and the slightest touch breaks it. The point of the bubble simile is not brevity but conditions — the existence of conditioned phenomena depends entirely on the maintenance of conditions; shift the conditions, and the dharma is no longer there.

**Shadow** — existing in dependence on another. A shadow arises in dependence on light and an object; remove the light and the object, and there is no shadow. But a shadow is not "nothing" — you genuinely see it, and it genuinely moves as the object moves. The shadow simile points directly to dependent arising — it exists in dependence on another, not by its own substance, yet it genuinely exists.

**Dew** — utterly fleeting, but real while present. A dewdrop forms in the early morning and evaporates the moment the sun rises. In the very moment it exists, the dewdrop is completely real — you can touch it, you can taste it — it simply does not last.

**Lightning** — illuminating in an instant, then gone. Lightning flashes across the dark night, lighting up everything for an instant before it vanishes. The lightning simile has two faces: one is impermanence (not abiding even for an instant), and the other hints at prajñā — prajñā illuminating the illusoriness of conditioned phenomena is like lightning illuminating the dark night, brief and intense.

The six similes fall into two groups. The first four (dream, illusion, bubble, shadow) stress "not substantially real" — conditioned phenomena are not the kind of substantial reality you take them to be. The last two (dew, lightning) stress "impermanence" — even if they exist, they are utterly fleeting. Put together, the two groups form a complete contemplation: conditioned phenomena are neither substantially real nor permanently abiding — yet they do genuinely arise within conditions and genuinely produce effects.

⚠️ A detail easily missed: the other Chinese translations of the Diamond Sūtra (Bodhiruci, Yijing) give nine similes — star, cataract, lamp, illusion, dew, bubble, dream, lightning, cloud. Kumārajīva translated only six, and this was a deliberate decision in translation. The three similes "star, lamp, cloud" possess "substance" and possess "arising" — a star has a luminous body, a lamp has fuel, a cloud can give rise to rain — and so they easily prompt the reader to imagine substantial reality. Kumārajīva deleted these three and kept the six similes best able to point directly at "no substance." This is not a loss; it is a choice made by prajñā.

### Seemingly Existent Yet Non-Existent — Where Is the Middle Way

The doctrinal core of the six similes is captured by the *Cheng weishi lun* in four characters: **seemingly existent yet non-existent**.

"Non-existent" — not the kind of substantial reality ordinary people take things to be. The table at your side is not an independent, self-subsisting entity — it is a phenomenon manifested through the convergence of countless conditions: wood, glue, a craftsman's skill, the human concept that classifies it. Withdraw any one of those conditions, and "the table" no longer exists.

"Seemingly existent" — yet it genuinely manifests as existent through the convergence of causes and conditions. The table can hold a book, can be written on; within conditions it genuinely performs its function.

Non-existent, yet not nothing. Not substantially real, yet not nihilistically void. The Middle Way is right here.

This point is especially important, because the Diamond Sūtra is most easily misread as annihilationism — "everything is illusory, so nothing exists and nothing matters." The Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra left a line precisely to guard against this misreading:

> "Birth-and-death is like an illusory dream, yet karma therein is not destroyed."

Birth-and-death is like an illusory dream — but karmic cause and effect do not vanish on account of illusoriness. If you strike someone in a dream, upon waking you have not actually hurt anyone — but within the "dream" of saṃsāra, karmic cause and effect operate in full. Illusory does not equal without causality. Illusory is, on the contrary, the precise way that cause and effect operate.

### One Should Contemplate Them Thus

The final five characters of the six-simile verse are the landing point of the whole verse — and also the five characters most easily taken lightly.

"One should contemplate them thus."

"Contemplate" is not "know." After reading this chapter, you may feel "I now know that all conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows" — but "knowing" is not "contemplating." Knowing is a grasp at the conceptual level; contemplation is a sustained cognitive practice in which body and mind both take part. Master Zongmi makes the point in a single line: "Contemplation is the wondrous wisdom of prajñā." The "contemplation" of the six similes is prajñā observation, not conceptual understanding.

This distinction is what the Diamond Sūtra has stressed again and again all along — and it is the last time the "knowing / realizing" divide established in the second chapter (the Kōan Mirror) comes back around here. You can recite "all conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows" flawlessly; you can even write an academic paper analyzing the precise meaning of all six similes — but all of that is still at the level of "knowing." What "contemplation" asks for is this: the next time you face pressure, entanglement, pain, or delight, can that illusory observation arise in the very moment?

⚠️ The *Cheng weishi lun* gives a very level-headed reminder: truly "comprehending the paratantra nature as being like illusory events" is the function of subsequently-attained wisdom — you must first realize suchness through non-discriminating wisdom, and only then, within subsequently-attained wisdom, can you truly "comprehend" the illusory. This means: the six similes are not a contemplation you can carry out the instant you read them; they are a direction, a map. But this does not mean that practitioners who have not yet realized suchness have nothing to do with the six similes — their function is this: each time you face conditioned phenomena, they remind you, "This is real experience, but not the kind of substantial reality you take it to be." This reminder is not itself prajñā, but it is prajñā's preliminary — it loosens the reifying attachment to conditioned phenomena, preparing the way for the final realization of prajñā.

### In the Movie Theater

Let us put this principle from another angle.

You are sitting in a movie theater. On the screen, the protagonist struggles on the brink of life and death. Everyone knows this is a movie — acted by actors, shot by a director, made with special effects — yet your heart still races. When the protagonist is rescued, you breathe a sigh of relief; when the villain is brought to justice, you feel a faint satisfaction. After the credits roll you walk out of the theater, a cool breeze hits you, and you return to "reality" — yet the emotions of that hour and a half inside the theater genuinely happened.

"Knowing it is fiction" does not make the experience disappear. Knowing the movie is fake, you still laugh and cry — because the experience itself is dependently arisen, arising in dependence on the director's narrative, the actors' performances, your own memories and emotions — and in the moment it arises, it is real.

But "knowing it is fiction" does change one thing: you are not trapped inside it. After leaving the theater, you do not go on worrying about the characters. You do not call the police to report that someone was kidnapped. You know it was a story — real experience, but not a substantially real world.

"All conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows" is like the Buddha saying: you have been sitting in a movie theater all along — your life, your work, your relationships — all of it is a "movie" of converging causes and conditions. It is not fake — your pain is real pain, your joy is real joy. But it is not the "independently self-existing entity" you take it to be.

What is "one should contemplate them thus"? It is not asking you to leave the theater (that would be the world-transcending nirvāṇa of the two vehicles, a path the Buddha does not encourage Mahāyāna practitioners to take); it is not asking you to pretend the movie is no good (that would be the false practice of suppressing emotion) — it is asking you to know, while watching the movie, that you are watching a movie. To keep awareness alive within experience, without being trapped by experience.

⚠️ This analogy has one important boundary: a movie theater has a clear "entering" and "leaving"; practice does not. No one can "walk out" of saṃsāra's theater — you can only awaken while the "movie" plays on. The Diamond Sūtra's contemplation is not fleeing from conditioned phenomena; it is transforming cognition within them.

### Illusory Does Not Mean No Need to Practice

There is one more misreading to block off first.

When you read "all conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows," if the next thought to surface is "since everything is illusory, practice is illusory too, so why practice?" —

this question hides a premise: "illusory things produce no effects." But the very core of the six similes is the opposite — **illusory things do produce effects**.

Fear in a dream is real fear — waking from a nightmare, your heart races, your body is soaked in cold sweat. "Like a dream" does not equal "no effect." An illusionist's spectacle leaves the audience gasping — "like an illusion" does not equal "useless." The tears in a movie theater are real tears — "like an illusion" does not equal "did not happen."

Nāgārjuna's words apply once more here: "Although magical transformations are truly empty, they can cause sentient beings to give rise to sorrow, anger, joy, and delusion." Conditioned phenomena are illusory, but illusory conditioned phenomena genuinely produce suffering and joy. Sentient beings suffer within illusory conditioned phenomena — and this suffering is real suffering. The meaning of practice does not vanish on account of illusoriness — quite the contrary: precisely because beings do not know the illusory and therefore suffer, practice has meaning.

The Great Prajñā Sūtra pushes this logic to its most thoroughgoing point:

> "If sentient beings could by themselves understand that all dharmas are like magical illusions and entirely not substantially real, then bodhisattva-mahāsattvas would not need to practice the bodhisattva path for immeasurable kalpas for their sake. Because sentient beings cannot by themselves know that all dharmas are like magical illusions and not substantially real — therefore bodhisattva-mahāsattvas practice the bodhisattva path for immeasurable kalpas for their sake."

Notice the structure of this passage — precisely because beings do not know that all dharmas are illusory, the bodhisattva must practice the bodhisattva path within the illusory. The illusory is not the termination of practice; it is the ground of practice.

The raft analogy of 5.4 comes back around here: the raft is illusory — but an illusory raft genuinely ferries you across the river. You need not wait until you "know the raft is illusory" before you begin crossing; you cross first, and upon reaching the far shore you naturally know. The six similes work the same way: you practice first, and at a certain stage you directly realize the illusory for yourself. Knowledge first, then practice is the conceptual path; practice first, then realization is the prajñā path.

### Seeing the World As Nirvāṇa

The *Poqu zhu buhuai jiaming lun*, translated by Yijing in the Tang, offers a final summation of the Diamond Sūtra's doctrinal import across the whole sūtra:

> "Therefore all buddhas and tathāgatas see the world as nirvāṇa — unlike the two vehicles who turn away from birth-and-death and delight in abiding in nirvāṇa without transforming beings. They see nirvāṇa as the world — unlike ordinary beings who never tire of birth-and-death and do not seek the fruit of transcendent nirvāṇa."

Seeing the world as nirvāṇa. Seeing nirvāṇa as the world.

This is the ultimate aim of the six similes. To contemplate all conditioned phenomena as illusory is not in order to leave the world — it is in order to see that the world itself is nirvāṇa. Not to discard this illusory world in search of another, real world; but, within this illusory world, to see as it is the way it actually exists — non-existent yet seemingly existent, seemingly existent yet non-existent — and that very place is nirvāṇa.

Master Zixuan sums up the whole Diamond Sūtra in eight characters: "Active yet perpetually serene, serene yet perpetually active." Functioning within conditioned phenomena (active), yet not bound by conditioned phenomena (serene); abiding in quiescent emptiness (serene), yet not departing from altruistic action within conditioned phenomena (active).

This is the entire lesson the Diamond Sūtra has taught across all thirty-two sections.

---

The Diamond Sūtra ends here. But S5 is not yet done.

The "construction" part of S3 spent fourteen chapters precisely classifying all dharmas — the hundred dharmas of the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*. That was a constructed map. By S5, the prajñā blade of the Diamond Sūtra has cut through the substantial reality of all of it — the hundred dharmas too are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows.

A question arises: after the cutting is done, what remains? Is the map of the hundred dharmas still of any use? Construction and deconstruction — what, in the end, is the relationship between them? That is what the next chapter — and the final chapter of Part Five — sets out to answer.
