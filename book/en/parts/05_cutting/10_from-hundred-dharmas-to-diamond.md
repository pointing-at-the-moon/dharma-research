## 5.10 From the Hundred Dharmas to the Diamond: The Dimensional Ascent of the Toolkit

### After the Cutting, What Remains?

We have come to the end of Part V, and the Diamond Sūtra's teaching is finished. We have cut all the way down the line—cut through marks (5.3), cut through the teaching (5.4), cut through merit (5.5), cut through the place where the mind abides (5.6), cut through "I am a practitioner" (5.7), cut through the Tathāgata's physical form (5.8), cut through all conditioned dharmas (5.9). All things are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, and shadows.

But the end of the last chapter left a sharp question hanging:

Part III, "Practice," spent fourteen chapters precisely assembling the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*—the eight consciousnesses, the fifty-one mental factors, the eleven form dharmas, the twenty-four dissociated formations, the six unconditioned dharmas—one hundred dharma-items in all, taking apart every operating facet of the mind. That was an exceedingly precise map. By the time we reached Part V, the prajñā blade of the Diamond Sūtra had cut clean through the whole of it—all ninety-four conditioned dharmas among the hundred are like dreams and illusions.

So then: is the toolkit of Part III still of any use?

This is not a rhetorical question. It is the confusion every serious practitioner is bound to collide with in the course of study and reflection—first you spend a great deal of time learning a precise analytical system, and then you are told it is all illusory—so what was all that earlier study? A waste of time?

This chapter answers exactly that question. The answer is not "throw out the hundred dharmas"—that is the nihilistic view. The answer is also not "the hundred dharmas are still what matters most"—that swings right back into reifying attachment. The answer is a more precise change:

**The hundred-dharma toolkit has not been destroyed—it has undergone dimensional ascent, from "substantial tools" to "illusory designations." The tools are still the same tools, but the relationship between the person and the tools has fundamentally changed.**

### The Map Is Not the Forest

A simple analogy can make this clear.

A person has been finding their way through a forest with a map. Part III taught this person how to read the map—which symbols stand for rivers, which for mountains, how to read contour lines, how to work the scale. This is a fine technique, and a necessary one—without the map, you get lost in the forest.

Part V tells them: the map is not the forest.

The "river" on the map is a blue line; the river in the forest is running water that can drown you. The "mountain" on the map is an area enclosed by contour lines; the mountain in the forest is rock you have to climb with your feet. To take the map for the forest—no matter how precise the map—is to take the symbol for the thing itself.

But the sentence "the map is not the forest" is **not** an instruction to throw the map away.

So long as the person is still in the forest, the map remains their only navigational instrument. The difference is this: before, they may have supposed "reading the map = walking the forest"—that if they only studied the map thoroughly enough they would arrive. Now they know the map is the map and the forest is the forest; they still look at the map, but they look at it in order to walk out, not in order to stop and dwell on the map.

The hundred dharmas are that map. What the Diamond Sūtra does is not tear the map up; it tells this person—you have been staring at this map, and the map is not the patch of ground you have to walk.

### Two Roads, One Answer

There is one detail worth particular attention.

The opening line of the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* is: "**As the World-Honored One has said: 'All dharmas are without self.'**" The whole treatise unfolds this single sentence—taking all dharmas apart into a hundred components, examining each one, and finding that not one of them is a "self." This is arriving at no-self by the **analytical** road.

The Diamond Sūtra §17 says: "What is called all dharmas is not all dharmas, and therefore is called all dharmas." And again: "Therefore the Buddha says: '**All dharmas are without self**, without person, without sentient being, without life-span.'" This is arriving at no-self by the **prajñā** road.

The endpoint of the two—treatise and sūtra—is the very same sentence: all dharmas are without self. Only the way of walking differs.

The hundred dharmas walk the road of "taking apart"—pulling all dharmas open, seeing each component clearly, discovering that within each component there is no "self" to be had. This is the rational road, the analytical road, the road of the mind-consciousness.

The Diamond Sūtra walks the road of "is not"—all dharmas are not all dharmas. It does not pull the dharmas apart; it cuts directly on the dharma—this dharma is not the dharma you took it to be. This is the prajñā road, the contemplative road, the road of non-discriminative wisdom.

The two roads are not opposed; they are complementary. Like a single mountain—you can climb it step by step (the gradual cultivation of the hundred dharmas), or, at some turning, suddenly catch sight of the whole (the sudden illumination of prajñā). The sudden cannot be had apart from the gradual—without the accumulation of all those earlier steps, the moment of glimpsing the whole would never come. And the gradual cannot be had apart from the sudden—if there were only the taking-apart and no illuminating sight of the whole, analysis would become an endless labyrinth.

### The Raft

All of Part V has returned again and again to one analogy—the raft.

5.4 said it: the teaching is a raft, used to cross the river, to be let go at the far shore. Now we can extend the analogy to the whole toolkit—

**The hundred dharmas are the raft.**

Part III spent fourteen chapters building the raft—the eight consciousnesses are the keel, the fifty-one mental factors are the planking, the form dharmas are the waterproofing, the dissociated formations are the ropes, the unconditioned dharmas point the direction of the far shore. This raft is precise, sturdy, fully functional.

Part V spent ten chapters teaching the river-crossing—the "is not" structure points out that the raft is not the shore; the kōan-mirror shows whether the practitioner is still clutching the raft; "all marks are false" says that what the raft looks like is not the point; "the Dharma should still be let go" says to release your grip on reaching the shore; the merit comparisons say not to count up the raft's mileage; "giving rise to mind without abiding" says not to take root on the raft; the dual-layer structure lets you see the raft's front half and back half; "the Tathāgata cannot be seen through marks" says the raft's outward appearance is not the Buddha; the six similes say the raft itself is illusory.

By the time we reach this chapter, the question is: you are already standing on the raft and you have seen the shore—now what?

The answer is not to throw away the raft.

The bodhisattva stands at the shore and looks back, and sees that across the river there are still beings in the water—what do they do? Build more rafts. Build them out of what? Out of the hundred dharmas, still.

But this time the manner of building is different—they know the raft is a raft, not the shore; they know the raft is illusory, yet the illusory raft truly does cross the river; they know the self that builds the raft is illusory too, yet the illusory self truly does have to build the raft.

Using illusory hundred dharmas to build illusory rafts to ferry illusory sentient beings—this is the hundred dharmas after dimensional ascent. The tools have not changed, but the one who uses them has.

### Basis-Transformation: What Changes Is Not the Tool, but the Relationship

Yogācāra gives this dimensional ascent a precise name: **basis-transformation** (*āśraya-parāvṛtti*).

The "basis" in "basis-transformation" refers to "the dependent"—the dependent nature, which is to say the hundred-dharma map itself. The "transformation" in "basis-transformation" is not transforming the dependent away; it is doing two things upon the dependent: **transformatively abandoning** the imagined nature, and **transformatively attaining** the perfected nature.

In plain terms: the hundred-dharma map has not been withdrawn. What changes is this—before, the person took the symbols on the map for real rivers and mountains (this is the grasping of the imagined nature); now they no longer do so (this is the abandoning). Before, the person never noticed the tabletop the map was spread out upon; now they see it—that tabletop was there all along, only it was covered over by the map (this is the attaining).

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says: "[One] can abandon parikalpita superimposed on paratantra, and can attain pariniṣpanna within paratantra."

The dependent nature has not changed—the hundred dharmas have always been the dependent nature. What changes is the person's **relationship** to the dependent: from "grasping it as substantial" to "using it while knowing it is illusory."

This is the precise mechanism of dimensional ascent. Not throwing away the toolkit (that is nihilism), and not enshrining the toolkit as a treasure (that is attachment)—but, while continuing to use the toolkit, changing your relationship to it.

The Master Kuiji left behind a famous illustration—**the rope and the snake**:

> "What appears to mind is called the dependent; grasping it as real is called the imagined; when both dependent and imagined are extinguished, this is the consummated. As a rope depends on hemp and a snake arises dependent on the rope—once the rope-snake both vanish, the hemp stands revealed."

Hemp is twisted into a rope (the dependent nature—the hundred-dharma classification); you see the rope and take it for a snake (the imagined nature—taking the classification for an entity), and you begin to be afraid. Someone tells you "the rope is not a snake"—and you are no longer afraid. You have not cut the rope—the rope is still there; you have simply stopped taking the rope for a snake. Walk on further, and you discover—the rope is not a rope either; the rope is hemp twisted up. To see the hemp is the perfected nature.

The hundred dharmas are the same. Prajñā does not cut the hundred dharmas off; it lets you see—the hundred dharmas are not the substantial structure of the mind (the rope is not a snake). Walk on further, and you see—the hundred dharmas are not even an independent "thing" (the rope is not a rope either)—they are the designation of suchness (the rope is hemp).

### Learning to Drive

Let me try one more analogy, a modern one.

A person learns to drive.

The first stage—they learn the traffic rules: stop on red, go on green, the speed limit, signal before turning. They learn how the engine works, the logic of shifting gears, the operating sequence. Every step has to be clearly remembered and deliberately carried out. While driving, their mind runs continuous calculations: "Accelerate now… check the mirror… three seconds behind the car ahead…" This is the state of hundred-dharma contemplation in Part III—using conceptual tools to analyze, one line at a time, the operations of the mind.

The second stage—after ten thousand kilometers, the rules have internalized. They no longer recall them line by line—they see a red light and stop naturally, they read the road and judge automatically. They no longer "know" they are using the clutch—the body has merged with the car.

The traffic rules have not vanished, the engine principles have not changed. They have only undergone dimensional ascent, from a "memorized checklist" to "natural awareness."

The Diamond Sūtra is not telling you to forget the traffic rules. It lets the traffic rules ascend from a checklist to awareness. The hundred dharmas are the same—ascending from "conceptual tools for line-by-line analysis" to "the prajñā wisdom of intuitive contemplation."

But there is an important boundary to mind: if a person who never learned the traffic rules takes to the road, they will have an accident. The construction of Part III is the precondition for the dimensional ascent of Part V—you cannot skip construction and ascend directly. This point needs saying plainly, because modern readers are prone to a romanticized misunderstanding: "Since it all has to be let go in the end, wouldn't it be more direct just not to learn it in the first place?"

There is a hidden error in this thought. To let go, you must first take up. What has never been taken up cannot be spoken of as let go—that is just "absence," not "letting go." A person who never learned the hundred dharmas saying they are "not attached to the hundred dharmas," and a person who studied the hundred dharmas through and saw them as illusory saying they are "not attached to the hundred dharmas"—they sound the same, but they are separated by the whole of practice. The former is ignorance; the latter is wisdom.

There is an old saying in the Chan tradition: "If you have not broken the first barrier, you cannot abide at the double barrier." To skip those steps and declare yourself at the summit—that is not arrival; that is not yet having found even the gate.

⚠️ So the order from the hundred dharmas to the Diamond is not from wrong to right, not from low to high; it is from construction to dimensional ascent. Construction is necessary. After the ascent, construction has not vanished—it has been newly resituated.

### Who Is the One Who Sets Down the Map?

Here Part V draws to its close.

From 5.1, the "is not" three-part formula, to the end of this chapter 5.10, the Diamond prajñā has run the complete arc—it established the tool of cutting (the "is not" structure), established the teaching of the mirror (the kōan-mirror), cut through marks, cut through the Dharma, cut through merit, cut through the abiding of the mind, cut through the practitioner's self, cut through the Tathāgata's marks, cut through all conditioned dharmas, and finally drew even the hundred-dharma toolkit into the compass of the illusory.

This prajñā blade is not for destroying. It is for liberating—liberating attachment to marks, attachment to the teaching, attachment to merit, attachment to "non-abiding," attachment to "I am a practitioner," attachment to the image of the Tathāgata, the reifying of all conditioned dharmas, and at last, the attachment to "liberation" itself.

What the Diamond Sūtra truly means for the reader to see at the very end is not "grasp this" or "let go of that." It is to let the reader see—**what you have been holding all along, and what you have all along been trying to set down, were never the thing you imagined them to be.**

But—Part V has ended; practice has not.

A question rises before us: in this whole process—learning the hundred dharmas, reading the Diamond, using the map, setting down the map—the one who is doing all of this, who is it?

Part III told the reader the structure of the mind. Part V taught the reader not to grasp the structure of the mind. Next, Part VI will ask directly: **the "mind" that this structure points to—where, exactly, is it?**

In the first fascicle of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra, the Buddha asks Ānanda directly: "Where is your mind?" Ānanda gives seven answers, and the Buddha demolishes them one by one—the "seven locations searching for mind." Demolished to the very end, Ānanda weeps—he discovers that after years of practice he has not truly settled even where the mind is.

The next part walks exactly this road. From "the structure of the mind" into "the mind itself." From the toolkit into the subject who holds the toolkit. From the map into the hand that holds the map.

This is the Śūraṅgama. This is the demon-revealing mirror. This is a barrier on the path of practice that there is no way around.
