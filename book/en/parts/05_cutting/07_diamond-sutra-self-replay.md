## 5.7 The Diamond Sūtra Replays Itself—The Same Question, Asked Again

### The Re-Asking at §17

In §2 of the Diamond Sūtra, Subhūti puts to the Buddha the question that sets the whole sūtra in motion:

> "Good men and good women who have generated the mind of supreme awakening—how should they abide? How should they subdue their minds?"

From §3 onward the Buddha answers—give without abiding in marks, transcend the four marks, all marks are illusory, even the dharma should be let go, abide with a mind that does not abide. He answers densely, thoroughly, all the way to §16.

Then, at §17, Subhūti opens his mouth again—

> "Good men and good women who have generated the mind of supreme awakening—how should they abide? How should they subdue their minds?"

Almost word for word the same. But look closely, and there is a subtle difference. In §2 the character *yīng* ("should") leads the phrase; in §17 it slides to mid-position—from "how-*should*-abide" to "how-should-*abide*." The word has shifted from the head of the sentence into its body.

This re-ordering is unlikely to be a translator's accident. Kumārajīva was one of the most precise translators in the whole history of Chinese Buddhism; his ear for cadence was exact. More to the point, the Buddha's answers to the two questions operate on entirely different levels—if the question were truly the same, the answer would not differ so radically.

§2's "how-*should*-abide" puts its weight on "should"—*what should I do?* This is the practitioner reaching for a rule, the typical question of a beginner. §17's "how-should-*abide*" moves the weight onto "how"—*what does it actually mean to truly abide?* This is no longer asking for a norm; it is asking about a mode of being.

Subhūti's re-asking is not a sign that he failed to grasp the first half of the teaching. Just the opposite—he grasped it. And precisely because he grasped it, a deeper question surfaced. The Diamond Sūtra has encoded this progression into itself. This is the boldest piece of pedagogical design in the entire sūtra—**the Diamond Sūtra replays itself once over**.

§17 is the pivot. Everything taught across the first sixteen sections gets a fresh press of the power button here. But what boots up is not the same system—it is the system one level higher. The same question; the first half's answer on one level, the second half's answer on a deeper one.

What this chapter does is make that dual-layer structure clear.

### Marks and Views—Three Pieces of Evidence

Besides the re-ordering of words, the Diamond Sūtra leaves two more pieces of textual evidence, encoding the difference between its two halves right into the page.

The second piece is in word choice. The first half speaks again and again of the "four marks"—the mark of self, the mark of person, the mark of sentient being, the mark of life-span. By the second half, at §31, the same set of concepts swaps out a single character:

> "Self-view, person-view, sentient-being-view, life-span-view are not self-view, person-view, sentient-being-view, life-span-view, therefore called self-view, person-view, sentient-being-view, life-span-view."

"Mark" has become "view." That one-character difference is a doctrinal upgrade.

A **mark** is the *object* of cognition—what you see. The first half cuts this: a person takes themselves to be a really existing "I," takes what they face to be really existing "sentient beings"—and these objects are illusory.

A **view** is the *manner* of cognition—what does the seeing. The second half cuts this: not only is the "I" you see unreal, but the very gaze that does the seeing—the cognitive frame that carries "there is an I here, looking"—is unreal too.

In the language of Yogācāra: the first half cuts the *grasped* (the object that is cognized); the second half cuts the *grasping* (the subject that does the cognizing).

A practitioner sets down the "mark of self"—good. And then? Is there still, somewhere inside, a "person who has set down the mark of self" standing there? If there is—then "I" has merely moved from being content to being location. The "I" that used to be a thought you were clutching has now become the very ground you stand on. Because you cannot see it, you assume it is gone.

The third piece of evidence is in the structure of "X is not X, therefore called X" itself.

In the first half, what this formula cuts is external names and forms—"to adorn the buddha-field is not to adorn, therefore called adorning," "what is spoken of as atoms is not atoms, therefore called atoms." The buddha-field one clings to, the atoms one clings to, the world one clings to—these are not what an ordinary person takes them to be. What gets cut is the outer object.

In the second half, what the formula cuts is upgraded to the tools of practice themselves—"all dharmas are not all dharmas, therefore called all dharmas" (§17), "these are no-mind, therefore called mind" (§18), "there is no dharma to teach, therefore called teaching the dharma" (§21), "the Tathāgata says wholesome dharmas are not wholesome dharmas, therefore called wholesome dharmas" (§23). Even "all dharmas," "mind," "teaching the dharma," "wholesome dharmas"—the very core concepts you reach for in practice—must each be cut once.

Word order, word choice, the object of the cut—three pieces of evidence point to one conclusion: §17 is not repetition, it is progression. Here the Diamond Sūtra folds itself once.

### Afflictive Obstruction and Cognitive Obstruction

Yogācāra gave these two layers of grasping their own names.

**Afflictive obstruction**—the affliction stirred up by grasping at an "I." Craving, aversion, delusion; the tangle of the passions; the recoil from birth, aging, sickness, and death—all of these begin from one assumption: "there is an I." Break that assumption, and the root of affliction loosens. This is the work of the first half.

**Cognitive obstruction**—the cognitive blindness caused by grasping at "the dharma." Practice to a certain depth and craving, aversion, and delusion grow fainter—but a subtler layer of grasping comes out: "I get it now," "look how far I've come," "this teaching is better than that one." These attachments do not breed craving or aversion, yet they screen off the direct seeing of truth. This is the work of the second half.

The two obstructions are not side by side; they are in sequence. Without breaking afflictive obstruction, talking about cognitive obstruction is pointless—a person still tangled in their own emotions is in no position to consider subtle attachment to the dharma. But the reverse is also true: break only afflictive obstruction and not cognitive obstruction, and practice stalls at a comfortable spot that is in fact stuck—the afflictions have faded, the person even looks the part, but they cannot get past that last threshold of "I am a practitioner."

In his commentary, Kuiji left behind a piece of very sharp logic:

> "Ordinary beings who have self-grasping necessarily also have dharma-grasping; the two vehicles have dharma-grasping but do not necessarily have self-grasping; bodhisattvas who have no dharma-grasping necessarily have no self-grasping."

Ordinary beings who grasp at self also, without fail, grasp at the dharma. The two vehicles—the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha—have cut only self-grasping; dharma-grasping remains, which is why they are released from birth-and-death yet cannot become buddhas. The bodhisattva's road is different—the bodhisattva aims at breaking dharma-grasping; and once dharma-grasping breaks, self-grasping is naturally gone.

This tells us something important: **breaking dharma-grasping is more fundamental than breaking self-grasping.** Afflictive obstruction is the branches and leaves; cognitive obstruction is the root. Branches and leaves are easy to see; the root grows underground, hard to see. The first half of the Diamond Sūtra cuts the branches and leaves; the second half digs out the root.

The first half pulls a person out of their afflictions; the second half pulls that person out of "the very self that has already been pulled out."

### Two Mirrors

In the second chapter we saw that the Diamond Sūtra is a mirror.

Now we can say it more precisely: the Diamond Sūtra is **two mirrors**.

The first half is the first mirror. It reflects a person's grasping at outer things—how much they are clutching, what they fear to lose, what they want to prove. Standing before this mirror, a person sees their own craving, fear, and self-defense. See it clearly, and the grasping loosens.

The second half is the second mirror. What it reflects is not the thing being clutched, but **the very act of clutching**. Harder still, what it reflects is **the posture of "having already let go."**

By the time the first half's teaching ends, the reader is carrying something new—"I have read the Diamond Sūtra now," "I already know that all marks are illusory," "I am not so attached anymore." These are real advances in practice—but at the same time, a new "I" is being rebuilt on the foundation of those very advances.

The re-asking at §17 pushes this new "I" up to the mirror. And so, after §17, the Buddha begins to say things that sound like the first half but in fact go one layer deeper:

> "There is truly no dharma that generates the mind of supreme awakening."
>
> "What is spoken of as all dharmas is not all dharmas, therefore called all dharmas."
>
> "The Tathāgata means the suchness of all dharmas."

There is no real "mind of awakening" that can be generated—the mind is generated, but neither the "I that generates the mind" nor the "mind that is generated" is a substance. "All dharmas" are all buddha-dharma—yet "all dharmas" itself is not. The Tathāgata is not an object—it is simply the suchness of all dharmas as they truly are.

Before §17, "prajñāpāramitā" was the knife used to cut. After §17, even the knife itself must be cut:

> "The Buddha says prajñāpāramitā is not prajñāpāramitā, therefore called prajñāpāramitā."

Prajñā, that knife, has cut the marks. But toward "prajñā," that very tool—are you not also clutching?

**The second mirror is always harder to face than the first.** Because the first reflects the mistakes you have made; the second reflects the places where you believe you have got it right.

### Harboring "I Am a Bodhisattva" Violates the Path of Non-Abiding

In his treatise on the Diamond Sūtra, Vasubandhu left a verse that diagnoses, with precision, exactly the sickness §17 means to cut:

> "Cultivating within the mind, harboring 'I am a bodhisattva'—this itself obstructs the mind, violating the path of non-abiding."

Four lines, sixteen characters, every line weighty.

"Cultivating within the mind"—this is no longer outer conduct (giving, keeping precepts, and the like); it is inner cultivation of the mind. Vasubandhu is explicit: the first half cuts outer conduct; what §17 cuts is different—the practitioner has already reached the level of the inner mind.

"Harboring 'I am a bodhisattva'"—there is, inside, a self-identification as "I am a bodhisattva." Surely a person who has generated the awakening mind and is walking the bodhisattva path ought to regard themselves as a bodhisattva? That is precisely where the problem lies—so long as that identification remains, "bodhisattva" becomes a status, and a status becomes something to be grasped, compared, shown off.

"This itself obstructs the mind"—this thought of "I am a bodhisattva" is, in itself, an obstruction of the mind. The problem is not in any outer act; it is that the inner self-identification of the practitioner has become the last wall.

"Violating the path of non-abiding"—it goes against the road of abiding nowhere.

This line hooks directly back to the last chapter. "Abide with a mind that does not abide." Vasubandhu's point is this: any "practitioner's I" that can be held and identified with violates non-abiding. After reading 5.6, if a thought arises inside—"now I understand non-abiding"—then that "I that understands non-abiding" is itself the everyday replica of "harboring 'I am a bodhisattva.'"

This sickness is not one you catch early in practice. The early sickness is "I am not good enough," "I have not practiced enough"—that is afflictive obstruction. This sickness only shows up after practice reaches a certain depth—"I am good enough now," "I have reached a certain place." It is the classic symptom of cognitive obstruction.

The second half of the Diamond Sūtra cuts this, again and again. "The bodhisattva does not receive merit" (§28)—the bodhisattva cannot take merit as a personal gain. "If one thinks, 'I shall liberate immeasurable sentient beings,' then one is not called a bodhisattva" (§17)—if you suppose you are liberating sentient beings, that thought itself is no longer a bodhisattva. "There is truly no dharma called a bodhisattva" (§17)—even "bodhisattva," that dharma, cannot be grasped.

Every cut lands in the same place: **the "I" that has already been practicing.**

This is the most awkward thing about the second mirror—the image it reflects is often a person who is in the middle of practicing, who looks very good, who themselves believes they are practicing. The sickness hides underneath "looks very good." The more earnestly a person practices, the more concealed this sickness becomes.

### Daring to Stand Before the Second Mirror

To walk the Diamond Sūtra all the way through, you must dare to stand before the second mirror.

This is far harder than standing before the first. What the first reflects are flaws—uncomfortable to look at, but at least there is a direction: just cultivate the flaws away. What the second reflects are virtues—no, more precisely, **the places you take to be virtues**.

"I have read this book already"—the second mirror, seeing this sentence, says: having read, and having understood, are two different things.
"I have understood already"—the mirror says: having understood, and having done it, are two different things.
"I have done it already"—the mirror says: having done it, and abiding on "I have done it," are two different things.

At each layer, the road of retreat is cut off.

But this is not a dead end—the Diamond Sūtra's design is not to corner the reader. It is to let the reader see: **any attainment in practice that can be grasped is not the final resting place.** When you truly see that there is nowhere to grasp, you have arrived—not because you found a new place to stand, but because you no longer need to stand anywhere at all.

This is exactly the "abide with a mind that does not abide" of the last chapter. §17 upgrades that line from the first half's teaching (§10) into the second half's mode of being. In the first half, "abide nowhere" is a lesson to be learned; in the second half, "abiding nowhere" is the whole person's way of operating.

Subhūti's re-asking at §17 is not that he failed to understand—it is that, having understood, he saw there was still a layer he had not reached. His re-asking is not confusion; it is awakening.

This too is where the whole Diamond Sūtra wants every reader, in the end, to arrive: not to finish reading the sūtra, but, in the course of reading, to catch sight of the little house labeled "I have read the Diamond Sūtra" that you keep quietly rebuilding each time—and then to smile, and tear it down once more.
