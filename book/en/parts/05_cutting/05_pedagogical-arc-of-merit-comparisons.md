## 5.5 The Reward Paradox of the Merit Comparisons—Destroying the Reward with the Reward

### A Sūtra That Teaches Letting Go—Comparing Merit

By the time you reach this point, you run into a structure that looks, on its surface, very strange.

The opening sections of the Diamond Sūtra have been teaching letting go all along—letting go of the mark of self, letting go of the mark of dharma, letting go of the raft. Then, starting at §4, a scene appears that keeps recurring: the Buddha asks Subhūti, "If someone filled a trichiliocosm with the seven treasures and gave them in charity, would the merit gained by this person be great?" Subhūti answers, "Very great." Then the Buddha says: "But if someone, within this sūtra, upholds even a four-line verse and explains it to others, the merit surpasses the former."

The same scene begins at §4 and returns at §8, §11, §15, §24, §28, and §32. Each time the scale of giving is raised—from the seven treasures of a trichiliocosm, to the seven treasures of Ganges-sand-number trichiliocosms, to giving away one's body and life three times daily without ceasing for immeasurable kalpas, to piles of seven treasures as tall as Mount Sumeru, to the seven treasures of immeasurable asaṃkhyeya worlds. Each time, the Buddha says the same thing: dharma-offering prevails.

This structure seems at odds with the style of the sūtra as a whole. A sūtra that teaches letting go—why does it stop here to compare rewards over and over?

Behind the surface contradiction lies a very precise pedagogical design. The Buddha is not setting up a new leaderboard—He is demonstrating a complete operation: **He uses the reward to pull you onto the raft, and once you are on the raft, He lets you set down the reward along with it.**

What this chapter sets out to do is to make this operation—"destroying the reward with the reward"—clear.

### The Same Sentence Spoken Seven Times

The seven merit comparisons look, at first glance, like repetition. But Vasubandhu pointed out, long before the Tang dynasty:

> "The phrasing is similar but the meaning differs; therefore it is not repetition."

Similar in wording, different in meaning. The same proposition is spoken seven times, but each time the context has shifted—so each time it cuts a different attachment.

First look at the escalation of scale:

| Section | Giving-Scale | Dharma-Offering Threshold |
|---------|-------------|---------------------------|
| §4 | Trichiliocosm of seven treasures | Uphold a four-line verse, explain to others |
| §8 | Ganges-sand-number worlds of seven treasures | Uphold a four-line verse, explain to others |
| §11 | Ganges-sand bodies-and-lives | Uphold a four-line verse, explain to others |
| §15 | 3× daily, Ganges-sand bodies × immeasurable kalpas | Hear this sūtra, faith without opposition |
| §24 | Sumeru-king heaps of seven treasures | Uphold a four-line verse |
| §28 | Ganges-sand worlds of seven treasures | Know all dharmas are non-self, attain patience |
| §32 | Immeasurable asaṃkhyeya worlds of seven treasures | Arouse the bodhisattva-mind, uphold the sūtra |

The scale of giving climbs from "imaginable" (a trichiliocosm) all the way to "beyond imagining" (immeasurable asaṃkhyeya worlds). The threshold of dharma-offering at one point drops to its lowest—§15's "faith without opposition"—even a single moment of faith that does not oppose surpasses the giving of one's body and life, three times daily, without ceasing, for immeasurable kalpas.

This design has a precise purpose: **to break the common scale.** If dharma-offering and material giving stood on the same scale, then there would be a "critical value"—give enough, and at some quantity you could surpass dharma-offering. By repeatedly raising the numbers of giving, the Buddha is saying: no matter how high you raise it, it will never do, because the two are simply not on the same scale.

The object of comparison also changes between the first half and the second. The first half (§4, §8, §11, §15) compares **behavior**—upholding and reciting. The second half (§28, §32) compares **state** and **aspiration**—"knowing all dharmas are non-self and attaining patience," "arousing the bodhisattva-mind." From "what you do" up to "what you are."

This is what Vasubandhu meant by "the meaning differs." The same sentence, "this merit surpasses the former," at §4 cuts the attachment "wealth equals merit," while at §28 it cuts the deeper attachment "doing more practice equals greater merit."

### This Merit Is Precisely Non-Merit in Nature

What is truly remarkable about the merit comparisons is that, more than once, they give away their own secret.

Subhūti's very first answer at §4 already lets the answer slip. When the Buddha asks whether the merit of giving is great, Subhūti says "very great"—but he adds a line:

> "This merit is precisely non-merit in nature; therefore the Tathāgata says the merit is great."

This line is the *jífēi* structure—the three-part "is not, therefore called" form shows up in full armor at the very first merit comparison.

Translated into modern terms: **this merit has no entity called "merit-nature"; precisely because it has no such entity can the Tathāgata conventionally call it "great."**

Kuiji's explanation is simple: "This merit is separate from the intrinsic nature of merit; therefore it is called great." Because merit is free of any fixed self-nature, it can be described as "great." If merit had a fixed self-nature, it could be neither "great" nor "small"—a self-natured dharma neither increases nor decreases.

This is entirely consistent with the doctrine of the preceding chapters:

- Merit itself ＝ paratantra (arising from conditions, genuinely useful)
- Clinging to merit as real ＝ parikalpita (treating the karmic result as something you can store away)
- Knowing, as it truly is, that merit is free of self-nature ＝ pariniṣpanna (rightly using it within the conventional name, not bound by the name)

So when the Buddha says "this merit is greater than that merit," He is not setting up a leaderboard. He is speaking inside a comparison system that **He has already dismantled from within.** Merit is "great," but "great" does not point to a quantity—it is a conventional designation, skillfully set up.

§19 gives a confirmation from the reverse angle:

> "If merit had substance, the Tathāgata would not say the merit is great; because merit has no [substance], the Tathāgata says the merit is great."

If merit were a real thing, the Tathāgata would in fact not say it is "great." Precisely because it has no self-nature can it be called "great." This line and Subhūti's answer at §4 are exactly two sides of the same coin.

Once you grasp this layer, look again at the seven merit comparisons—they were never a leaderboard from the start. They are a teaching tool that **comes with its own dismantling mechanism.**

### First Grasp, Then Let Go

Why would the Buddha use a tool that looks like a leaderboard but really is not?

In Yogācāra there is a precise name for the answer: **wholesome aspiration.**

The mental factor of *aspiration* (*chanda*) is not craving—it is a hope, a leaning of interest, toward some object. It can be directed toward the worldly five desires, or it can be directed toward wholesome dharmas. The *Yogācārabhūmi* has a line:

> "The root of both cyclic existence and cessation—this is aspiration."

The same mental factor, pointed in different directions, yields outcomes worlds apart. Worldly aspiration is the root of cyclic existence; wholesome aspiration is the root of cessation.

The *Cheng weishi lun* positions wholesome aspiration as the entry condition for practice—without wholesome aspiration, one cannot take even the first step. But wholesome aspiration does not arise out of nothing; it has a generative path: **hear the true dharma → gain pure faith → give rise to wholesome aspiration → practice.** The first step is hearing the dharma.

Look again at the merit comparisons—when the Buddha says "much merit" over and over, what He is doing is **arousing wholesome aspiration in the reader.** Reading "the merit of upholding a four-line verse and explaining it to others surpasses giving away the seven treasures of the entire universe," a thought stirs in the heart: "I want to try upholding this sūtra too." That stirring is the sprout of wholesome aspiration.

The Buddha does not say directly "you should practice"—He says "the merit of practice is great," letting the reader grow the impetus to practice out of their own aspiration. This is a very skillful pedagogical move. Demand that a person practice, and the person resists. Use "much merit" as bait, and the reader is pulled onto the raft by their own aspiration—actively, not passively.

But the Buddha will not leave the bait dangling forever. The seven comparisons raise the stakes higher and higher, and at the seventh, §32, there is a closing move that dismantles the entire reward structure along with it—which we will reach shortly.

The whole arc is a complete three-stage progression:

- **Stage one:** use "much merit" to arouse wholesome aspiration, turning the reader from worldly aspiration toward wholesome aspiration.
- **Stage two:** wholesome aspiration drives practice, accumulating uncontaminated seeds within the mind-consciousness.
- **Stage three:** even wholesome aspiration itself is set down.

Stage three cannot skip the first two stages. To tell someone who has never genuinely practiced that "merit is all dreams, illusions, bubbles, shadows"—that is not prajñā, it is lip-service prajñā.

### The Bodhisattva Does Not Receive Merit

The climax of the entire merit-comparison series is the line at §28:

> "Bodhisattvas do not receive merit."

Subhūti is immediately confused—how can a bodhisattva not receive merit? The Buddha's explanation is exact:

> "The merit a bodhisattva creates should not be clung to; therefore it is said they do not receive merit."

The key lies in the word "creates"—**the bodhisattva is still doing** merit. Not abstaining from doing, but doing without clinging.

Vasubandhu's reading of this line is extremely concise, using a very fine distinction—**receiving vs. grasping.** "Receiving" is acknowledging that merit exists (the level of conventional truth); "grasping" is clinging with a contaminated mind (parikalpita). The bodhisattva "receives" but does not "grasp"—doing good deeds, but not clinging to the karmic result.

This is the most exquisite paradox in the entire design of the merit comparisons: **the one with the most merit is precisely the one who does not care about merit.**

The more you cling to merit, the more contaminated the merit becomes—it can only ripen into maturation results within the three realms, never escaping the framework of saṃsāra. Doing without clinging—only then does merit truly lead toward the uncontaminated, only then can it touch liberation.

This paradox has an awkward way of being misused: after reading §28, some people will think, "Good, then I too will achieve not clinging to merit."—And this very "wanting not to cling" is itself a new clinging.

The kōan-mirror (5.2) works here once more. Hand your immediate reaction upon reading "the bodhisattva does not receive merit" to the mirror, and let it be reflected: if you think "I will learn not to receive merit"—the mirror reveals you are still at the parikalpita level. If it never even occurred to you to imitate, and you only saw "so this is how it is"—you may already be in paratantra.

"Not receiving" is not an action you can decide to perform. It is a cognitive shift that occurs naturally when practice reaches a certain depth. A deliberate "not receiving" is still receiving.

### The Points System and the Self-Destruct Device

Let us bring this chapter down into modern life.

Imagine you are using a language-learning app. After each lesson, the screen pops up: "Congratulations! 50 XP earned!" To accumulate more experience points, you open it every day—the points are the driving force.

A few months later, one day you discover that you can already converse fluently with foreigners in the new language. Looking back at those experience points now—they helped you build a study habit (the right use of paratantra), but what you actually learned—that ability to produce correct sentences without thinking—has nothing to do with the number of experience points.

If at this moment someone is still obsessively screenshotting their XP ranking every day, comparing "I have 300 points more than him," you can see it very clearly: what they care about is no longer learning itself, but the score itself. The tool has become the purpose—carrying the raft on the road.

The best gamification design has one feature: a **self-destruct mechanism.** After the user has established an autonomous learning habit, the system gradually fades the points—moving the person from "learning for points" toward "learning for learning's sake."

The Diamond Sūtra's merit comparisons are designed in exactly this way. The Buddha uses "much merit" to pull the reader onto the raft, uses "upholding a four-line verse and explaining it to others" to tell them what to do, and then at §32 closes the entire sūtra:

> "All conditioned phenomena are like dreams, illusions, bubbles, shadows, like dew and like lightning—one should contemplate them thus."

The six similes are the self-destruct device. **Even "much merit," the teaching tool itself, is a conditioned phenomenon, like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow.** This is not a negation of the preceding seven comparisons—that teaching was genuinely effective and truly pulled the reader onto the raft. The six similes are telling those who are already on the raft: now you no longer need to keep staring at the points. What you have truly learned has nothing to do with the points.

⚠️ Here a common misuse must be specially flagged: the six similes are written for those who are already on the raft. For someone still standing on this shore, the merit comparisons remain the tool they need. If, while you have not yet begun to read the sūtra and not yet begun to practice, you use "it's all dreams and illusions anyway" to excuse yourself—that is not letting go; it is simply never having picked anything up.
