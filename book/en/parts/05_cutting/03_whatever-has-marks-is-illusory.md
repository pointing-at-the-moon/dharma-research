## 5.3 Whatever Has Marks Is Illusory—The First Cut

### The First Cut

In the opening sections of the Diamond Sūtra, the Buddha has not yet said plainly what the *jífēi* structure is meant to cut. Subhūti asks, "How should one abide, how should one subdue the mind?" and the Buddha answers, "without self-mark, person-mark, being-mark, or life-span-mark"—this is the first time the target is named, but it is not yet stated as an open proposition. By §5, the Buddha speaks the matter very directly:

> "Whatever has marks is illusory; if you see that all marks are no-marks, then you see the Tathāgata."

This is the first time in the whole sūtra that the object the *jífēi* structure cuts is named in the open—"marks." The four marks earlier were only a beginning; this one sentence pushes the scope all the way out to "whatever has"—no exceptions, no blind spots.

This chapter does one precise thing: it lays out, layer by layer, what this first cut cuts, where it falls, and what remains once the cutting is done.

The sentence "whatever has marks is illusory" looks simple, and after reading it most people arrive at an intuitive understanding: "all appearances are false." But this "simple understanding" is exactly what the Diamond Sūtra is meant to illuminate—you have just pasted a new label, "marks = false," onto the situation and completed one fresh round of imagined nature. What lets you understand this sentence is not your comprehension; it is your letting go.

### Marks Are Not Appearances

First, one thing must be made clear—the "marks" the Diamond Sūtra speaks of are not "appearance" or "looks" in the everyday sense.

Yogācāra has a very precise definition of "marks." When a person sees a flower, this act of "seeing" is not the outer flower running directly into consciousness. The *Cheng weishi lun* says, "what resembles the object of cognition is called the object-aspect": when the eye-consciousness contacts the light-waves, it **projects** an "image of a flower," and this image, generated by consciousness itself, is called the **object-aspect**.

The object-aspect is of dependent nature—it arises through the convergence of conditions. Only when there is an eye, light, an object, and consciousness is there this object-aspect. It is not nothingness. The process of "seeing" is a genuinely occurring event.

But the next move is the decisive one. The moment you see the object-aspect, a judgment immediately arises in the mind: "this is a real, independently existing flower with a flower-nature." This judgment is not the object-aspect itself—it is superimposed on top. This superimposition is what Yogācāra calls **imagined nature** (*parikalpita*).

The *Cheng weishi lun* captures imagined nature in four characters: **emotionally existent, rationally non-existent**. From the standpoint of affective cognition, it is genuinely there (emotionally existent); under rational investigation, it is not the real thing you grasp it to be (rationally non-existent).

When the Diamond Sūtra says "whatever has marks is illusory," what it cuts is not the object-aspect but the invisible rope between you and the object-aspect—imagined nature. The flower is still there, the tree is still there, the whole world is still there—but the layer of reifying attachment to all of it is severed.

Kuiji, in his *Commentary on the Diamond Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra*, offers a beautiful image: mind is the support, marks the supported. All marks arise from mind; mind itself has no self-nature—and if mind is already illusory, how can the marks that arise from it possibly be real? If the skin no longer remains, where will the hair attach?

So the two words "illusory" mean, precisely: **not existing in the way you grasp it as existing.**

### Five Layers of Spectrum, Each Cut Deeper Than the Last

The "whatever has" of "whatever has marks" is no figure of speech—its reach the Diamond Sūtra unfolds layer by layer. There are five layers in all.

**Layer one: the four marks.** Self-mark, person-mark, being-mark, life-span-mark—in the mind of an ordinary being there is always a permanent, unchanging "I," an "other" distinct from "I," an individual bounded by this single lifetime, a timeline from birth to death. The core of the four marks is "I"—the other three are extensions of "I." This is the Diamond Sūtra's first cut, falling at the coarsest and most fundamental place.

**Layer two: dharma-marks and non-dharma-marks.** A person who has set down "I" often turns at once to grasping "dharma"—the Buddha's teaching is real, the teaching can be seized. §6 says, "one should not grasp dharmas, nor should one grasp non-dharmas." Not only dharma-marks must be set down; even non-dharma-marks—the concept used to negate dharma—must be set down too. You cannot leap from grasping "there is dharma" to grasping "there is no dharma," believing that "negating dharma" is the ultimate truth.

**Layer three: bodily marks and the thirty-two marks.** Can the Tathāgata be seen by means of bodily marks? This question is asked again and again across §5, §13, §20, and §26—four times, each time with a more precise object. A practitioner who clings to an image of a resplendent, golden Buddha is holding another imagined nature. The Buddha keeps asking the same question not because his memory is poor, but because the mirror is reflecting the same attachment from different angles.

**Layer four: no-marks.** "Self-marks are precisely no-marks; person-marks, being-marks, life-span-marks are precisely no-marks." Even "no-marks" cannot be grasped. You have seen that "marks are illusory," and so you begin to pursue "marklessness"—but the moment "marklessness" becomes a new goal, it is once again a mark. Sēngzhào's warning is sharp: "they will hope for a markless Buddha standing clearly before their eyes—if so, how does that differ from those very marks?" To expect a "markless Buddha" to appear distinctly before you—how is that different from the "marks" you grasped to begin with?

**Layer five: real-mark.** §14: "what is called 'real-mark' is precisely no-mark; therefore the Tathāgata calls it 'real-mark.'" The truest mark is precisely no-mark. If you are searching for a "final truth," what you find will only be another imagined nature. Real-mark is not finding something; it is setting down the search.

The five layers are not parallel but progressive. Each layer is subtler than the last. Each time you think you have let go, the Diamond Sūtra tells you—the very act of "letting go" has just produced a new attachment. Each cut goes deeper than the one before.

A pause is needed here. These five layers are not a progress bar for practice. "I've already cut to the fourth layer"—this sentence is itself a fresh product of the first layer (self-mark). To use the five layers to measure yourself, that measuring is imagined nature. The five layers are an analytical tool for seeing the structure of the Diamond Sūtra clearly, not a scale for grading yourself.

### Subhūti Answered Wrong at §26

The Diamond Sūtra has one very dramatic passage. Across the whole sūtra, Subhūti answers almost every question perfectly—the Buddha asks, and he answers without the slightest error. But there is one place where he is wrong.

§26:

> "Subhūti, what do you think—can the Tathāgata be perceived by means of the thirty-two marks?" Subhūti said: "Yes, yes! By the thirty-two marks one perceives the Tathāgata."

He had answered correctly at §5—"the Tathāgata cannot be seen by means of bodily marks." He answered correctly at §13 too—"the Tathāgata cannot be seen by means of the thirty-two marks." Then at §26, the same question put a different way, and he suddenly says, "Yes, yes."

Subhūti has not grown stupid. He has been caught before the mirror.

His correct answers from §5 to §13 were conceptual understanding—he **knows** "one should not see the Tathāgata by means of marks." His "wrong answer" at §26 exposes a deeper problem: he knows it conceptually, but in a single instant his cognitive habit slipped back into the default mode of "seeing by means of marks." Yogācāra speaks of the *manas*—the seventh consciousness, the self-centered cognitive habit that operates without ceasing—and it does not obey commands issued at the conceptual level. However correct the view established at the level of the mind-consciousness (the sixth consciousness), the *manas* will in the very next instant bypass all of it and run on according to its old habit.

This is one of the Diamond Sūtra's most finely calibrated pieces of pedagogical design. What §26 illuminates is not a gap in Subhūti's knowledge—his knowledge is sufficient. What it illuminates is the distance between knowing and realizing.

The Buddha's way of correcting him is also worth noticing. He does not say "you are wrong"; he says, "if the Tathāgata could be perceived by the thirty-two marks, then a wheel-turning sage-king would be a Tathāgata"—a wheel-turning king also has the thirty-two marks, so by this logic a wheel-turning king would be a Buddha. This is a reductio ad absurdum. The mirror does not say "your face is dirty"; the mirror simply reflects—the person sees for himself.

### The Designer Bag in the Department Store

Let us do a concrete exercise and bring this cut down into daily life.

You see a bag in a department store. You see its shape, its color, its material—this is the object-aspect, of dependent nature, and you genuinely see it. Then you see the brand logo on it—this is a nominal designation, a symbol. Then a cascade of reactions arises in the mind: "this is a luxury brand," "owning it will make me more sophisticated," "others will admire me," "I deserve it."

You think you are looking at a bag. You are actually looking at your own projections.

"Luxury" is not the bag's own self-nature. The same material, the same craftsmanship, without that logo, is no longer "luxury." The leather and hardware of the bag have not changed; what changed is only a symbol. What you cling to, from beginning to end, is not the bag—it is the whole string of concepts you have superimposed on top of it.

Once you become aware, the bag does not vanish. You still see it, you still know its brand name. You can buy it, or not buy it. But whether you buy it or not is no longer because you need that label to confirm who you are.

This first cut of the Diamond Sūtra cuts exactly this. It does not tell you not to look at the bag, it does not tell you to empty out your desires, it does not tell you "everything is false so do as you please"—what it cuts is that invisible rope: you assumed the logo referred to a real "noble self-nature," and then you tied yourself into it as well.

The object-aspect has not deceived you. Imagined nature is deceiving you.

### Illusory Is Not Empty Nothingness

One of the most common misreadings must be dealt with.

On reading "whatever has marks is illusory," there is a dangerous leap—straight to "therefore everything is false, non-existent, meaningless." In Buddhism this is called **nihilism** (*uccheda-dṛṣṭi*), one of the misreadings the Diamond Sūtra guards against most vigilantly.

The *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* gives a stern warning:

> "If one regards the dependent characteristic and the perfected characteristic as having no characteristics, one thereby also repudiates the imagined characteristic."

What does this mean? If, on hearing "all is illusory," you conclude that nothing exists at all—negating even the dependent nature, negating even the perfected nature—then you have not only failed to understand imagined nature; you have repudiated imagined nature itself along with everything else. This is an error deeper than imagined nature: imagined nature at least acknowledges that something exists, it merely mistakes what it is; nihilism negates the very fact that "something exists."

So the precise position of "illusory" is: **not existing in the way you grasp it as existing.** The flower is not non-existent—as a dependently arisen dharma, a convergence of conditions, the flower genuinely exists. But the flower is not what you think it is—there is no permanent, independent, essential "flower" waiting there.

There is a simple practical test. If, after reading this chapter, your feeling is "since everything is illusory, then it doesn't matter what I do"—then you have read it wrong. The next chapter will show that the Diamond Sūtra §6 immediately offers an image: the dharma is a raft, used to cross the river. Cross the river first, do not refuse to cross. Practice diligently first, do not stop practicing because of "illusoriness."

"Whatever has marks is illusory" is not the destination; it is the first cut. After the blade comes down, the rope between you and marks loosens, but you are still standing where you were—and only then does practice truly begin.
