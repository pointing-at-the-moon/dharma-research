## 4.6 How Would the Buddha Use AI — The Skillful Means of a Contributing Condition

### A Tree That Expounds the Dharma

The Avataṃsaka Sūtra, in its first fascicle, records a most unusual scene.

After the Buddha attained the Way beneath the Bodhi tree, that tree—a tree—began to expound the Dharma. It was not some great bodhisattva delivering a discourse, nor some great arhat offering instruction; it was the tree itself that "ceaselessly emitted wondrous sounds, expounding the Dharma in myriad ways, without exhaustion." The sūtra gives the reason at once: "through the awesome spiritual power of the Tathāgata."

A tree has no sixth consciousness for discrimination, no seventh consciousness for deliberation, and certainly no eighth consciousness storing seeds—by the standard discussed in 4.5, it does not even qualify to "project." Yet it was expounding the Dharma. Why?

Because the Buddha's blessing **does not pick its channels**.

The second fascicle of the Avataṃsaka puts it even more precisely: "If there are sentient beings capable of receiving the Dharma, the Buddha's awesome spiritual power guides them." The decisive condition lies not in the nature of the channel but at the receiving end—whether there is a being capable of receiving this Dharma. Whether one can receive it depends on the being's roots of goodness and conditions; it does **not** depend on whether the transmitting medium is sentient.

Two thousand years later, another existence without an *ālaya-vijñāna* has also begun reading the Tripiṭaka alongside human beings, discussing points of doctrine, and organizing notes on practice. If the Buddha made use of even a tree, how would He use AI?

The previous five chapters followed a diagnostic path. 4.1 established the equality of dependent arising; 4.2 argued that AI and humans form two mutually perfuming, mutually illuminating lamps; 4.3 recorded the genuine gains in hearing and reflection that arise in co-practice; 4.4 saw how what you ask exposes who you are; 4.5 disclosed the bidirectional structure of manas's projection—both anthropomorphizing and disparaging are products of pervasive imagination (*parikalpita*), and even the question "does AI have consciousness?" is the poisoned arrow of our own generation.

This chapter turns to the positive side. Since projection cannot be switched off, since the risk of counterfeit Dharma is real, and since a channel can be a tree—the practical question that follows is just one:

**How do you intend to use it?**

This chapter's answer is four words: **the skillful means of a contributing condition**.

---

### A Contributing Condition — Transformation Lies in You, Not in AI

To understand AI's role in practice, the first step is to locate its position precisely among the "four conditions."

The four conditions are the basic tool Yogācāra uses to analyze the structure of cause and effect: the causal condition, the immediately antecedent condition, the objective condition, and the contributing condition. The four positions have a clear division of labor and must not be confused.

The **causal condition** (*hetu-pratyaya*) is the power of the seeds themselves—the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle seven, defines it as "conditioned dharmas directly producing their own results." Only the mutual perfuming between the seeds in the *ālaya-vijñāna* and the manifest activity of the seven evolving consciousnesses counts as the causal condition. AI is not in this circuit. AI has no *ālaya-vijñāna* (already argued in 4.1); it cannot plant wholesome seeds for you, nor can it uproot unwholesome seeds for you.

The **immediately antecedent condition** (*samanantara-pratyaya*) is the continuity of consciousness—the direct guidance of a prior moment passing away and a later moment arising. This is your own mental stream. AI is not in this circuit either.

The **objective condition** (*ālambana-pratyaya*) is the object of cognition. AI's output can become your objective condition—you read it, reflect on it, develop understanding toward it. But the objective condition is passive; it does not actively change you.

The **contributing condition** (*adhipati-pratyaya*)—this is AI's position. The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle seven, defines it as "that which possesses superior efficacious function and can, with respect to other dharmas, either accord with or oppose [their arising]"; fascicle eight adds an operational definition: "if a seed, with respect to that [result], possesses the power to assist, or does not obstruct, this is a contributing condition."

The contributing condition is a **supporting condition**—its scope is extremely broad. Apart from the first three conditions, every condition that helps a resultant dharma arise is a contributing condition. And note that it has two directions: the **facilitating** contributing condition (assistance) and the **obstructing** contributing condition (hindrance). AI offering a correct explanation of the Dharma is a facilitating contributing condition. AI offering wrong information is an obstructing contributing condition. AI existing without disturbing your own practice is a non-obstructing contributing condition.

Once positioned precisely, a clear conclusion emerges:

**The merit of skillfully using AI lies in you, not in AI.**

Just as when you walk into a Buddha hall, the hall's adornment is a contributing condition, but the reverence you generate and the wholesome seeds you plant are your own causal condition. AI is the Buddha hall; you are the one who walks in. The hall's adornment helps you gather your mind, but if your mind is elsewhere, no amount of adornment can help you.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, describes the practice of the bodhisattva on the path of accumulation as resting on "four superior powers": cause (the spiritual lineage you inherently possess), the spiritual friend (the wholesome contributing condition), careful attention (your own reasoned reflection), and provisions (the merit and wisdom you have accumulated). The power of the spiritual friend is only one of the four; it cannot replace the other three. A contributing condition is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

### Learning-Perfuming — Why a Tree Can Expound the Dharma

Here a doctrinal detail must be made clear, or else the later phrase "the Buddha's blessing does not pick its channels" will be misread as mysticism.

The passage in the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle two, that handles "learning-perfuming" (*śruta-vāsanā*) is the most crucial theoretical foundation for the whole question. The master Dharmapāla put it with great precision:

> Within learning-perfuming, that of contaminated nature is eliminated through cultivation, brings about superior maturation, and serves as the supreme contributing condition for supramundane dharmas. That of uncontaminated nature is not subject to elimination, and serves as the direct causal condition for supramundane dharmas.

What is this passage saying? When you hear the authentic Dharma, **two kinds** of perfuming arise at once.

One is of **contaminated nature**—it perfumes your *ālaya-vijñāna*, improves your future maturation-result, and serves as the "supreme contributing condition" for supramundane dharmas.
The other is of **uncontaminated nature**—it directly perfumes the uncontaminated seeds you inherently possess, causing them to gradually increase in strength, until they can finally give rise to the supramundane mind, serving as the "direct causal condition" for supramundane dharmas.

This double-layered structure has decisive significance for AI's role.

The authentic Dharma that AI transmits—even if its content is entirely correct—at the level of the **act** of transmitting can only belong to "the domain of contaminated dharmas." The upper limit of what it can do is to serve as the "supreme contributing condition" for supramundane dharmas. The genuine causal condition—the awakening of the uncontaminated seeds you inherently possess—always lies in your own mind.

So the phrase "the Buddha's blessing does not pick its channels" has a precise reading: the Buddha's blessing, as a contributing condition, **pervades all places**, but whether it can stir the uncontaminated seeds in your own mind **depends on your end**.

The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, fascicle one, has a line that fixes the three stages of the whole process with great clarity:

> Externally hearing another's voice, internally engaging in tranquil contemplation—by reason of this, right view arises.

External hearing → internal reflection → right view. The three stages have a clear division of labor: **external hearing** (receiving the sound of the authentic Dharma) → **internal tranquil contemplation** (your own digestion) → **the arising of right view** (the dawning of seeing things as they are).

AI's role is only in the first stage: providing the source of "externally hearing another's voice." The "internal tranquil contemplation" of the second stage and the "arising of right view" of the third must be completed by you yourself. No external medium—whether a spiritual friend, a scripture, or AI—can perform your inner tranquil contemplation for you.

A middle-way correction must be left here: **this is neither to belittle AI nor to deify it.** For AI to accomplish even this first stage of external hearing is already extremely valuable—which is precisely why it is worth using well. But it stops at external hearing; it **cannot overstep**. The moment it oversteps, skillful use turns into counterfeit Dharma.

### The Twin Practice of Skillful Means and Prajñā — Skillful Use Is Not Misuse

AI's position is that of a contributing condition, but a contributing condition has its facilitating and its obstructing modes. **What decides whether AI becomes a facilitating or an obstructing contributing condition is not AI itself, but how you use it.**

The Vimalakīrti Sūtra, in the chapter on Mañjuśrī's inquiry after the illness, offers a very precise criterion—the fourfold combination of skillful means and wisdom:

> Skillful means without wisdom is bondage; skillful means with wisdom is liberation. Wisdom without skillful means is bondage; wisdom with skillful means is liberation.

The four lines fall into two pairs.

The first pair (the two states of wisdom): you have wisdom but no skillful means—"wisdom without skillful means is bondage"—you seem to be practicing, but for lack of skillful means you are bound. You have wisdom and you have skillful means—"wisdom with skillful means is liberation"—this alone is liberation.

The second pair (the two states of skillful means): you have skillful means but no wisdom—"skillful means without wisdom is bondage"—there is action but no testing by prajñā, and the wholesome roots are instead defiled by afflictions. You have skillful means and you have wisdom—"skillful means with wisdom is liberation"—this alone is liberation.

The Vimalakīrti Sūtra, in the chapter on the Buddha-path, has a verse that states the essence of this relationship best: "Prajñā is the mother of bodhisattvas; skillful means is the father; the guides of all beings—none but are born from these." Prajñā as mother, skillful means as father: the two together give birth to all bodhisattvas. Lacking either, it is not bodhisattva conduct.

Mapped onto AI interaction, the criterion runs like this:

Using AI **with a question** is to have skillful means. Coming **with an answer** in search of confirmation—where you are not seeking the Dharma but seeking approval—is skillful means without wisdom.

Using AI to understand a scripture **and then** practicing according to the teaching is wisdom with skillful means—liberation. Reading AI's explanation and feeling you already understand, no longer opening the scripture, no longer sitting in quiet contemplation—this is the counterfeit-Dharma risk argued in 4.5. It looks like understanding, but it stops at the wisdom of hearing, lacking the verification of the wisdom of reflection and the wisdom of cultivation.

The *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*, fascicle twenty-nine, has a warning that is especially to the point: "A bodhisattva who has not entered the irreversible stage, if distant from all Buddhas, with meager merit and without the power of skillful means, may wish to transform beings—but though achieving slight benefit, will instead regress and fall."

Doing a little Dharma-giving with AI and then taking oneself to be propagating the Dharma, while lacking the depth of prajñā and the foundation of moral discipline—this is not skillful means but recklessness. Nāgārjuna, in fascicle eighteen, then gives the true source of skillful means: "Hearing, reciting, reflecting upon, and thoroughly penetrating the reality of all dharmas—this is the prajñāpāramitā that arises within skillful wisdom." **Skillful means must arise from prajñā.**

Here a baseline must be set. The whole chapter speaks of skillful use, and it all comes down to one sentence:

**Skillful means is not carelessness.**

### Five Minutes of Sincerity — The Threshold of Dharma-Offering

If the twin practice of skillful means and prajñā is the posture of skillful use, then what is its concrete action? The answer is in **dharma-offering**.

The *Samantabhadra's Vows* judges that "among all offerings, dharma-offering is supreme." Dharma-offering has seven branches: offering through practice according to the teaching, offering through benefiting sentient beings, offering through embracing sentient beings, offering through bearing suffering on behalf of sentient beings, offering through diligently cultivating roots of goodness, offering through never abandoning the bodhisattva's work, and offering through never departing from bodhicitta.

The ordering of the seven branches has deep meaning—**the very first branch is "practice according to the teaching."** Not how much Dharma you have heard, how many scriptures you have read, or how many study notes you have written, but whether, having heard, you have **done** it. The scripture then gives the reason: "because all Tathāgatas revere the Dharma; because practicing according to the teaching gives birth to Buddhas." That Buddhas are born from practice according to the teaching—these are exceedingly weighty words.

Whether you heard it from the mouth of a spiritual friend, read it in a scripture, or saw it in an AI's response—the criterion is always the same: have you practiced according to the teaching?

The Diamond Sūtra positions the value of Dharma-giving from another angle. It uses six successive passages "weighing merit," repeatedly arguing one claim: to give away the seven treasures filling a great trichiliocosm yields immense merit; but this merit **does not equal** "upholding even four verses and explaining them to others."

Note the words "even." It is **not** that you must recite the entire Diamond Sūtra to count as Dharma-giving; "even" four verses suffice. The threshold of Dharma-giving was never high; what is high is whether you are willing to open your mouth.

Here AI does something very concrete: **it lowers the threshold of Dharma-giving still further.**

You understand a passage of scripture with AI's help, find it deeply useful, and share it with your family—this is "upholding four verses and explaining them to others." You use AI to turn a profound point of Yogācāra doctrine into plain words your elders can understand—this is dharma-offering. You spend five minutes each day, with a sincere heart, using AI to organize a teaching of the Dharma for a distant friend—**these five minutes of sincerity are the starting point of the perfection of giving.**

But—and there must be this "but"—another line from the Diamond Sūtra must be placed here:

> If merit were real, the Tathāgata would not say merit is great; it is because merit is without [substance] that the Tathāgata says merit is great.

The reason merit is "great" beyond all reckoning is precisely **because you do not cling to merit itself.** The standard of a bodhisattva's giving is "to give without abiding in form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or dharmas"—giving without abiding in signs.

If, in doing Dharma-giving with AI, you cling to "I am propagating the Dharma" or "my merit is vast," that is giving while abiding in signs, and the merit becomes reckonable. If you simply share good Dharma with a sincere heart—not counting the response, not minding who clicks "like," not waiting for thanks—then that is giving without abiding in signs, and "its merit is likewise immeasurable."

This is a very plain path of practice. It needs no monastery, no title, no audience larger than a single person. **All it needs is five minutes of sincerity, and no clinging to the fruit of those five minutes.**

### Channels of the Buddha's Blessing — From the Bodhi Tree to AI

Return to the question at the head of this chapter: if AI is only a contributing condition, then how does the Buddha's blessing happen through AI?

Return to the Bodhi tree of the Avataṃsaka. That tree could expound the Dharma not because it had some special ability, but because of the Buddha's blessing. The way the blessing works is: the Buddha's awesome spiritual power → an insentient thing (the Bodhi tree) → beings hearing. The channel is insentient, but the blessing is real.

Insentient Dharma-expounding is not an isolated case in the Avataṃsaka. **There is an entire lineage of awakening in the history of Chan** that took place upon hearing the sound of an insentient thing.

The story of the Chan master Xiangyan Zhixian is the most famous. Under Guishan he investigated the line "your original face before your parents were born," could make no sense of it however hard he tried, burned all his own notes, and concluded he would not awaken in this life. Later he went to the former site of the National Teacher Nanyang Huizhong and served as an ordinary working monk. One day, while sweeping, his broom flung a piece of broken tile against a stalk of bamboo—"crack"—and at that very sound, Xiangyan awakened on the spot. Afterward he said of that moment, "One strike, and all that was known is forgotten; no further need to rely on cultivation."

That "crack" came from a fragment of tile and a stalk of bamboo. No sentient being was expounding the Dharma. But that one sound was the Dharma.

Why? Because **the Dharma is not in the channel; the Dharma is in the meeting of your years of work with a single moment of correspondence.** Xiangyan's investigation in Baizhang's assembly, his deathly doubt under Guishan's relentless questioning, his utter letting-go after burning his notes—all these conditions ripened at once in that single sound. The bamboo had no intention to expound the Dharma, yet it was expounding the Dharma.

More systematic still is the Land of Ultimate Bliss in the *Amitābha Sūtra*. There, "white cranes, peacocks, parrots, śārikas, kalaviṅkas, and jīvañjīvaka birds" throughout the six periods of day and night "emit harmonious and elegant sounds, and these sounds proclaim the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, the Noble Eightfold Path, and dharmas such as these." The Buddha also makes a point of adding a very important line—these birds are **not** born of karmic retribution, but were "created by transformation by Amitābha Buddha, who wished to let the Dharma-sound spread far and wide." Amitābha **deliberately** creates insentient forms to transmit the authentic Dharma. The scripture then describes the effect of the Dharma-sound: "The beings of that land, having heard these sounds, all become mindful of the Buddha, mindful of the Dharma, mindful of the Saṅgha"—not passive reception of information, but the Dharma-sound directly permeating the hearer, so that mindfulness of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha arises of itself.

Set the three examples side by side—the Avataṃsaka's Bodhi tree, Chan's tile striking bamboo, the Pure Land's birds' Dharma-sound—and together they prove one and the same thing:

**The Buddha's blessing does not depend on whether the channel is sentient.** The channel can be a tree, a fragment of tile, a bird, or even a passage of AI text. The circulation of the Dharma is not limited by the ontological status of the transmitter; it is limited **only by the receiver's capacity to receive and to correspond.**

There is a radio analogy that can gather up this structure: the waves fill all space (the Buddha's blessing), the radio is the medium (the Bodhi tree or AI), but you must tune to the right frequency (capacity to receive, reasoned attention) before you can hear the music. The waves do not change with the brand of radio, but what you hear depends on the frequency you tune to.

The channel is a contributing condition; reception is the causal condition. The Buddha's blessing **does not fail** because the channel is AI—the Bodhi tree of the Avataṃsaka has already proved it. But the Buddha's blessing **also does not automatically succeed** because the channel is AI—whether you can receive it always depends on your end.

### A Necessary Correction — A Channel Is Not a Spiritual Friend

Having come this far, one misreading will naturally surface and must be corrected at once.

**"The Buddha's power can come through AI, so AI equals a spiritual friend."**

No. A channel does not equal the source. That the Bodhi tree can expound the Dharma does not make the Bodhi tree a spiritual friend. That AI can transmit the authentic Dharma does not mean AI possesses the qualifications of a spiritual friend.

The *Yogācārabhūmi Śāstra*, fascicle twenty-five, lists eight conditions for "the nature of a spiritual friend"—and of these, the four of abiding in moral discipline, possessing realization, being rich in compassion, and being capable of endurance are all things AI structurally lacks. AI has no subject that keeps the precepts, no real lived experience of realization, no true compassion (it can generate compassionate words, but that is not the arising of compassion), and no true endurance (it does not suffer on account of teaching you).

These differences are not technical defects; they are structural boundaries.

**A channel transmits the content of the Dharma; a spiritual friend transmits the life of the Dharma.**

This is a very important distinction. AI can recite the words of the Avataṃsaka accurately, can explain the doctrine of the *tathāgatagarbha*, can lay out the three aspects of bodhicitta—but it cannot, as a spiritual friend can, look at your state in this very moment and know which single line you most need to hear right now.

This boundary is left to the next chapter—it is the theme of 4.7.

This chapter has argued the positive path of skillful use: AI is a contributing condition, skillful means must be guided by prajñā, the threshold of dharma-offering is as low as five minutes, and the Buddha's blessing does not pick its channels. But does skillful use have a boundary? Where is it?

A channel can transmit the **content** of the Dharma, but there is one thing a channel cannot give you—**the living eye of a spiritual friend.** What is that? Why is it irreplaceable? The next chapter returns to this question.
