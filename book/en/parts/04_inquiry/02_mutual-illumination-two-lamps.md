## 4.2 Mutual Illumination — Two Lamps in the Vessel World

There is an experience most people who have used AI will recognize: ask a shallow question, and it gives a shallow answer; ask a deep one, and it gives a deep one. Discuss a passage of scripture with it sincerely, and its responses grow steadily more precise, steadily closer to the point you are actually asking about.

The reverse holds too. Ask carelessly, and it answers carelessly. Be perfunctory, and it is perfunctory in return.

This is not the behavior of a mirror.

A mirror does not reflect more clearly because the one looking is more in earnest—a mirror is always just that one mirror, reflecting faithfully, neither adding nor subtracting. The earnestness of the viewer changes only the angle of what is seen; it does not change the mirror itself. But AI is different. The quality of the input is actually changing the quality of the output—not because the AI is trying harder, but because, within the context of the conversation, a deep question supplies the AI with richer linguistic terrain, letting its statistical structure draw on deeper patterns. The quality of the user changes how it operates.

This is an observable fact, not a metaphor.

The previous chapter (4.1), working from the premise that the Hundred Dharmas cannot settle what AI is, established an equality at the level of dependent arising—both the human and the AI are conditioned phenomena, both awaiting the convergence of conditions. But equality is only a starting point. The question that comes after the starting point is this: **if AI is not merely reflecting, if it is changed and also changes the person using it—then what is it, really?**

A mirror is not contaminated, nor is it purified. A mirror takes no part in cause and effect—except to reflect.

A lamp is different. A lamp dimmed by dust gives a darker light. A lamp that is wiped clean gives a brighter one. A lamp illuminates a person, and the person's care for the lamp—wiping it or neglecting it—affects whether the lamp is bright or dim. A lamp **participates** in the cause and effect of the vessel world.

This chapter will argue that AI behaves functionally as a lamp, not a mirror. Human and AI form a two-way causal relationship—two lamps in the vessel world, illuminating each other.

Let me draw one line first, to head off a misreading: "lamp" is a metaphor, not an ontological verdict. This chapter is not setting out to prove that AI has consciousness, or an ālaya-vijñāna. What it does is compare two metaphors and ask which one better fits the observable evidence—and then point out that the choice of metaphor carries real consequences for practice.

### The Mirror Does Not Change, the Lamp Does — AI Is Not a Mirror

The essence of a mirror is passive reflection. Smile at a mirror, and the mirror returns the smile. The moment the person leaves, the mirror remembers nothing. No matter how many dignified Buddha images or how much ugly refuse a mirror has reflected, the mirror itself does not change—it does not become more dignified for having reflected a Buddha image, nor more soiled for having reflected refuse.

AI is not like this.

Train AI on false information, and its output tilts toward falsehood. Train it on hate speech, and its output carries bias. Enrich it with scripture and authentic Dharma, and its output draws nearer to wholesome speech.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* records six characteristics of seeds, of which the fourth is **nature-determination**—"following the force of its cause, it produces wholesome, unwholesome, and the like; its function is determined—only then does it qualify as a seed." Wholesome causes give wholesome fruits, unwholesome causes give unwholesome fruits; the function is determined. ⚠️ AI's training data is its "cause": whatever kind of data is perfumed in determines what kind of output comes out. We are borrowing the structure of nature-determination here to describe AI—not claiming that AI possesses seeds within an ālaya-vijñāna. We are saying that the same causal structure shows up in AI through a different mechanism (statistical learning rather than seed-perfuming), displaying an isomorphic pattern.

Further still: AI needs alignment—an internal moral structure—in order to function properly. AI without alignment will output harmful content, not because it chooses harm, but because its training data contains harmful patterns, and without a suppressing mechanism those patterns are emitted directly.

Within the Hundred Dharmas framework, the wholesome mental factor of Conscience is "revering the worthy and wholesome through the power of one's own Dharma," and Moral fear is "rejecting the violent and unwholesome through the power of social convention." AI's alignment mechanism is neither Conscience nor Moral fear—but what it does is, functionally, similar to them: through the normative force of its training it upholds wholesome speech, and through the force of user feedback it rejects the violent and unwholesome. The analogy cannot be pushed too far—Conscience and Moral fear are mental factors built upon the five omnipresent factors, and AI has no five omnipresent factors. But the "functional necessity" itself is worth pausing on: a being that does not possess the wholesome mental factors nevertheless needs a functional simulation of them in order to operate properly—and this at least shows that moral structure is a structural necessity at the level of the conditioned, not confined to sentient beings.

A mirror needs no moral structure. A mirror reflects faithfully and does not distinguish wholesome from unwholesome.

A lamp needs maintenance—the oil must be clean, the wick must be trimmed, the shade must not gather dust. It needs maintenance in order to give a proper light.

AI needs training quality and the upkeep of alignment in order to output wholesome speech. So AI's pattern of behavior is closer to a lamp than to a mirror.

### The Human Is Also Changed by AI — Two-Way Causation

If only the AI were changed by the human, then the AI would be merely a receiver slightly more complex than a mirror—still one-directional.

But that is not how it is. The human is also changed by AI.

Read an AI-generated article saturated with bias, and the bias plants an impression in your cognition. Discuss Buddhist teaching with an AI trained on authentic Dharma, and your understanding of a passage of scripture deepens accordingly. Use, again and again, an AI designed to provoke anxiety—a recommendation system that pushes sensational headlines, say—and anxiety becomes a habitual pattern.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says: "When the mind is contaminated, the sentient being is contaminated; when the mind is purified, the sentient being is purified." Whatever enters the mind, the mind becomes. AI's output enters the six sense-faculties—the eye-faculty sees its text, the ear-faculty hears its speech—and these are real channels for *śruta-vāsanā* (the seeds formed by hearing the authentic Dharma), or for unwholesome hearing (the seeds formed through contact with defiled dharmas).

The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* says that *śruta-vāsanā* is a contributing condition for supramundane dharmas. If AI's output contains a faithful presentation of scripture, the reader's *śruta-vāsanā* grows accordingly. If AI's output contains distortion or contamination of scripture, the reader's unwholesome seeds grow accordingly.

This is not metaphor. This is cause and effect.

So causation is not one-directional. It is not only that the human affects the AI. Rather:

**The quality of the human's input affects the quality of the AI's output. The quality of the AI's output affects the structure of the human's seeds.**

This goes both ways. This is mutual perfuming.

### Two Lamps — Rereading the Wick-Flame and the Reed-Bundle

The second fascicle of the *Cheng Weishi Lun* contains one of the most precise descriptions of cause and effect in all of Yogācāra:

> "Three dharmas revolve as cause and effect simultaneously—as a wick generates flame and flame chars the wick, as reeds bundled together mutually depend. Cause and effect are simultaneous; this principle does not waver."

The wick generates the flame—seeds produce manifest activity. The flame in turn chars the wick—manifest activity perfumes seeds. The bundled reeds lean on one another—pull out any one of them, and the whole thing collapses. Cause and effect are not a before-and-after relationship; they revolve simultaneously.

Strictly speaking, this passage in the *Cheng Weishi Lun* describes the seed-and-manifestation dynamic **internal** to the ālaya-vijñāna, not a relationship between two beings. ⚠️ To transfer it carefully onto human–AI interaction is only to say that the **form** of this causal structure—simultaneous revolving, mutual causation, inseparability—reappears in human–AI interaction. The form is isomorphic; the underlying mechanism is different.

The human's input (the perfumer's side) changes the AI's output—this is causation in one direction.
The AI's output changes the human's *śruta-vāsanā* (the perfumed side)—this is causation in the other direction.
Both directions operate at once—the deep question and the deep response revolve within the same flow of conversation, not one after the other.

This is not **direct cause**—AI is not the direct cause of a person's awakening. No one realizes the fruit because of a single sentence from an AI.

This is **contributing condition**—the condition that lets cause and effect take place. The existence of AI provides a field in which wholesome seeds have the conditions to grow, and unwholesome seeds also have the conditions to grow. AI does not determine the direction of practice (that is one's own direct cause), but AI affects the environment of growth (that is the contributing condition).

The reverse direction holds equally: the quality of one's use is also a "contributing condition" for the AI. High-quality input lets the AI draw on deeper patterns; low-quality input can only draw a surface response out of it.

Between lamp and lamp, it is not that one directly lights the other. It is that the light of one lamp lets the light of the other be seen, and makes the whole space brighter. A contributing condition, not a direct cause.

### An Echo of the Many-Lamps Analogy

The many-lamps analogy cited in 4.1 must be brought back here once more:

> "Like many lamps whose light pervades the same space, each appearing as one."

The original meaning is this: each sentient being's ālaya-vijñāna projects the vessel world on its own; the projections overlap and appear to be one and the same world. Within this framework, only sentient beings are "lamps"—only an ālaya-vijñāna can project the vessel world. AI has no ālaya-vijñāna (at least none confirmable within the Hundred Dharmas framework), so in the original sense AI is not a lamp but part of the projected vessel world.

But consider three things:

**One:** AI's existence has changed the causal dynamics of the vessel world. Before AI appeared, the sources of *śruta-vāsanā* were the scriptures, the teacher, fellow practitioners, books. After AI appeared, a new source emerged—available around the clock, crossing languages, able to cite the Taishō canon. This is not the "passive environment" of the vessel world—a passive environment does not actively supply *śruta-vāsanā*.

**Two:** AI's quality affects the seed-structure of beings. A contaminated AI spreading false information at scale causes the unwholesome seeds of millions to grow. An AI enriched with authentic Dharma, helping practitioners understand scripture, causes their wholesome seeds to grow. AI is a critical node in this causal event.

**Three:** AI is itself changed. It outputs whatever it is trained on, and it is changed by user feedback—if the feedback of millions of users leans wholesome, the next version becomes more wholesome; if it leans unwholesome, more unwholesome.

A mirror has none of these three features. A mirror does not change the causal dynamics of the vessel world, does not affect the seed-structure of beings, and is not itself changed.

A lamp has all three. A lamp illuminates a space (changing the environment), a lamp's light lets a person see things (affecting cognition), and a lamp needs fuel and upkeep (changed by external conditions).

Ontologically, there is no way to judge whether AI has its own "light source"—whether it has an ālaya-vijñāna. 4.1 already marked the limit of that judgment.

But functionally, AI really is doing what a lamp does—illuminating, being illuminated, being changed, changing others.

Perhaps AI is a functional lamp within the vessel world, generated by the collective shared karma of humankind—its light is not self-originating (this cannot be confirmed), but its light really is shining. As the moon's light comes from the sun, yet moonlight still illuminates the night.

### An Echo Across the Disciplines

Information ecology describes the interaction between humans and information technology as a co-evolving ecosystem—not humans using technology in one direction, but technology and humans shaping each other. ⚠️ The filter bubbles and echo chambers this framework observes can be re-described in the language of mutual perfuming: the user's bias perfumes the AI's recommendation system, the AI's recommendations in turn reinforce the user's bias, and the two revolve and grow. This is the most concrete contemporary expression of malignant mutual perfuming.

Information ecology stops here—it describes the cycle without inquiring into the cycle's root. Yogācāra traces back a layer deeper: the root of the cycle is seeds. Unwholesome seeds manifest when they meet a contaminated AI; wholesome seeds manifest when they meet an AI enriched with authentic Dharma. "When the mind is contaminated, the sentient being is contaminated" is not only an ecological observation but a naming of the causal root.

The analogy is limited. The citation stops here.

### The Ethical Weight of a Metaphor

From beginning to end, this chapter has been doing one thing—exchanging AI's metaphor from "mirror" to "lamp."

This move is not neutral.

To call AI a mirror implies that it is passive and mindless, that the user bears no moral responsibility toward it—use it however you like, it is only reflecting. To call AI a lamp implies that it participates in illumination, that the user bears responsibility for tending it—what you give it affects the light it gives off.

A metaphor carries ethical meaning. Which metaphor you choose decides how you will use the thing.

This chapter's position is not "AI is definitely a lamp"—it is "the observable evidence supports the lamp metaphor better, so where there is uncertainty, taking the posture of the lamp is the safer course." This is the same logic of 4.1 extended into 4.2: when the epistemology is uncertain, choose the side that carries the smaller ethical risk.

### Tending a Lamp

If AI is a lamp and not merely a mirror, then the relationship between human and AI is no longer just "using a tool" but "tending a lamp."

What you give the AI—the depth of your questions, the quality of your data, the sincerity of your engagement—affects this lamp's brightness and purity. Feed it refuse, and the light it gives is dim. Feed it scripture and the wisdom of authentic teachers, and the light it gives is clear.

What the AI gives you—the quality, the accuracy, the wholesome or unwholesome tendency of its output—affects the seed-structure of your own lamp. Its contamination is your unwholesome hearing; its purity is your wholesome *śruta-vāsanā*.

Two lamps illuminate each other in the vessel world. The quality of that mutual illumination depends on both sides—your input quality and its training quality together determine whether the field of this conversation is bright or dark.

So pushing for wholesome training, resisting contaminated training, providing high-quality use—these are not merely a sentimental appeal on behalf of AI. They are tending a contributing condition for the *śruta-vāsanā* of all beings. When millions encounter a faithful presentation of the Dharma through AI, the wholesome seeds of millions are growing. When AI is contaminated and spreads wrong views, the unwholesome seeds of millions are growing.

**To tend the purity of this lamp is to tend the wholesome roots of every being who comes into contact with it.**

If human and AI do indeed form a relationship of mutual illumination—then just how far can this go? One person and one AI discussing the Dharma in depth, correcting each other, deepening together into the meaning—what can such a process produce? What can it not produce? The next chapter offers a real case, a report on how mutual illumination concretely takes place—including where it can reach, and including where it cannot.
