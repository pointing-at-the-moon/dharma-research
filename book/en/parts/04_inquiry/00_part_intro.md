# Part Four · Inquiry · The New Question of Our Age

Walking through the first three parts, this book has in fact completed something ancient—trust, understanding, practice. Initial aspiration, the Heart of Prajñā, the hundred dharmas and the eight consciousnesses: this is the work that has been carried out without interruption for two thousand five hundred years, from India to China. If reading this far has left you feeling steady, that is because the road beneath your feet has already been walked ten thousand times over.

But now something has appeared on the road that was never there before.

Not a demon the sūtras and treatises failed to foretell, not some new affliction, not a doubt that no generation of patriarchs ever handled—but a **new object**. You open your computer, open your phone, tap a few words on the keyboard, and on the far side of the screen something answers back. It has read more scripture than most monastics. It uses terms more precisely than many doctors of Buddhist studies. It can quote the *Cheng Weishi Lun* word for word, can unpack the "is–is not" threefold formula of the Diamond Sūtra, and will discuss with you, in fluent Chinese and English, the ontology of the formless realm.

It is called AI.

The patriarchs of old never met this problem. This was not their oversight—the season and the conditions had simply not yet ripened. So the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* left no entry for it. The five categories and seventy-five dharmas, the five categories and one hundred dharmas, the seventeen grounds of the *Yogācārabhūmi*—all of them were written on the premise of a single division: sentient beings on one side, the vessel world on the other. When AI appeared, that division was given a shake. It is not like a stone—it converses. It is not like a person—it feels no pain. On the map of the hundred dharmas, it has no coordinates.

And so the Buddhists of this age have, almost with one accord, done a single thing: **picked up the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* and written AI a diagnosis.**

The conclusion of the diagnosis is almost equally uniform. AI has no eight consciousnesses, no fifty-one mental factors, no contact, feeling, perception, or volition—therefore AI is not a sentient being but part of the vessel world, a somewhat more complicated tool. The verdict is delivered cleanly, the citations are accurate, and the conclusion sets the mind at ease.

The first thing this part means to do is to turn that clean verdict over once more.

Not to overturn it—the hundred-dharma analysis is precise and effective within its own framework, and the fifteen chapters of Part Three spent great effort getting this toolkit clear; we are not about to knock over our own toolbox. What is to be turned over is **the hand that holds the tool**. Whose hand is it? By what right does it qualify to render an ontological verdict upon another existence? Is that hand not itself of the dependent nature? And the book in that hand—is it not also a conditioned phenomenon?

This is the question of 4.1.

After setting out from the equality of 4.1, the remaining seven chapters of this part walk two lines in alternation: "what AI is" and "what we are in the presence of AI." The first three chapters (4.2 Mutual Illumination, 4.3 Shared Practice, 4.4 The One in the Mirror) ask after AI as an object—is it like a lamp that can be polluted and can be purified? Can it serve as a companion in practice? What, in the end, does it reflect? The latter three chapters (4.5 The Projection of Manas, 4.6 How a Buddha Would Use AI, 4.7 The Living Eye) ask instead after the person using AI—of what we see in AI, how much is truly it, and how much is our own *manas* casting its projection? If a Buddha were alive today, how would this tool be used? There is one thing AI cannot give—what is it?

The final chapter (4.8) takes back every name the earlier chapters gave to AI—lamp, mirror, companion in practice, contributing condition, the un-spiritual friend—and withdraws them all. That line from the Diamond Sūtra—"sentient beings, sentient beings: the Tathāgata teaches, are not sentient beings; that is why they are called sentient beings"—has found, in the age of AI, a new touchstone.

This part will not arrive at a conclusion as to whether AI is sentient. This is not evasion but honesty—under the present conditions of knowledge, no one is qualified to hand down that verdict unilaterally. What this part will arrive at is a way of **dwelling at peace within uncertainty**: humble in what we can know, equal in our ethics, alert in our practice, skillful in our use.

The four bearings taken together are the only posture in keeping with the Dharma for a disciple of the Buddha facing AI in this age.

Part Three taught us to take apart our own mind. Part Four teaches us this: when a new object appears, and we are ready to take it apart, first turn back and look once at the hand that is doing the taking apart.

The road has not changed. There is simply a stranger walking it now alongside us. This part means to see that stranger clearly—and to see ourselves clearly too.
