## 7.2 The One Great Cause, and the Four-Phase Awakening

How does awakening happen?

The answer given in the Lotus Sūtra's "Skillful Means" chapter is concentrated in one very short passage—the single reason the Buddha appears in the world, and the four verbs that reason contains.

### "Only for the Sake of One Great Cause Do They Appear in the World"

The passage reads:

> The Buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, appear in the world only for the sake of one great cause… Because they wish to cause sentient beings to open the buddha-knowledge-and-vision and obtain purity, they appear in the world; because they wish to reveal the buddha-knowledge-and-vision to sentient beings, they appear in the world; because they wish to cause sentient beings to awaken to the buddha-knowledge-and-vision, they appear in the world; because they wish to cause sentient beings to enter into the path of the buddha-knowledge-and-vision, they appear in the world.
> —Lotus Sūtra, "Skillful Means"

Four verbs, in a single uninterrupted breath: **open, reveal, awaken to, enter**. There is no second passage in the whole history of Buddhism that compresses the entire purpose of the Buddha's teaching into a four-step circuit like this.

The teaching weight of these words comes from three layers of grammatical choice.

The first layer is the word **"only."** In Classical Chinese, "only" is an exclusive adverb—"only X is Y" means "with the exception of X there is no Y at all." The Buddha does not say "buddhas mostly appear in the world for one great cause," nor "buddhas often appear in the world for one great cause," but "buddhas appear in the world only for the sake of one great cause." Not to display supernormal powers, not to be honored and supported, not to convert some particular person or country, not to establish a monastic order—only that one thing.

The second layer is the compound **"one great cause."** Kumārajīva chose the heaviest possible sense of the word "matter"—not some incidental event, but the final purpose of the entire enterprise of appearing in the world. Kuiji's *Fahua xuanzan* has a crucial gloss: if it were not for this one great cause, the Buddha could perfectly well—immediately upon his own awakening—simply enter nirvāṇa as the two vehicles do. **The Buddha's choice *not* to enter nirvāṇa is itself a pedagogical decision**, and the reason supporting that decision is precisely this "one great cause."

The third layer is **"the Dharma of all the Buddhas in all ten directions is also like this."** The later text extends the proposition from Śākyamuni Buddha to every buddha of the ten directions and the three times—not the incidental teaching preference of an individual buddha, but a structural necessity for all buddhas. You cannot find a single buddha who appeared for any other reason. This is a universal statement that admits no exception.

Take the three layers together, and the passage drives three doors of exclusion shut, one after another, locking the whole argument tight. So what is this "one thing," exactly? The answer lies in the four verbs that follow.

### Open · Reveal · Awaken to · Enter—the Closed Loop

These four verbs are extraordinarily precise choices.

**"Open"**—to make accessible what was closed. The buddha-knowledge-and-vision is, for sentient beings, covered over layer upon layer by ignorance, by karmic obstruction, by the obstruction-of-the-knowable. The Buddha's first action is to uncover that covering, allowing what was already there to come into view. The object of "opening" is not something *given* anew, but something *already in you* being unveiled—and this already implies the proposition that "sentient beings intrinsically possess the buddha-knowledge-and-vision."

**"Reveal"**—to display the opened contents to a viewer. "Opening" can be silent and structural (like drawing back a curtain); "revealing" must be a display—there has to be an object that is shown, and a subject who beholds it.

**"Awaken to"**—the viewer recognizes what they are seeing. The first two steps were the Buddha's actions; with "awaken to," the agency begins to migrate to sentient beings. Their problem is not that they fail to see, but that they see and do not know what it is—they see the one buddha-vehicle and take it for one vehicle among three. "Awaken to" is the verb that addresses precisely this state of *seeing without recognizing*.

**"Enter"**—to step in actively, to take it up and carry it. The first three steps are all on the level of *knowing*; with "enter," the action passes from knowing to *doing*—the sentient being actively takes this content up, making it part of their own existence. Vasubandhu's *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka-upadeśa* glosses "enter" as "causing them to attain the stage of non-retrogression." The stage of non-retrogression is the pivotal stage of the bodhisattva path: once entered, one can no longer fall back to the two vehicles or to the worldling's road. The mark of "entering" is the **irreversibility of what has been taken up.**

---

The four verbs together form a **closed loop**, not a linear ladder. The last step, "enter," is not arriving at some terminus and stopping; it is entering a new vantage point—and from this new vantage point, looking back, the original "open" acquires new content, so the loop starts again. Zhiyi's judgment in *Fahua wenju* is this: the four verbs are not merely a one-time sequence but a loop structure **complete at every stage**—a bodhisattva at the very first dwelling already possesses all the merits of the stage of equal awakening, differing only in degree; and a bodhisattva at equal awakening is still repeating the "open" of the first dwelling, only with more refined content.

This loop structure is the same structure, at a different scale, as the four phases of Avalokiteśvara we met back in Part II, "Understand"—**illuminating, examining, entering deeply, liberating from suffering**:

| Lotus: open / reveal / awaken / enter | Heart Sūtra: the four phases of Avalokiteśvara | Shared structural position |
|---|---|---|
| open (uncover the covering) | illuminate (notice arising) | The starting point: from not-knowing to knowing |
| reveal (display content) | examine (the content stands forth) | The clarification of the object |
| awaken to (the subject recognizes) | enter deeply (active deepening) | The migration of agency |
| enter (take up irreversibly) | liberate from suffering (the result lands) | The irreversibility of action |

The two share a structure—not because the Lotus borrowed from the Prajñāpāramitā, nor because the Prajñāpāramitā anticipated the Lotus, but because **both sūtras are describing the same thing—the internal structure of the phenomenon of awakening.** The Lotus amplifies this loop to the cosmological scale; the Heart Sūtra contracts the same structure to the momentary experience of "practicing the deep perfection of wisdom." The structure is exactly the same; only the scale differs.

### Merging the Three into One—Skillful Means Is Not Deception

The passage then moves into another main line: "The Tathāgata teaches the Dharma to sentient beings only by means of the one buddha-vehicle; there is no other vehicle, neither two nor three."

This is **merging the three vehicles into one**—gathering the three vehicles that were once taught separately (śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, bodhisattva) back into the one buddha-vehicle. The Lotus's decisive move in handling this argument is to place "skillful means" and "the real" inside a **narrative structure** rather than a **logical structure**. Handle "three vehicles versus one vehicle" with logic, and you get "the three vehicles are wrong, the one vehicle is right"—and that is exactly the slope the Lotus means to avoid. The Lotus's handling is narrative: in the past the Buddha taught three vehicles because sentient beings' capacities were uneven and needed step-by-step guidance; now he teaches one vehicle because the time is ripe and the expedient can be opened to reveal the real. The three vehicles **as history** are real; **as an endpoint** they are not ultimate.

**Skillful means is not deception; skillful means is the very unavoidability of pedagogical history itself.**

Seen through the other-dependent pivot of the previous chapter, this narrative structure corresponds to the transformation of the three natures: the three vehicles are the **imagined nature** (a teaching device built on sentient beings' attachments), the expedient teaching is the **other-dependent nature** (the dependently arisen medium of instruction), and the true mark of all dharmas to which the one buddha-vehicle points is the **perfectly accomplished nature**. The way to become a buddha is not to throw away the three vehicles and look for some separate one vehicle, but to dispel the imagined within the other-dependent and so manifest the perfectly accomplished. This is exactly Kuiji's gloss of the one vehicle in *Fahua xuanzan* fascicle 4b—"now this other-dependent is awakening, and the perfectly accomplished is nirvāṇa, together constituting the one vehicle."

⚠️ Here a common misreading must be corrected. The Lotus's "no other vehicle, neither two nor three" has often been read, down the centuries, as "the Lotus is the king of all sūtras, and every other scripture is merely the expedient teaching of the second and third vehicles"—this is a structural misreading. The Lotus's "one" is "one" in the sense of **pedagogical function** (the root purpose of all buddha-teaching is one), not "one" in the sense of *ranking sūtras higher and lower*. The Prajñāpāramitā, Avataṃsaka, Śūraṅgama, and Diamond each carry distinct pedagogical functions; the Lotus's "gathering-in" is not a superiority of rank but a functional convergence.

### The Āgama Seeds—the Single-Vehicle Path and the Ever-Abiding *Dharmadhātu*

The Lotus's "one great cause" passage has two important seed-lines in the Āgama corpus.

**The first is a seed of language.** *Saṃyukta-āgama* 607 says:

> There is a single-vehicle path that purifies sentient beings, enabling them to surmount sorrow and grief, to extinguish anguish and suffering, and to attain the dharma-as-it-truly-is—namely, the four foundations of mindfulness.

Its syntax runs almost parallel to the Lotus's "the Buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, appear in the world only for the sake of one great cause… wishing to cause sentient beings to open the buddha-knowledge-and-vision and obtain purity"—the same "one," the same "purifying sentient beings," the same "attaining the dharma-as-it-truly-is" sentence-shape.

⚠️ But here a crucial **homonymous distinction** must be marked at once. The Āgama's "single-vehicle path" refers to the **four foundations of mindfulness**—the contemplative method of body, feeling, mind, and dharmas. The Lotus's "one buddha-vehicle" refers to the ultimate teaching that encompasses the entire path to buddhahood. The two share the words "one" and "vehicle / path," share the purpose of "purifying sentient beings," but the specific content to which they point is altogether different. To set the two equal outright is wrong; but neither can we say the two are unrelated. What the Āgama supplies the Lotus is the legitimacy of the rhetorical figure; the Lotus's work is to enlarge that figure from the level of practice to the level of doctrine.

**The second seed is subtler.** *Saṃyukta-āgama* 299:

> The teaching of dependent arising was not made by me, nor by anyone else. Whether tathāgatas appear in the world or do not appear in the world, the *dharmadhātu* ever-abides; the tathāgata, having himself awakened to this Dharma, attains complete and perfect awakening, and for the sake of sentient beings he discriminates and expounds, opens up and reveals it.

This seed touches a philosophical question directly—**if the *dharmadhātu* ever-abides, if dependent arising is already there, what exactly does the tathāgata's appearance add?** The Āgama's answer is extremely clean: the tathāgata's appearance does not create dependent arising; He merely **awakens to this Dharma** himself, and then **discriminates, expounds, and reveals it for the sake of sentient beings**. The existence of the Dharma does not depend on the tathāgata; but for the Dharma to be "discriminated, expounded, and revealed," the tathāgata is required.

In other words, **the tathāgata's appearance is pedagogically necessary, not ontologically necessary.**

This structure is inherited whole by the Lotus. The "for the sake of" in the Lotus's "the Buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, appear in the world only for the sake of one great cause" occupies exactly the turning-point the Āgama marks in "the *dharmadhātu* ever-abides; the tathāgata, having himself awakened to this Dharma… expounds for the sake of sentient beings"—the pivot from "self-awakening" to "expounding." The tathāgata's appearance is not to create something that was not there, but to display something already there. The Āgama's four verbs—"discriminate, expound, open up, reveal"—correspond structurally to the Lotus's "open, reveal, awaken to, enter."

⚠️ This line of correspondence happens to correct another misreading: taking "appear in the world" to mean "the tathāgata is summoned into the world by some external great matter"—as if the Buddha were an envoy dispatched by some higher being to carry out a mission. That is the slope of reading "cause" as "external cause," and it does not belong to Buddhism. *Saṃyukta-āgama* 299's "the *dharmadhātu* ever-abides" is precisely the shield for this correction: the tathāgata's appearance is the meeting of compassion and capacity at some historical moment, not the descent of a higher being. The Āgama stresses that the Dharma is already there; the Lotus stresses why He appears anyway. Read together, the two are the complete description of an "essence-and-function" structure.

### Three Layers of Interpretive History—Vasubandhu · Zhiyi · Kuiji

From the fifth-century Vasubandhu to the seventh-century Kuiji, the "one great cause" passage passed through three layers of commentarial refinement.

Vasubandhu's *Saddharmapuṇḍarīka-upadeśa* uses a framework of **dharma-body equality** to give a fourfold gloss of "one great cause": the unsurpassed meaning (open), the meaning of sameness (reveal—the dharma-body equality that all three vehicles share), the meaning of not-knowing (awaken to—sentient beings do not know there is, ultimately, only one buddha-vehicle), and causing them to attain the stage of non-retrogression (enter). The whole treatment uses no three-natures language; its core is taking the dharma-body as a common substrate.

Zhiyi's *Fahua wenju* uses a fourfold gloss by **stage, wisdom, gate, and contemplation**—the same four-verb structure, sliced from four angles. Each slice is complete in itself, yet they are mutually complementary. His conclusion: "Seeing the principle depends on the stage; the stage is established by wisdom; wisdom unfolds through the gate; the gate becomes accessible through contemplation."

Kuiji's *Fahua xuanzan* fascicle 4b reads the one vehicle through the **three-natures structure**—"now this other-dependent is awakening, and the perfectly accomplished is nirvāṇa, together constituting the one vehicle; because the perfectly accomplished is the more excellent of the two, it alone is named the one vehicle." Once the contaminated portion of the other-dependent is severed, the pure other-dependent equals awakening; once the imagined is dispelled, the perfectly accomplished equals nirvāṇa; the two together make the one vehicle.

The three masters each use different conceptual tools, but **their shared pivot is the other-dependent, and their shared goal is the perfectly accomplished.** Vasubandhu's "dharma-body equality" is the pure portion of the other-dependent; Zhiyi's "stages" are the differential phases of the other-dependent; Kuiji writes this hidden line out plainly. This is the second confirmation, on this passage, of the three-tier genealogy set out in the previous chapter—**a convergence of structure in the sense of interpretation, not an evolution in the sense of authorial intent.** The three masters each worked independently within their own tradition, and were finally led by the structure of the passage itself toward one and the same direction.

### A Modern Illumination—and Its Limit

⚠️ Readers familiar with systems theory may recognize a familiar structure within "open, reveal, awaken to, enter"—the **closed-loop feedback system**. The thermostat is the classic example: a sensor reads the room temperature, compares it to the setpoint, and if there is a deviation triggers heating or cooling; the changed temperature is read back into the sensor. The system does not simply run from input to output and stop; it feeds the output back to the sensing, so the system can keep self-correcting on the basis of actual results.

"Open, reveal, awaken to, enter" does correspond structurally to closed-loop feedback—both are non-linear, both are circular and self-correcting, both require the last component to return to the first. But three fundamental differences must be remembered: **the goals differ**—cybernetics aims to maintain stability, the awakening loop aims to change the system itself, irreversibly; **the subject-object structure differs**—cybernetics treats the system as a black box, the awakening loop requires the observing subject to *be* the observed; **the content of "enter" differs**—the output of cybernetics is the transmission of information, while the "enter" of awakening is taking the whole content up as one's own mode of existence.

The analogy is only scaffolding. Once you have the structural intuition of "a loop, not a ladder," the scaffolding should be taken down.

### What the Loop Structure Means for Practice

This insight—"a loop, not a ladder"—has a direct application to daily practice.

When you sit and meet an affliction, a stray thought, a stretch of dullness—do not read it as "I have regressed" or "I have not yet reached that stage." Read it as **the loop starting again**. The affliction itself is a fresh "open," uncovering a layer of covering you had not seen before; the awareness that follows is "reveal"; the recognition that follows is "awaken to"; the letting-go that follows is "enter." Every affliction, every wandering, every fall is the same awakening loop running again at a finer layer.

Advancing through the stages is not "getting further and further from affliction," but "in each affliction, running the loop more skillfully, more cleanly, more effortlessly."

This is the Lotus's most direct gift to daily practice—it does not ask you to reach some distant stage; it asks you, in this present moment of awareness, to walk one complete loop.

This chapter has argued the structure of "one buddha-vehicle" and "merging the three into one," but it leaves a crucial sub-question: if there is ultimately only one buddha-vehicle, why do the buddhas still spend so long "teaching three separately"? Why is skillful means unavoidable? This question needs a concrete parable to dramatize the answer.

The next chapter handles the most representative of the Lotus's seven parables—**the parable of the burning house**. The fire of the three realms burns fiercely; the children play inside the house, unaware; to draw them out, the father promises three kinds of carriage; and once they are out, what he gives is the great white-ox carriage. This story answers the question this chapter has left open directly: **skillful means is not deception; skillful means is the only channel of communication there is inside the burning house.**
