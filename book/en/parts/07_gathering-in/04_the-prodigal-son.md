## 7.4 The Parable of the Destitute Son — You Were Always the Elder's Son; Why Call Yourself a Hired Hand of Low Station?

The question left over from the previous chapter is this one — the Burning House parable carried us out of the house, but we still did not know that the great white-ox carriage waiting outside was something that was always ours to begin with. After leaving the house, what the child thinks is: "Father bestowed a carriage on me" — not "I have reclaimed what was always supposed to be mine."

If even this one thing — "this was always mine" — has not been recognized, then the work of skillful means is not yet complete.

The parable of the destitute son, in the *Faith and Understanding* chapter, is what handles this.

### This Time the Speaker Is Not the Buddha

The Burning House parable was spoken by the Buddha to Śāriputra. The destitute son is different.

After the *Parables* chapter is finished, something extraordinarily rare in the *Lotus Sūtra* happens in the text — Subhūti, Kātyāyana, Kāśyapa, and Maudgalyāyana, the four most senior disciples of the śrāvaka vehicle, stand up of their own accord. They say to the Buddha: "Today we are truly śrāvakas, causing all to hear the voice of the Buddha-path; today we are truly arhats." And then — instead of listening to the Buddha tell another parable — they themselves tell a parable.

This is the moment when **the disciples voluntarily speak the parable**. In the *Lotus Sūtra*, this structure appears only once.

This structural shift is not a narrative accident. The Burning House is spoken by the father about the children — the father knows that you do not know, the father devises a means to call you out. The destitute son is spoken by the son about the father — the son finally knows that he previously did not know, and so he uses a parable to voice this state of "having just recognized it." The first is skillful means from above; the second is faith and understanding from below. Only when the two directions are joined together do we have the complete loop.

"We understand now," the four great disciples say. "Our situation is just like that destitute son."

### Fifty Years of Wandering

The story goes like this.

> Suppose there were a man, still young, who left his father and fled, dwelling long in another land — ten years, twenty, even fifty.

A wealthy elder's son leaves home as a child and wanders for ten years, twenty years, even fifty years. Why did he leave? The text does not say. Perhaps a misunderstanding, an impulse, an inexplicable departure — the point is not the cause, the point is the fact: he left home, and he left far, and for a long time.

Over those fifty years, the son's condition is this: "growing up, becoming poor, dashing about in the four directions in search of food and clothing." The farther he wanders, the poorer he grows, and a whole life passes in odd jobs and drifting.

Meanwhile, the other thread of the text is the father, who settles down in another city and becomes a vastly wealthy elder: "gold, silver, lapis lazuli, coral, amber, crystal pearls — his storehouses all overflowing." Wealth so great the storehouses cannot hold it. But there is one thing the father cannot set aside in his heart — the son he lost. The text writes the father's thought: "If only I could find my son and entrust the wealth to him, then I would be at peace and content, with no more anxiety." If I could find my son and hand him these goods, only then could I grow old at ease.

The two threads run parallel in the narrative: **the son wanders ever farther and grows ever poorer; the father accumulates ever more and longs ever more**. The two threads converge at the very center of the story — the father's city, the father's gate.

### Turning and Running at His Own Gate

> At that time the destitute son, taking work from place to place, by chance arrived at his father's house.

Note these two characters — *by chance* (展轉). The son is not actively led to his father's house by any force; in the drift of his own odd jobs, changing from one employer to the next, by chance and without awareness, he arrives at the city where his father lives, at that very gate. To him, this is just one more possible job.

The son stands at the gate and looks in. What he sees is a scene like this:

> Seated on a lion throne, his feet on a jeweled stool, with brahmins, kṣatriyas, and householders reverently surrounding him, his body adorned with pearl necklaces worth millions.

The elder on the lion throne, flanked by the reverent throng of brahmins, kṣatriyas, and householders, the necklaces on his body worth millions, his feet on a jeweled stool, a jeweled canopy above his head.

The son's reaction is immediate — not curiosity, not envy, but fear:

> This must be a king or someone of a king's rank — this is not a place where someone like me can earn his keep. Better to head to the poor quarter, where I can put my strength to use and find food and clothing easily. If I linger here I might be coerced and forced to labor. Having thought this, he ran off swiftly.

Here let us slow down.

This passage is one of the most precise pieces of psychological description in the whole parable of the destitute son. In the instant the son sees the elder, he makes a judgment — "this is not a place I can stay." But this judgment is not as simple as "I am not worthy." Let us lay open each layer of what is in his mind:

**Layer one**: "This must be a king or someone of a king's rank" — this person is a king, or someone of a king's standing. This layer is a judgment about the other.

**Layer two**: "This is not a place where someone like me can earn his keep" — **this is not a place where a person like me can put his strength to use and get something for it**. This layer is a judgment about himself.

**Layer three**: "Better to head to the poor quarter, where I can put my strength to use and find food and clothing easily" — I should go to the poor quarter, that is where there is room for me, where food and clothing are easy to earn. This layer is an active choice of his own "correct position."

**Layer four**: "If I linger here I might be coerced and forced to labor" — if I stay here too long, I might be forced to work. This layer is the deepest — he experiences this place as a **threat**.

And then: "he ran off swiftly." He turns and runs.

A house that was always his own — he stands at the gate, takes one look, feels threatened, and turns and runs.

This running is not because he is stupid. His reasoning is entirely coherent within his own coordinate system — I am a hired laborer, this is a house of wealth and rank, I cannot stay, I will be forced to work. **The problem lies in the coordinate system itself.** He has no idea what his coordinate system is missing — that the master of this great house is his own father, that everything here was always his to inherit. But this fact is not in his coordinate system, so none of his reasoning can ever reach it.

The father, in that same instant, does something else. The text says "seeing his son, he recognized him, and his heart filled with great joy" — he recognized him at a single glance. The father immediately sends servants to bring the son back. The result: "the destitute son was terrified and cried out in distress: I have done nothing wrong, why am I being seized?" — he shouts in terror that he is innocent, why is he being captured? When the pursuit reaches its limit, the son simply "fainted and fell to the ground" — collapsed unconscious.

The father, watching all of this from the window, immediately realizes one thing: **"The father knew that his son's mind was small and weak, and that his own nobility was difficult for his son to bear."**

The father knows that fifty years of wandering have already shaped his son's inner structure into the form of "I am a base laborer." And the father's own nobility, the splendor of the father's house, are no longer an attraction to this son but a threat. To take him back by force would only break him.

So the father decides: release him. "Sprinkle cold water on his face to revive him, and do not speak to him further" — let him come to, but for now do not speak to him.

### Twenty Years of Removing Excrement

> At that time the elder, intending to draw his son in, devised skillful means.

The father's skillful means is designed in three layers.

**Layer one**: the men sent to hire the son must be "haggard in appearance and lacking in dignity." They must be poor men who look just like the son — so that the son will not once again feel the distance of "I do not belong here."

**Layer two**: double wages. "I will increase your wages" — a material attraction above the market rate.

**Layer three**: the work is removing excrement.

What is removing excrement? Carrying away dung. The lowest, dirtiest work, the work no one is willing to do. Why does the father arrange it this way? Because **this work must be one that the son's self-judgment ("I am a base laborer") can accept**. If he were sent to manage the accounts, to be a steward, to do any work that was a little more respectable, he would once again flee because "this is not a place I can stay." To make him stay, the father must give him work he himself feels worthy of.

After the excrement-removing laborer is hired, the father does something himself. The text writes:

> He removed his necklaces, his fine soft outer garments, and his ornaments, and put on coarse, worn, soiled clothing, smeared dust over his body, took up a dung-collecting tool in his right hand, and assumed the manner of one who is afraid.

The father removes his necklaces, his fine soft garments, his ornaments — removes the entire outward form of the elder — puts on filthy clothes, smears dust all over his body, takes up the dung tool, and **"assumed the manner of one who is afraid"** — puts on a posture of trembling timidity.

These few words carry special weight. The father deliberately performs a cowering posture just like his son's. Not to put on a show of humility, but to press the possible power-differential between the two of them down to the lowest possible point — so that the son will be willing to come close.

Then the father speaks to his son:

> Hey, fellow! Stay and work here, do not go elsewhere, and I will increase your wages. Whatever you need — earthen vessels, rice, flour, salt, vinegar — do not hesitate to ask. There is even an old servant here to give you what you need. Be at ease in your mind. I am like a father to you; do not worry... From now on, you are like a son born to me.

And then the father formally gives him a name — "at that time the elder gave him a new name and called him 'son'."

He gave the son a new name: "son." Within this house, from now on he is addressed as "son."

The son's reaction is:

> At that time the destitute son, though glad at this treatment, **still considered himself a hired laborer of low station**.

This is the central sentence of the whole parable.

"Still considered himself a hired laborer of low station" — he still regards himself as a hired hand of low station. Though his wages have been raised, though the father comes in person to work beside him, though he is called "son," though he is told "I am like a father to you" — **his inner judgment about himself has not moved**.

The text goes on: "On account of this, for twenty years he constantly removed excrement."

Twenty years.

Let us stop and think about these twenty years.

A person, working every day at the very center of what is truly his inheritance. Seeing his father every day (though not knowing it is his father). Called "son" every day. Drawing double wages every day. Living in his father's house every day. All these facts are right before his eyes, but the judgment in his heart never moves: **I am only a hired hand, I am only a hired laborer of low station**.

Twenty years.

These twenty years are not literary exaggeration. They are the text's truthful depiction of one thing — **a person's fundamental judgment about himself does not change directly just because the external facts change**. You can give him all the evidence — he can see it every day, hear it every day, be called it every day — and the sense of lowness in his heart is still the sense of lowness, the self-mark of "hired laborer of low station" is still that mark.

Why?

Because **his self-judgment is an inner structure that he accumulated, bit by bit, over fifty years of wandering**. Every thought of "a person like me is not worthy," every choice of "I can only do lowly work," every retreat of "I do not belong here" — these actions, repeated day after day, month after month, year after year, planted seeds in the ālaya-vijñāna that grew thicker and thicker. By the moment he stood at his father's gate, these seeds had already become an extremely solid self-structure — solid enough that the sight of his father would trigger the automatic judgment "this is not my place," solid enough that twenty years of being called "son" would still leave him "still considering himself a hired laborer of low station."

This structure cannot be directly overwritten by an external proclamation. It needs time.

### The Gradual Entrusting of the Estate

> After this, their hearts grew in mutual trust, and he came and went without difficulty — yet he still lodged in his original quarters.

After twenty years, the son's heart begins to loosen a little. He grows in "mutual trust" with the father and comes and goes through the father's house without difficulty. But the text leaves a detail — "yet he still lodged in his original quarters." The place he lives is still that same humble lodging. His psychological space has shifted; his physical dwelling has not.

Then the father falls ill.

The father knows he will not live long, summons the son, and tells him: "I now have abundant gold, silver, and jewels; my storehouses overflow. As for how much should be taken in and given out among them, you know it all. This is my mind." All the goods, you shall manage; how much there is, to whom it should be given, how it should be used — all of it is for you to decide.

This is an enormous transfer of trust. The father has handed the actual management of the entire estate to the son. By the standards of worldly law, this is already the state of "becoming the master of the house."

But the son's reaction is —

> At that time the destitute son, receiving this charge, managed all the goods, the gold and silver, the jewels and storehouses, **yet had no thought of taking even one meal's portion for himself**. And his lodging remained in its old place; **the mind of low station, too, he could not yet cast off**.

Two key sentences.

**"No thought of taking even one meal's portion for himself"** — managing the entire family's whole estate, he never once thought to keep even a single meal's worth for himself. **"The mind of low station, too, he could not yet cast off"** — the mind of low station has not yet been cast off.

His material position is now completely in place — he is, in substance, already the manager of the estate. But his inner self-judgment is still "I am someone managing things for another." He will not take anything from these goods for himself, not because he is morally noble, but because deep down he simply does not believe these things have anything to do with him.

This passage cuts deeper than the previous twenty years. The previous twenty years were "I am only a hired hand"; this passage is "though I manage these things, these things are not mine." Management is not the same as recognition. He could faithfully work in this position for another ten years, twenty years, a whole lifetime — and still not know that these things were his from the very beginning.

### The Deathbed Declaration

The father watches the son's psychological state grow easier, bit by bit — "the father knew his son's mind had gradually grown at ease, that he had achieved great resolve, and that he looked back with shame on his former mind" — and knows the moment has finally come.

On his deathbed, the father does four things.

**First, he convenes the assembly.** He gathers his relatives, the king, the ministers, the kṣatriyas, the householders — all the witnesses of weight he can summon.

**Second, he formally declares the father-son relationship.** Before all the witnesses, the father says: "Gentlemen, take note. **This is my son, born of me**... **He is truly my son. I am truly his father.**"

**Third, he recounts the history.** He explicitly states the fifty-odd years of separation: "In such-and-such a city he left me and fled, and wandered in hardship for over fifty years. His original name is such; my name is such. Long ago in my home city, full of grief, I sought him; here, by chance, I have found him." He makes public the time, place, course, and names of the separation.

**Fourth, he formally transfers the property.** "**All that I now possess belongs to my son**, and all that was taken in and given out before, my son knows." All the goods were always his; the past income and expenditure, he knows it all.

The four actions joined together are one thing — **under the greatest possible witness, the fact is formally established**. Not told in private, not implied, not left for the other to guess on his own; but in public, in the most formal language, the three sentences — "you are my son, I am your father, all of this is yours" — are spoken aloud.

The son's reaction, this time, finally turns:

> At that time the destitute son, hearing his father's words, was filled with great joy, gaining what he had never possessed before, and he thought: **I never had any thought of seeking, and now this treasure has come to me of itself**.

"I never had any thought of seeking, and now this treasure has come to me of itself" — this is the last line of the whole parable of the destitute son, and the core line on which all its later interpretation turns.

Note its structure.

This is not "I have at last obtained what I strove for." The destitute son never strove for this treasure — he did not even know the treasure existed. Nor is it "I have at last become worthy of this position" — he did not suddenly become worthy; he is still the man who removed excrement for twenty years.

This is a statement of a completely different structure — **I never sought, yet the treasure came on its own**.

The four characters "came of itself" (自然而至) carry the entire weight. **When what is originally possessed is recognized, the recognizer's posture is not "acquisition" but "acceptance of a fact that was always there."** Acquisition is: I go and take it, I strive, I achieve. Recognition is: I finally see the thing that was always here, waiting for me to see it. The two are completely different postures.

### "Originally Possessed Yet Unknown" — A Motif Not Found in the Āgamas

Reading this far, a reader who has studied early Buddhism might ask: does this story have a seed in the Āgamas?

The answer is: no.

The Burning House parable can be traced all the way back to the *Saṃyuktāgama*'s "all is burning" discourse; but the destitute son has no structurally corresponding seed anywhere in the entire Āgama system. There is no narrative of a father and son separated, no scene of a father recognizing a son while the son does not recognize the father, no structure of inheritance gradually revealed. The destitute son is **a purely Mahāyāna literary creation**.

But this "no" is not an empty gap. It points to something else — the lineage of the destitute son lies not within the Āgamas but within the Mahāyāna itself.

In the Mahāyāna sūtras and treatises there is a set of structurally resonant parables that together form a lineage of "originally possessed yet unknown":

The **jewel-in-the-garment parable** in the *Five Hundred Disciples Receive Predictions* chapter — a man lies down drunk, and a close friend sews a priceless jewel into the inside of his garment before leaving. The man wakes and wanders in search of food and clothing, "content with a little," not knowing there is a jewel in his garment, until the friend meets him again and points it out to him.

The **poor woman's treasure parable** in the *Tathāgata Nature* chapter of the *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra* — a poor woman has pure gold buried in her house, and no one in the household knows of it, until someone uses skillful means to excavate it. The *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra* itself gives the gloss directly after this parable: "The skillful one is the Tathāgata, the poor woman is all immeasurable sentient beings, and the gold treasure is buddha-nature itself."

The **warrior's forehead-jewel parable** in the *Tathāgata Nature* chapter of the *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra* — a strong warrior has a diamond jewel set in his brow; in a fight the jewel sinks into his flesh, and he thinks he has lost it. The physician holds up a mirror for him to look into, and only then does he see that the jewel was there all along.

The *Laṅkāvatāra*'s **tathāgatagarbha wrapped like a great priceless jewel in filthy garments** — the *tathāgatagarbha* is intrinsically pure, wrapped in the filthy garments of the aggregates, sense-bases, and sense-elements, but **it has been there all along**.

These four parables, together with the destitute son itself, share a single formula:

> **Originally possessed + not noticed + skillful means + manifestation**

The decisive feature of "manifestation" is that **it is not new acquisition**. All these parables say the same thing: the thing recognized was always there; you do not obtain it, you finally see it.

There is one important internal difference among the five parables — the mode of revelation differs. The jewel-in-the-garment is the friend pointing it out face to face; the poor woman's treasure is someone excavating it on her behalf; the forehead-jewel parable is the physician **holding up a mirror so the warrior sees it himself**; the *Laṅkāvatāra* is the buddhas expounding it; the destitute son is **a public proclamation before a convened assembly**. Each mode of revelation corresponds to a teaching situation: individual pointing-out, self-witnessing guidance, scriptural exposition, institutional establishment.

The special place of the destitute son within this lineage is **institutional establishment** — the convening of the assembly, the recounting of the history, the formal proclamation, these three actions joined together, are not a private pointing-out but a **public acknowledgment**. This public dimension is the core feature distinguishing the destitute son from the other parables of "originally possessed yet unknown." It is not only about a turn in individual psychology, but also about how that turn **is established as fact before the assembly**.

Where does this lineage finally converge? It converges on the root Mahāyāna proposition: "all sentient beings possess buddha-nature." The *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra* itself states this directly after the poor woman's treasure parable; Kuiji, annotating the destitute son in his *Lotus Compendium*, also cites the *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra*'s "excavating the gold treasure, thus revealing to all sentient beings the jewel of their own awakening," joining the destitute son, the poor woman, and buddha-nature into a single line.

### The Burning House and the Destitute Son — A Matched Pair

The destitute son and the previous chapter's Burning House, joined together, form the most important matched pair among the *Lotus*'s seven parables.

Vasubandhu's *Lotus Treatise* maps the seven *Lotus* parables onto seven kinds of self-conceit. The Burning House is the first, the cure for the "power-seeker" — one who pursues worldly fortune and treats a better worldly position as the way out. The destitute son is the second, the cure for "**the śrāvaka who one-sidedly determines** that his vehicle is no different from the Tathāgata's vehicle" — one who has already practiced, already been liberated, and believes he is already home.

The two cures point in exactly opposite directions:

**The Burning House cures the misplacement of "believing one has not yet arrived"** — out there in the world are better fruits, that is where I ought to go. This is mistaking the contaminated for the uncontaminated.
**The destitute son cures the misplacement of "believing one has already arrived"** — I have already accomplished it, my liberation is as complete as the Buddha's. This is mistaking the small uncontaminated for the great.

The methods of cure are also exactly opposite. Vasubandhu's formula for the Burning House cure is "**treating one as three**" — in reality there is only the one Buddha-vehicle, but to coax the children out of the house it is expediently spoken of as three carriages. His formula for the destitute son cure is "**treating three as one**" — the laborious work of the three vehicles is not discarded, but all of it is gathered into the inheritance of the great vehicle.

The first opens one carriage out into three (a differentiation at the teaching stage); the second gathers the three vehicles into one son (a unification at the inheritance stage). The first is the father coaxing the children out of the house; the second is the father receiving the son back from his self-judgment as a hired laborer.

This matched pair is not a literary accident; it is the internal design of the seven-parable system: the first two parables handle the two most fundamental, most oppositely directed misplacements, framing in one stroke the typical wrong turns of every practitioner.

### An Honest Divergence — How Three Commentators Read the Destitute Son Differently

The previous chapter noted something — that on the point "there were no three carriages outside the gate," Jizang of the Madhyamaka and Kuiji of the Yogācāra were, unusually, in complete agreement. At the time we read this agreement as an important scholarly fact.

But on the destitute son, these same two (together with Zhiyi of the Tiantai) show a clear divergence.

Zhiyi and Jizang read the destitute son in a **universal** way — the destitute son is every śrāvaka, every śrāvaka is the destitute son, with no lineage boundary set. Every practitioner of the two vehicles can recognize himself in this parable, and every one of them can, like the end of the text, be connected to the great-white-ox-carriage inheritance of the estate.

Kuiji reads the destitute son in a **restrictive** way. In his *Lotus Compendium*, he draws on the doctrinal tradition of his time and divides śrāvakas into four kinds — **the determinate-lineage śrāvaka** (the one bound for quiescence, who enters final nirvāṇa without remainder and never turns his mind back), **the conceited** (the ordinary being who claims realization without having realized), **the back-turned-bodhicitta** (one who originally aroused the great mind and then regressed to seeking the small), and **the apparitional** (a bodhisattva manifesting in śrāvaka form). Then Kuiji explicitly determines: the *Lotus* gives predictions only to the back-turned-bodhicitta and the apparitional śrāvakas; the determinate-lineage and the conceited, "the Buddha does not give predictions, because their roots are not yet ripe."

In other words — **Kuiji holds that the destitute son applies only to the indeterminate-lineage śrāvaka**. Those who originally aroused the great mind and then regressed, the *Lotus* will receive back into the Mahāyāna; but those who from the root walk only the śrāvaka path, who upon entering nirvāṇa without remainder extinguish body and consciousness — for these determinate-lineage śrāvakas the *Lotus* gives no prediction, and they **will never become buddhas**.

This is a doctrinally very hard determination. It is not a surface difference of exegesis from Zhiyi and Jizang's universal reading; it is a divergence of fundamental position.

Why do Madhyamaka and Yogācāra agree on the Burning House yet diverge on the destitute son?

Look closely and you can see it — the core issues of these two texts fall on different axes.

The core issue of the Burning House is **skillful means**: are there three carriages outside the gate? Are the three carriages a deception? Is the worldly operation of post-attainment cognition a downgraded truth? These are all **epistemological** questions — about "the true or false status of a teaching medium." On this axis, the conclusions of Madhyamaka (all dharmas are empty, so the expedient may be said to exist) and Yogācāra (post-attainment cognition is itself the way truth operates in the conventional) can agree.

The core issue of the destitute son is **lineage**: can the determinate-lineage śrāvaka become a buddha or not? Is the difference among the five lineages a fact or an expedient? These are **ontological** questions — about "the true face of sentient beings' inner structure." On this axis, Madhyamaka (which treats lineage difference too as dependently arisen and ultimately empty) and Yogācāra (in which the seeds within the ālaya-vijñāna have real differences) diverge deeply, and the conclusions cannot agree.

This gives a correction and a supplement to the previous chapter: **the relation between Madhyamaka and Yogācāra is neither constantly aligned nor constantly opposed, but issue-sensitive** —

> aligned on epistemological issues (skillful means, teaching, cognition),
> divergent on ontological issues (lineage, seeds, the structure of the real).

This is not a difference that can be easily smoothed over. Both schools have deep scriptural and treatise foundations; both are handling real doctrinal problems.

### The Tension Between the *Lotus* and Yogācāra — Presented, Not Falsely Dissolved

Kuiji's restrictive reading brings out a larger problem — between the *Lotus*'s "gathering three into one" and Yogācāra's "five distinct lineages," there is a real tension.

The *Lotus* says "there is no other vehicle, neither two nor three"; Kuiji says "the determinate-lineage śrāvaka will never become a buddha." Read directly, these two statements contradict each other. Across the generations, the Yogācāra tradition developed two routes of reconciliation.

**The first is the *Saṃgraha* bridge.** The verse in fascicle three of the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* is the root basis of this route:

> For the indeterminate-lineage śrāvakas and the other bodhisattvas, the One Vehicle is taught to draw them into the Mahāyāna; for the determinate-lineage, because dharma, no-self, and liberation are equal, the One Vehicle is taught only in the ultimate sense.

The meaning is: when the Buddha speaks of "the One Vehicle," there are two kinds of audience — the indeterminate lineage and the other bodhisattvas. For these two kinds of beings, the Buddha speaks the One Vehicle to "draw them into the Mahāyāna." For the determinate-lineage śrāvaka, the Buddha speaks the One Vehicle only in the sense of "the equality of dharma and no-self-liberation" (an equality at the level of principle), not to turn them into bodhisattvas. Kuiji's conclusion following this route is — **the *Lotus*'s "One Vehicle" is in fact an expedient teaching addressed to the indeterminate capacity**.

**The second is Fazang's mediation.** Fazang of the Huayan school, in his *Commentary on the Awakening of Faith*, offers another way of handling this — judging "five distinct lineages" and "all will become buddhas" as **two teachings, the conventional and the real**:

> First, under the conventional teaching, it concerns the bodhisattva-lineage and the indeterminate, but not the other three, since they have no part — as the *Yogācārabhūmi* and others say. Second, under the real teaching, all sentient beings are its audience, since none will fail ultimately to attain bodhi.

Fazang's meaning is: "five distinct lineages" is spoken under the conventional teaching, framed only by the capacities that can be judged at present; but under the real teaching, all sentient beings will ultimately attain bodhi. Fazang also cites the *Buddha-Nature Treatise*'s "saying there are some without lineage permanently is non-ultimate teaching" — clearly judging the no-lineage doctrine to be a non-definitive teaching. He even goes further, proposing that the determinate-lineage śrāvaka, after entering nirvāṇa without remainder, still has "three remainders" and will in the end turn his mind back.

These two routes joined together provide a workable reconciliation — the destitute son can be read as the indeterminate lineage (the *Saṃgraha* route), or as the determinate lineage who will eventually turn his mind back (Fazang's route).

But here we must honestly stop and offer an observation: **this reconciliation does not fully dissolve the tension; it only relocates it.**

Relocates it to where? To the question of "will the determinate-lineage śrāvaka ever turn his mind back?" Kuiji's hard position is: never. His scriptural support is fascicle thirty-five of the *Yogācārabhūmi*: "the lineage-less *pudgala*... is by determination unfit to fulfill supreme perfect awakening." Fazang's soft position is: in the end he will turn his mind back, supported by the *Buddha-Nature Treatise*'s "saying there are some without lineage permanently is non-ultimate teaching."

Both positions have scriptural and treatise support, and those supports are themselves traditions never fully unified within the Mahāyāna. Kuiji's authorities cluster in the early treatises of the Yogācāra system; Fazang's cluster in the *tathāgatagarbha* corpus and later treatises. **The two positions represent two fundamentally different Mahāyāna answers to the question of whether there are real differences in the inner structure of sentient beings** — a question the Mahāyāna never fully unified within itself, and to try to dissolve it directly with the *Lotus*'s single phrase "gathering three into one" is to read the *Lotus* too lightly.

This book's stance is — **present both sides, do not pretend to dissolve them**.

Kuiji's hard restriction has its doctrinal seriousness. It defends an important principle: the "inner structure of sentient beings" cannot be carelessly reduced to the rhetoric of "all beings are equal." The *Saṃgraha*–Fazang reconciliation also has its doctrinal seriousness. It defends another important principle: lineage difference cannot be made an unbridgeable ontological gulf that renders the *Lotus*'s "One Vehicle" hollow. Both principles should be preserved, rather than one used to crush the other.

⚠️ Reading this far, the reader may have a natural reaction: "So which kind am I? Am I indeterminate lineage or determinate-lineage śrāvaka?" — this self-questioning is the wrong direction. Kuiji's classification is a determination at the level of doctrine (a doctrinal tool for classifying sentient beings from an external standpoint); a practitioner's judgment about himself is a question at the level of practice (looking, from an internal standpoint, at how he himself moves forward). From the standpoint of practice, you should never classify yourself as "determinate, so the *Lotus* does not speak of me" — that very self-classification is the contemporary version of the destitute son turning and fleeing at the gate. At the same time, you should not classify yourself as "I was always the elder's son anyway, so all that work does not matter" — this is the mind of low station moving in the other direction. Both wrong classifications are misplacements of self-knowledge. The classification Kuiji left behind is a doctrinal tool, not a self-examination checklist for practice.

### "Still Considering Himself a Hired Laborer of Low Station" — The Meaning of Twenty Years

Let us return to the most central line of this chapter: **"still considered himself a hired laborer of low station."**

The destitute son works in his father's house for twenty years, called "son" every day, drawing double wages, laboring alongside his father, and still "considers himself a hired laborer of low station." This line is not literary rhetoric; it is a precise depiction of a real thing — **a person's fundamental judgment about himself does not change directly just because external conditions change**.

This point is especially important for today's practitioner.

You can take it back to yourself and test it. When you are in some high position, some good opportunity, or in the company of someone of higher attainment, what is your first reaction? If it is "no, that is not my place" — then you have seen the contemporary form of the destitute son's mind of low station in yourself. This reaction need not be suppressed (suppression becomes another kind of arrogance), nor need it be affirmed (affirmation misses its hollowness) — it needs to be **seen**. After being seen, it will continue to arise, but the seeing itself is already the beginning of a turn.

The "twenty years" the text gives is no exaggeration. This is the real order of magnitude of time that the transformation of inner structure requires. If you expect that finishing a book, hearing one teaching, or completing one meditation retreat will turn the mind of low station around — you will be disappointed again and again. Real transformation requires sustained, end-not-in-sight, unromantic work.

⚠️ Here we must correct a common modern misreading. Modern spiritual writing likes to read "twenty years of removing excrement" as "humble and sacred inner work," "the dark night of the soul," "the practice of humility," and other such romantic images. This reading skips one thing — **for the destitute son, removing excrement was just removing excrement**. It was not romantic and not sacred. It was filthy, exhausting, real labor that lasted twenty years, and during those twenty years the son's inner state was always "considering himself a hired laborer of low station" — he did not, in his own mind, sanctify the work. He simply did the work he could do, the work he believed himself fit to do. The force of the destitute son comes precisely from this realism — it does not let the son suddenly awaken in the middle of the twenty years, does not let removing excrement become an occasion of sudden enlightenment, and does not let the father appear midway to disclose the truth. It lets twenty years be twenty years, removing excrement be removing excrement, the mind of low station be the mind of low station.

**This realism is itself the destitute son's respect for the practitioner's real situation.** The middle stretch of practice truly does contain a span of unromantic, unsacred, end-not-in-sight work, and this span of work cannot be skipped.

The promise the destitute son gives is not "rapid awakening," but **"the father is waiting all along."** This "waiting" — the fifty years of seeking in the text, the twenty years of laboring alongside, the convening of the assembly to proclaim only at death — is a fact the practitioner can rely on. You do the work you can do right now; the mind of low station is there when it is there, and turns when it turns; and on the father's side, he is waiting all along.

### Two Layers of Ignorance

The Burning House handles **the ignorance of one's situation** — "I do not know I am in the fire." The destitute son handles **the ignorance of one's identity** — "I do not know who I originally am." Together they form a complete diagnosis of ignorance.

But there is a third question.

Even if I know I am in the fire, and know that I was always the elder's son — when the same teaching falls upon listeners of different capacities, why do some, hearing it, immediately leave the house, some only after twenty years are willing to accept that they are the son, and some show no response at all? The teaching is the same teaching; why are the effects so different? Is this difference a **problem of the teaching** or a **problem of the capacity**?

The next chapter handles this question — **the parable of the medicinal herbs**. Like the clouds and rain coming down from the sky, the great trees receive their moistening as great trees, the small grasses receive their moistening as small grasses, "the moistening pervades all; herbs, trees, and forests each receive their share." One rain falls equally, and a thousand differing plants each take what they need. What the medicinal herbs parable means to say is: **universal moistening and difference are facts that hold simultaneously, not two things that contradict each other in sequence**.

This question will push the lineage tension to the next level.
