## 7.3 The Burning House — The Father's Skillful Means and the Fire of the Three Realms

The previous chapter left us with a question. If, in the end, there is only the one Buddha vehicle, why do the buddhas still spend such a long time "speaking of three by way of distinction"? Why is skillful means unavoidable?

The first of the Lotus Sūtra's seven parables — the parable of the burning house — exists precisely to answer this question dramatically. It is the body of the "Parable" chapter, and the longest single parable in the entire Lotus. Its position is not an accident. The previous chapter's "one great cause" and "gathering the three back into the one" were the direct doctrinal proclamation; the burning house takes that proclamation and *performs* it, for the sake of those who cannot hear direct teaching.

But this has never been a reassuring story.

### The Great House Catches Fire

It begins like this:

> Suppose, in a state, in a town, in a village, there is a great elder, advanced in years, of immeasurable wealth. ... His dwelling is vast; it has only one gate; many people live in it — one hundred, two hundred, even five hundred. Its halls have decayed; the walls are crumbling; the pillar-bases have rotted; the beams are leaning. Then, all at once, fire breaks out on every side and engulfs the house. The elder's children — perhaps ten, twenty, or thirty — are inside.

A great house, an old house — halls decayed, walls crumbling, pillar-bases rotted, beams leaning — catches fire on all four sides at once. Hundreds, even thousands, live inside it, and among them are thirty of the elder's own children.

The elder, standing outside the gate, sees the fire break out. His first reaction is terror, but immediately he grasps something deeper — his children are still inside, and they do not know they are in the fire. The sūtra's description of this is fine-grained to an almost unusual degree:

> The children are within the burning house, absorbed in their play, unaware, unalarmed, unfrightened. The fire is coming for them, suffering will press upon them, but their hearts feel no aversion, no thought of escape.

Notice the four parallel negatives — unaware, unknowing, unalarmed, unfrightened. The fire has already reached their bodies, the pain already presses upon them, and still the children are absorbed in play. This is not dullness. It is a structural inability to see.

The elder's first rescue plan is to carry the children out directly — "my own arms have strength; I could carry them out using my robe or a small table." But he rejects this plan himself: the house has only one gate, and that gate is narrow; the children are too young to understand, and to carry them out by force might only crush them into the fire.

He turns to a second plan — telling the children plainly: the house is on fire, come out at once.

This plan fails too. The sūtra's account of this failure is the single most crucial passage in the whole parable:

> Though the father, out of love, gave skillful warnings, the children, absorbed in their play, would not believe him. They were not alarmed, not frightened, had no will to leave. They did not even know what "fire" was, what "house" meant, what "destruction" could refer to. They simply ran east and west in their game, glancing at their father and continuing.

The children cannot understand. It is not that they refuse to listen — they cannot understand. "They did not even know what 'fire' was, what 'house' meant, what 'destruction' could refer to." The word *fire*, the word *burning*, the word *destruction* — these have no referent for them. They hear their father's voice, but that voice is only a kind of background noise; it does not constitute a motive. And so they "simply ran east and west in their game, glancing at their father and continuing" — they keep running about and playing, glance at their father, and turn back to their play.

It is in the face of this failure that the father arrives at the third plan: **"Now I shall set up a skillful means, that my children may be saved."**

He knows what the children love best in ordinary times — rare and curious playthings. He calls out from outside the gate: out here are the things you most want, sheep-carts, deer-carts, ox-carts, every kind of fine plaything, and if you do not come out quickly to take them, you will surely regret it later.

Hearing this, the children rouse themselves with excitement, push past one another, race together, and rush out of the burning house.

Once they are out, the children ask their father for the three carts. But the father gives each of them, equally, a single great cart drawn by a white ox — "the cart is tall and wide, adorned with every jewel, railed all around, hung with bells on all four sides ... drawn by a white ox of pure and glossy hide, of fine form and great strength, that walks evenly and swift as the wind."

The final layer of the passage is an exchange between the Buddha and Śāriputra. The Buddha asks: "In giving his children this precious great cart, did the elder commit any falsehood?" Śāriputra answers: "No, World-Honored One. The elder's intent was simply to save his children from the fire and preserve their lives, and this is no falsehood."

This exchange looks like a small summation, but in fact it forecloses one reading in advance and opens another. What it forecloses is the reading "the father lied." What it opens is the question — **what, exactly, is the relationship between skillful means and the real?**

The Buddha then applies the parable: "So too, the Tathāgata is the father of all the world. ... He produces beings within the burning house of the three realms in order to save them from the fires of birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, suffering, ignorance, and the three poisons." The verse section gives the four lines most often quoted from the entire Lotus:

> The three realms know no peace; they are like a burning house. They are filled with every suffering, supremely fearful.

From here, the cosmic-scale image of the three-realm burning house is fixed for good.

### All Is Burning — the Āgama Root of the Fire Image

This image of fire is not a Mahāyāna invention.

Already in the earliest phase of Buddhism, the Buddha used *burning* as a core teaching metaphor. The most direct root is found in the *Saṃyukta-āgama*, fascicle eight, Sūtra 197 — a passage that bears a famous name in English-language Buddhist studies, the *Fire Sermon*:

> Bhikṣus, all is burning. What is "all is burning"? The eye is burning; forms are burning; eye-consciousness is burning; eye-contact is burning; whatever feeling arises conditioned by eye-contact — pleasant, painful, or neither — that too is burning. So too the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind are burning. ... Burning with what? Burning with the fire of greed, the fire of hatred, the fire of delusion; burning with birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, and distress.

Several details in this passage are worth pausing over.

First, it lays out "all is burning" across the complete framework of the six sense-bases, six objects, six consciousnesses, six contacts, and six feelings. The Buddha is not making a vague claim that "the world is on fire." He is pointing to the location of the burning at every single node of cognition. The thing your eye sees is burning; the consciousness that arises when you see it is burning; the feeling that the seeing gives rise to is burning — from the outermost sense organ to the innermost emotion, the entire cognitive assembly line is on fire.

Second, the cause of the burning is precisely identified as three: craving, aversion, delusion — together with the existential, structural scorching of birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, and distress. This is the earliest version of what the Lotus burning house will call "the fire of the three poisons" plus "the fire of birth, aging, sickness, and death."

Third, the audience for this teaching was extraordinary. They were a thousand former fire-worshipping ascetics, whose original religious practice had centered on tending sacred fire. For the Buddha to say "all is burning" to this group was an exceedingly precise pedagogical choice — to point at fire with fire, turning them from the fire they tended to the fire they were (the fire of the three poisons in their own hearts). The closing line records that the thousand monks, hearing what the Buddha said, gave rise to no further outflows, and their minds attained liberation.

Deeper still is the sheer weight that the *Saṃyukta-āgama* as a whole places on the image of fire. Fascicle seven has the "hair and clothes on fire" series: the Buddha opens with "as a man whose hair and clothes have caught fire — what should he do?" and applies to each item in turn — the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right efforts, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, the noble eightfold path, the four noble truths — the urgency of "in order to cut off the fire of impermanence." Fascicle thirty-one goes so far as to list among the synonyms of nirvāṇa "free of blazing flame, free of burning" — and this is especially important, because it reminds us that **the literal meaning of the word *nirvāṇa* is "the extinguishing of fire."** Burning is not some literary flourish of the Āgama; it is the core identification of the very nature of saṃsāra.

Seen from this angle, the Lotus's burning house is not a Mahāyāna invention of a new symbol. It is the dramatic completion of this core metaphorical line from early Buddhism — from the individual, seated in meditation, observing the burning at the six sense-doors, enlarged into the cosmic-scale narrative of a great house, a group of children, a father, and a rescue.

⚠️ One thing needs to be made clear here: this is not a "Mahāyāna is higher than the lesser vehicle" graded judgment of teachings. "All is burning" and "the burning house" are the same doctrine at two scales — the former is the range of individual experience that can be observed in meditation, the latter the cosmological metaphor for the condition of ignorant beings. The two are structurally identical and substantively continuous; only the pedagogical situation and the intended audience differ.

### Why the Three Realms Are a Burning House

The compound concept "the three-realm burning house" needs to be taken apart.

**The three realms** is an existential structure already established in early Buddhism: the desire-realm, the form-realm, and the formless-realm. The three realms are the basic framework of the Buddhist cosmology, spanning all of saṃsāric existence from the hells up to the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Why does the burning house parable use "the three realms" rather than "the five destinies" (hells, hungry ghosts, animals, humans, gods)? This choice is itself an act of doctrinal classification. The five destinies are a horizontal sorting by degree of suffering and pleasure; the three realms are a vertical sorting by **object of attachment** — the desire-realm is attachment to the five sense-objects, the form-realm is attachment to the bliss of meditative absorption, the formless-realm is attachment to extremely subtle mental states. What the burning house parable means to say is this: **all three layers of attachment are on fire.**

Even one who has cultivated to the very summit of the formless-realm — the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, with its meditative bliss lasting hundreds of millions of great aeons — is still inside the burning house. It is only that the fire on this floor is more refined, harder to detect.

This verdict carries enormous pedagogical significance. If the fire were only in the desire-realm, then one who cultivated to the form-realm absorptions would have left the burning house; if the fire were only in the form-realm, then one who reached the formless-realm would have left it. But the verdict of the burning house parable is this: **the whole house is on fire.** There is no "liberation within the three realms" that is genuine liberation.

This verdict shuts off two roads directly. One is the road of "substituting meditative bliss for nirvāṇa" — however deep the absorption, it is still inside the house. The other is the road of "substituting divine and human merit for buddhahood" — however excellent the gods, they are still inside the house. The burning house's verdict on both roads is: whether you run up, run down, run left, or run right inside the house, you are running inside the fire. The only true direction is out the gate.

Kuiji's commentary in the *Fahua xuanzan* makes this layer of meaning explicit: the fire of the three realms is not an external fire — it is the three poisons within, unfolded to their full extent within the existential framework of the three realms. The three realms you inhabit are the very house your own afflictions project.

### The Children at Play — a Diagnosis, Not a Reproach

The part of the burning house parable most easily misread is the passage of "the children at play."

On the surface it looks like a reproach of beings: you are plainly in the fire, so why don't you come out? But this is a misreading.

Zhiyi maps "play" onto the turbidity of views and the turbidity of defilements among the five turbidities, and maps "unaware, unknowing, unalarmed, unfrightened" onto the turbidity of beings. This mapping is important — it is a **diagnosis**, not a value judgment. The reason beings do not know they are in the fire is not that they are stupid, bad, lazy, or morally defective. It is that **ignorance is simply like this: the core feature of ignorance is the inability to see the situation one is in.** If ignorance could see itself, it would no longer be ignorance.

This point matters greatly for where the reader stands. On reading the "children at play" passage, one's first reaction is easily: "how pitiable beings are," "how foolish beings are" — placing oneself in the father's position, looking down on those children who cannot see the fire. But that very posture is already misaligned. The true function of the burning house parable is to draw the reader *into* the house — **am I myself perhaps also at play right now? Is the "good" I am pursuing right now also interior to the burning house?**

That self-questioning is the entry point of the parable.

---

There is a modern parallel that can help us feel how precise the diagnosis "the children at play" really is.

⚠️ Contemporary disaster psychology has a concept called **normalcy bias** — when faced with an emergency or disaster, people tend to normalize it, deny its severity, and continue with their ordinary routines. The interview records from the 9/11 Memorial Museum contain a startling finding: after the Twin Towers were struck, many people inside did not evacuate immediately but instead returned to their desks to gather their belongings, called their families, organized their files — their cognitive systems "could not accept" that what had happened fell within the category of "a real emergency," and so they forced it back into a familiar category and went on acting by their ordinary routine.

The core mechanism of normalcy bias is this: human cognition relies heavily on the "continuity of past experience" to judge the present. When an event exceeds the range that past experience can cover, the system's first reaction is not to initiate an emergency response but to force the event back into a familiar category. "This is probably just a drill," "the fire won't reach this floor," "it has never happened before, so probably nothing this time." This mechanism is evolutionarily functional — it keeps a person from over-reacting to every small fluctuation — but in a genuine emergency, it kills.

Place this back in the context of the burning house: the eight words "the children at play, unaware, unknowing, unalarmed, unfrightened" are a precise mirror of contemporary cognitive psychology. Why does the father's direct warning — "the house is on fire, come out at once" — fail? Because the word "fire," the word "burning," the word "house," for children who have never experienced fire, are merely abstract vocabulary — they cannot connect these words to any present sensory experience. In the face of this **rupture between concept and experience**, any direct warning is automatically normalized by the cognitive system: this can't be real, father is only trying to scare me, let us keep playing.

⚠️ But this analogy must stop at structural illumination. Normalcy bias is an observation of individuals' experience within a specific disaster scenario; the Lotus's "children at play" is an ontological-level diagnosis of ignorance — the former can be lessened through training and drills, the latter requires the level of remedy that is the Buddha's skillful means. The analogy helps the reader feel the precision of the diagnosis; it does not reduce ignorance to a cognitive bias.

It is precisely in the face of this rupture that the father's "setting up a skillful means" becomes the only available option.

### Skillful Means Is Not Deception — a Three-Layered Answer

The most dangerous doctrinal trap in the whole parable is to read it as a story about a "benevolent lie."

On the surface, the father did promise three carts and give a great cart instead — there is a gap between "what was said" and "what was done," and in worldly ethics such a gap is usually classed as lying. But this reading carries an extremely grave consequence: if the Buddha's skillful means is lying, then the whole of the dharma can be read as "provisional words spoken to make you liberate" — including the four noble truths, the twelve links of dependent arising, the eightfold path, even nirvāṇa itself, all of which could be relativized into skillful means. Once one goes that far, the dharma collapses into a hollow shell of nihilism.

Precisely because this trap is so lethal, from Vasubandhu to Zhiyi to Jizang to Kuiji, every major commentator in the history of Lotus interpretation, as if by agreement, treats "skillful means is not deception" as a problem that must be confronted head-on. Integrating their three layers of answer, we move from the surface to the epistemological foundation.

---

**The first layer is Jizang's — there were no three carts outside the door to begin with.**

Jizang's handling of this question in the *Fahua yishu* is extremely direct, using a proof by contradiction: **if there really had been three carts outside the door, the children would have seen them the moment they came out, and there would have been no need to "ask" for them.** In the sūtra, once the children are out, they still have to ask their father for the three carts — "those playthings you promised before, the sheep-cart, the deer-cart, the ox-cart, please give them to us now." This very act of asking is itself proof that there were never any three carts outside the door.

The three carts are not physical objects the father promised; they are a sentence the father spoke. Jizang calls this "**vocal skillful means** — announcing three when there are none, pointing as though to say there are three carts outside the door." The three carts were, from the start, a linguistic device, not a promise of actual objects.

Once this verdict is established, the entire structure of the "father lied" complaint collapses. The father did not promise physical carts and then fail to deliver. The father, from beginning to end, did only one thing: **he uttered the sentence "outside there is something better to play with."** What that sentence conveys is true — there really is something better outside, something better than the toys the children were attached to. It is only that the father could not, in language the children could understand in the moment, say clearly what that "better thing" was, so he used language they could understand. After they came out, the children received the great white-ox cart — this is not "the father failed to deliver the three carts," it is "the father handed over what could not be directly expressed to children who now had the capacity to receive it."

---

**The second layer is Zhiyi's — the three came from beings, not from the Buddha's original intent.**

Zhiyi's direct argument in the *Fahua wenju* runs like this: the Buddha's original heart — "his original wish to teach the great" — never changed; it was always to teach the one Buddha vehicle. But the existence of "those who cannot receive it" forced this original heart to be realized by way of "speaking of three by distinction." The three carts are not the Buddha's aim; the three carts are the need of those who cannot receive the great vehicle. So in the argument, the key to "no falsehood" is not "the father did not break his word" but "the father remained faithful throughout to his own original heart" — the original heart is to teach the great, the actual method is to teach three first, but the very aim of "teaching three first" is to be able, in the end, to teach the great. Where there is no betrayal of the original heart, there is no falsehood.

Elsewhere Zhiyi puts it even more directly: "Skillful means may be provisional — but does the truth therefore become false?"

**The provisional is not the false.** The provisional is "a form temporarily adopted for the sake of a deeper aim"; the false is "an utterance that does not correspond to the truth." These two are, logically, different categories of thing. Worldly lying is the latter; the Buddha's skillful means is the former.

---

**The third layer comes from Yogācāra — post-attainment cognition.**

The distinction Zhiyi gives — "the provisional is not the false" — has a deeper epistemological foundation in Yogācāra. The *Cheng weishi lun*, fascicle eight, gives an extremely precise account of the relationship between root cognition and post-attainment cognition:

> Only after non-discriminating cognition has realized suchness can post-attainment cognition understand the other-dependent nature as illusion-like. ... It is not that one fails to see suchness and yet understands phenomena — though they appear, they are not real.

The two cognitions of Yogācāra — root cognition (non-discriminating cognition) and post-attainment cognition — are not two different cognitions; they are two aspects of one and the same wisdom. Root cognition directly realizes suchness (the perfected nature) — this is "seeing the essence"; post-attainment cognition then returns to the phenomenal world and, on the basis of having already realized suchness, sees the dependent nature as "illusion-like" — this is "arising into function."

The key to this structure is that **post-attainment cognition is not "downgraded truth," not "a version adjusted for teaching."** Post-attainment cognition is the very way suchness itself operates within the phenomenal world. The Buddha's skillful means is not a detour around the real in order to speak something false; **the Buddha's skillful means is the real, shown forth concretely at the level of the dependent nature.**

The *Cheng weishi lun*, fascicle nine, makes this clearer still: among the ten perfections, the last four — skillful means, vow, power, knowledge — are all the concrete unfolding of post-attainment cognition "engaging the conventional." The essence of the perfection of skillful means is just prajñā in operation after it has returned to the conventional. It is not a separate faculty alongside prajñā; it is prajñā's phenomenological form.

The reason the father is able to "speak of the three carts" is precisely that he first saw the real in its entirety; **one who has not seen the real cannot even set up a skillful means.**

### A Rare Concord Between Madhyamaka and Yogācāra

The first layer of answer used Jizang, of the Madhyamaka lineage; the basis of the third layer is Yogācāra. These two traditions are divided in their positions on most other questions, yet on this single point — "there were no three carts outside the door" — they are in rare and complete concord, and this concord is worth marking on its own.

Kuiji, in the *Fahua xuanzan*, gives an extremely fine analysis of the "cart-body" of the three carts. His verdict is that the "cart-body" of the three carts, seen from the Yogācāra angle, is essentially the empty designation of "contaminated post-attainment cognition" and "worldly samādhi." That is, the three carts are not genuine "vehicles"; they are "named but without a substantial body" — they have the name, but there is no actual thing one can board and drive away. The true cart-body — the thing that can actually carry beings to ultimate liberation — is one only: the all-knowing wisdom represented by the great white-ox cart.

Place Kuiji and Jizang side by side:

| Issue | Kuiji (Yogācāra) | Jizang (Madhyamaka) |
|---|---|---|
| Existence of the three carts | Empty designation, named but without substantial body | Vocal skillful means, no carts outside the door |
| "Cart-body" of the three carts | Contaminated post-attainment cognition, worldly samādhi | Pure linguistic device |
| Cart-body of the great cart | All-knowing wisdom | Middle Way, the one vehicle |
| Meaning of asking for the carts | The asking proves the three carts are empty designation | The asking proves there were no carts outside the door |
| Verdict on three carts → great cart | Not an upgrade, but a revelation | Not a fulfillment, but a revelation |

The two traditions use different terms, but their conclusions are identical: **the three carts are pure linguistic devices; there were never any three carts outside the door.** The historical reason for this concord is probably this: whether one comes by the Madhyamaka path of "all dharmas are empty, therefore skillful means speaks of the existent," or by the Yogācāra path of "contaminated post-attainment cognition is empty designation, while uncontaminated all-knowing wisdom is what truly exists," so long as one grants that the core of the dharma is conventional teaching given after the direct realization of the real, one cannot take skillful means for a physical fact. The essence of this concord is — **so long as the concept of skillful means is taken seriously, only one conclusion is available.**

This verdict also incidentally corrects another common misreading. It is easy to read the great white-ox cart as "a cart of higher grade than the three carts," carrying a sense of a ladder: the *śrāvaka* vehicle is the beginner's level, the *pratyekabuddha* vehicle the middle, the bodhisattva vehicle the advanced, the Buddha vehicle the highest. But this sense of a ladder runs counter to the Lotus's own verdict — the Lotus says "neither two nor three." The three carts were from the start three names for the same single cart; it is only that beings of different capacities recognize it under different names. **The great cart is not an upgraded version of the three carts; the great cart is the thing the three carts pointed toward all along, which the children simply did not yet recognize.**

### The Seeker of Power — First Among the Seven Inverted Graspings

Vasubandhu's *Treatise on the Lotus* maps the seven Lotus parables onto seven kinds of inverted grasping. The burning house ranks first in the seven-parable system, and the type of being it corresponds to is the "**seeker of power**" — beings who pursue worldly authority, merit, and excellent refined results.

This ordering is worth pausing on.

By ordinary common sense, if the seven-parable system were arranged "from grave to light," it ought to place the parable that remedies the deepest and gravest affliction first. But Vasubandhu places the burning house first, and it corresponds to the "seeker of power" — a kind of person who not only does not fall into depravity but on the surface looks quite well-inclined toward the good: what they seek is the excellent and refined sphere of gods and humans, the most beautiful result that the world can imagine. Why is such a person placed first?

The answer is: **this kind of person is the most numerous, the most dangerous, and the most insidious.**

The "most depraved" are a minority of the population, and their problem is easy to identify (they themselves often know there is a problem). The seeker of power covers nearly all beings with religious leanings, moral awareness, and an aspiration toward merit — which is to say, **most readers of scripture are already in this category.** And the problem of this kind of person is most insidious: they practice in earnest, do good actively, pursue what is beautiful, and they rarely doubt their direction. And precisely because they rarely doubt their direction, it is very hard for them to see that "the very thing they are pursuing is itself interior to the burning house."

The burning house remedies this kind of person not with the words "you are wrong" but with the image **"the safe place you imagine is another burning house"** — by rendering the whole of the three realms as a burning house, it is saying: no beautiful, refined result that can be pursued within the three realms can substitute for genuine liberation.

Where lies the line between the seeker of power and the seeker of the Buddha way? On the surface, the two seem to differ only in degree — the seeker of power small in scope, the seeker of the Buddha way large. But from the doctrinal structure of the burning house, there is one key qualitative difference between them: **the former takes "pursuing the beautiful within the burning house" as the goal; the latter takes "leaving the burning house" as the goal.** The difference is not in the quality of the object pursued but in the coordinate system of the direction pursued. A person can enjoy every excellence of divine and human merit and still be a seeker of power, because his coordinate system contains no dimension called "outside the burning house"; another person, even if his present circumstances are wretched, has already ceased to be a seeker of power so long as his coordinate system contains "outside the burning house."

What the burning house parable does is to redraw this coordinate system by force — it says: **the "good" you think you are pursuing is in fact a "good" interior to the burning house; the true good is outside the gate.**

Once this redrawing is complete, the whole worldview of the seeker of power is overturned. The precision of Vasubandhu's placing the burning house first among the seven parables lies exactly here: **it does not remedy "the person who does not practice"; it remedies "the person whose direction of practice is wrong."** And the latter are far more numerous than the former.

The burning house demonstrates the Lotus's first move — telling you that this place is already on fire, and that skillful means is the only channel of communication by which the father can lead you out.

But walking out of the burning house is only the first step. The children who come out do not yet know that the great white-ox cart was theirs to begin with — they still take what they received to be an "extra prize." The father's immeasurable wealth, his pure and glossy white ox, all look to them like "someone else's things," only that they happen to have been given a share. If even "this was actually mine all along" is not recognized, then the second stage of skillful means is not yet complete.

The parable of the next chapter takes up exactly this problem — **the parable of the prodigal son.** A son of a wealthy elder, lost abroad since childhood, spends decades as a beggar; one day he returns to the gate of his own father's house, sees the splendor of the scene, and is so frightened he turns and runs. He does not know it is his own home. The father recognizes him, but cannot tell him directly — for if he said outright "you are my son, you are the heir to all of this," the son would die of fright. The father must use a very long skillful means, drawing the son back home step by step.

The problem the parable of the prodigal son addresses is this: **I am plainly the son of the elder — why do I take myself for a lowly hired hand?**

This problem lies one layer nearer than the burning house — the burning house addresses "I do not know I am in the fire"; the prodigal son addresses "I do not know who I originally am." The former is the ignorance about one's situation; the latter is the ignorance about one's identity. Together they make the complete diagnosis of ignorance.
