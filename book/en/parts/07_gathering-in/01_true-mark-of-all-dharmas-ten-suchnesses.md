## 7.1 The True Mark of All Dharmas and the Ten Suchnesses

Where does the road home begin to open?

It opens with a single line from the Skillful Means chapter of the Lotus Sūtra.

The structural backbone of the Lotus is its division into two halves—**the fourteen chapters of the trace gate and the fourteen chapters of the origin gate**. The trace gate has the Skillful Means chapter as its core; the origin gate has the chapter on the Tathāgata's lifespan as its core. Every parable, every prediction of buddhahood, every gate of practice in the whole sūtra grows up out of these two cores. And the most crucial stretch within the Skillful Means chapter falls, in turn, upon a very short piece of text—the Buddha rises from *samādhi*, tells Śāriputra that the wisdom of the Buddhas is profound and measureless, then says one line, and immediately refuses to say more, presenting that unsayable content through the ten suchnesses instead.

This three-beat structure is itself the teaching.

### "Stop, Śāriputra"—A Refusal That Comes Before Any Explanation

The Buddha says to Śāriputra:

> Stop, Śāriputra, there is no need to say more. Why? Because what the Buddha has accomplished is the foremost, rarest, most difficult to understand of all dharmas. Only a Buddha together with a Buddha can exhaustively fathom the true mark of all dharmas—namely, their such-like marks, such-like natures, such-like substances, such-like powers, such-like functions, such-like causes, such-like conditions, such-like effects, such-like retributions, and such-like ultimate equality of beginning and end.
> —Lotus Sūtra, Skillful Means

There are three movements in this passage.

**The first movement—acknowledgment.** The Buddha first acknowledges that such a dharma exists: foremost, rarest, difficult to understand, that only a Buddha together with a Buddha can exhaustively fathom. He does not say, "I am now going to reveal all dharmas to you," but rather, "There is a dharma whose depth even Śāriputra—foremost in wisdom among the śrāvakas—cannot directly receive." This acknowledgment is already itself a doctrinal judgment: the true mark of all dharmas is not an object that can be captured by concepts, not a conclusion that can be reached by logical inference.

**The second movement—refusal.** "Stop, Śāriputra, there is no need to say more"—right after the acknowledgment, the Buddha refuses to explain. This refusal is not a withholding of the Dharma; it is a respect for the object itself. If the true mark of all dharmas could be stated directly, it would already have been lowered into some category that can be put into words; and the very reason it is foremost, rarest, and difficult to understand is that it overflows any single category of speech.

**The third movement—presentation.** Having refused to state it directly, the Buddha does not fall silent; instead, by means of ten "such-like" phrases—mark, nature, substance, power, function, cause, condition, effect, retribution, and the ultimate equality of beginning and end—he presents the ten facets of *how* the true mark of all dharmas shows itself. This is an exceedingly subtle teaching gesture: **stating it directly would lose the object's depth, but silence would lose the very possibility of teaching; the Buddha chooses a third road—not to define, only to display.**

The key to reading this passage is not to explain each of the ten suchnesses word by word, but first to see clearly the whole three-beat structure—**acknowledgment, refusal, presentation**—and the one thing it jointly points to: the true mark of all dharmas is not a question of "what is it," but a question of "how does it show itself."

### The Ten Suchnesses—The Ten-Facet Dynamics of the Dependent Nature

This is exactly why the ten suchnesses cannot be read as a static list of attributes.

Many readers, on first meeting these ten phrases, instinctively understand them as "the ten component parts of all dharmas"—mark is the outer look, nature is the inner, substance is the essence . . . This kind of reading lowers the ten suchnesses to a physics-style ontological classification, where each "such-like" becomes a static attribute label.

This is a common but consequential misreading.

The true structure of the ten suchnesses is the ten-facet dynamic unfolding of the **dependent nature** (*paratantra-svabhāva*, conditioned dharmas as an interdependent dynamic process). The ten "such-like" phrases are not ten attributes; they are the presentation of one and the same conditioned process under ten angles of observation:

| The ten suchnesses | Angle of observation |
|--------|---------|
| mark, nature, substance | what this conditioned dharma presently shows—its outer appearance, inner nature, and overall being |
| power, function | the potency this conditioned dharma holds and its actual operation |
| cause, condition, effect, retribution | the temporal structure of this conditioned dharma—primary cause, supporting condition, present-life effect, karmic maturation |
| ultimate equality of beginning and end | from the coarsest mark to the subtlest retribution, beginning and end are equal and not-two |

The final item—"ultimate equality of beginning and end"—is the gathering-up of the ten suchnesses, and also their subtlest point. The nine facets from mark to retribution look like a layered structure that runs from outer to inner, from coarse to subtle, from present to future; but at the end the Buddha adds the word "equality"—**these nine facets are, at the ultimate level, equal, none of them more real or more profound than any other.** The dependent nature, as conditioned dharma, has its outer appearance and inner nature, its present moment and its karmic maturation, its primary cause and its supporting condition all as different facets of one and the same dependently-arisen dynamic, with no hierarchy of master and servant.

This point is of the greatest importance for the parable teaching that follows. Why is the "three carts" of the burning house, that skillful provision, not a rung lower than the one Buddha-vehicle? Why is the prodigal son's deluded failure to recognize that he was always the elder's own son no more false than his later recognition of his father? Why is the rest taken in the phantom city no more secondary than the arrival at the treasure-place?—Because at the level of the dependent nature, the skillful and the ultimate, the misrecognition and the recognition, the phantom city and the treasure-place are all different facets of one and the same conditioned dharma, equal in beginning and end. **Skillful means is not a step on the way to the ultimate; skillful means is the ultimate displaying itself simultaneously on another facet.**

This is not an abstract philosophical stance; it is the operating premise of the entire Lotus Sūtra.

### The Dependent Nature as Pivot—Why the Lotus Can Teach Through Parables

The Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, in its Chapter on the Characteristics of All Dharmas, gives a crucial placement of the three natures:

> If bodhisattvas can, upon the dependent characteristic of all dharmas, truly know the imagined characteristic, they can then truly know all dharmas as markless . . . and by truly knowing the perfected characteristic, they can then truly know all dharmas as purified marks.

**The dependent nature is the starting point and the pivot of observation.** Setting out from the dependent nature, one can see two directions at once—at one end the imagined nature (mistaken grasping), at the other the perfected nature (the purified suchness). The dependent nature is not "the middle layer" among the three natures; it is the shared position upon which the three natures unfold.

This placement explains why the Lotus can teach through parables without any worry that the parables might be read as "not yet ultimate."

The parables—the burning house, the prodigal son, the medicinal herbs, the phantom city, the jewel in the robe, the jewel in the topknot—each one is a teaching manifestation at the level of the dependent nature. **Skillful means arises within the dependent nature and takes up the function of pulling sentient beings out of the imagined grasping and drawing them toward the perfected nature; when the function is complete, skillful means naturally reverts to its original place within the dependent nature**—neither grasped as substantially real (which would fall into the imagined), nor discarded as nothing (which would obscure the perfected).

A misunderstanding easily arises here: one might ask, "Then is the perfected nature not the ultimate? Why not just speak of the perfected nature directly, why take the detour through parables?" In fact, no. The perfected nature is not another level of existence standing apart from the dependent nature; it is **the perfected nature as it appears once the imagined grasping has been removed upon the dependent nature**—that is to say, the perfected nature is that face of the dependent nature which shows itself once the dependent nature has been correctly observed, not some thing located elsewhere. **To speak of the perfected nature directly while skipping the dynamics of the dependent nature would turn the perfected nature into yet another object of imagined grasping.** The Lotus's parables are not there to please the audience; they are there to protect the perfected nature from being turned into an object of imagined grasping.

⚠️ Another misunderstanding easily arises here too—reading "the true mark of all dharmas" as an independent metaphysical entity, something like the "thing-in-itself" of Western philosophy. This is incorrect. "The true mark of all dharmas" refers to the way in which all dharmas, as dependently-arisen dharmas, present themselves just as they are—not an independent entity, but a faithful pointing-out of the dependently-arisen nature of all dharmas. "Inherently so" does not mean "there inherently exists a thing that is so"; it means "all dharmas, from beginning to now, have operated in just this way, the way of dependent arising." To make it into a substance is to make the work of "gathering-in" fail—because what would then be gathered in is no longer the dynamics of the dependent nature but a static object.

### Three Registers—Three Expressions of One and the Same True Mark

Concerning the interpretive history of "the true mark of all dharmas," there is a rarely noted phenomenon worth pointing out briefly.

When the Indian Yogācāra master Vasubandhu composed his *Saddharmapuṇḍarīkopadeśa* to comment on the Lotus, **he used no trisvabhāva terminology at all**, unfolding the meaning of the one vehicle instead around the core of "dharma-body equality." This is a **devotional register**—not analyzing structure, but reverently praising equality.

When Jizang of the Chinese Sanlun school cited Vasubandhu in his *Commentary on the Meaning of the Lotus*, he read Vasubandhu's framework into the ontological language of "the substance of the tathāgatagarbha dharma-body." This is a step from the devotional register into an **ontological register**—"equality" read as "there is a substance that is equal."

By the Tang dynasty, the Faxiang master Kuiji completed the third layer in his *Profound Commentary on the Lotus*—analyzing the one vehicle into a trisvabhāva structure: "The dependent nature is bodhi, the perfected nature is nirvāṇa; together they form the one vehicle." This is an **analytical register**—using the precise terminology of Yogācāra to draw out the structure of the one vehicle.

These three masters are not contradicting one another; they are **three registers of expression of one and the same true mark of all dharmas.** Vasubandhu's devotional register appeals to the experiential face of the perfected nature, Jizang's ontological register to its existential face, Kuiji's analytical register to its face of realization. The dependent nature is the shared pivot of all three—at once the field of equality the devotional register reverently praises, the substance of the tathāgatagarbha the ontological register points toward, and the basis of transformation the analytical register locates.

This three-layer lineage is not an original discovery of this book—each layer is work already completed by the master in question. This book's contribution is only to set the three layers side by side, so the reader can see that, **faced with one and the same true mark of all dharmas, the different registers are complementary, not competing.** This is very helpful for reading the Lotus's parables later on—a parable operates simultaneously in all three registers: it is at once a devotional story (able to move people), an ontological pointing-out (with a real structure), and an analytical display (open to precise reading). All three registers are present at once in every single parable; remove one and it deforms.

(One reminder is also needed at this point: the teaching of the one vehicle does not mean that the Lotus stands doctrinally highest among the sūtras. Pointing-at-the-Moon's position is this—the one vehicle is the expression of the true mark of all dharmas in the particular register of the Lotus; the Avataṃsaka, the Prajñāpāramitā, the Śūraṅgama, and the Diamond each carry a different teaching function, none higher or lower than the others, only differing in sequence. "Opening the provisional to reveal the real" does not mean "the Lotus denies the value of the other sūtras." This must be kept continually in mind while reading the parables that follow.)

### A Modern Counterpart—and Its Limit

⚠️ Modern phenomenology has a methodological feature in its analysis of conscious phenomena: one and the same conscious phenomenon can be analyzed from several descriptive layers—the intentional object, the intentional act, the temporal structure of the stream of consciousness, the pre-intentional hyletic data, and so on—each layer being a description of a different facet of the same phenomenon, not a competing explanation that excludes the others. The ten suchnesses' ten-facet description of conditioned dharmas has a methodological analogy with this in structure.

But **the direction is reversed.** Phenomenology's multilayered description ultimately rests on transcendental subjectivity—it presupposes an observer in order to construct meaning; the ten suchnesses ultimately rest on dependently-arisen non-self—precisely in order to deconstruct the observer and so disclose the absence of own-nature. The former moves inward toward the subject; the latter reduces even the subject itself to dependent arising.

This analogy is only a structural hint, not theoretical support. One cannot say "the ten suchnesses are simply the Eastern version of phenomenology," nor "phenomenology proves the Buddhist ten suchnesses." There is a structurally isomorphic place, but in direction they are fundamentally opposed. Remembering this limit matters more than remembering the analogy itself.

### Why This Is the Cornerstone of the Whole Lotus

The ten suchnesses of the Skillful Means chapter appear only once in the entire sūtra, yet they bear the weight of the whole Lotus.

They give the Lotus three things.

**The first**: they give the true mark of all dharmas a complete description of **how it shows itself**—not "what it is," but "how it manifests." This keeps the one vehicle from degenerating into an abstract slogan, giving it instead ten facets that can be observed, that can be entered.

**The second**: they establish the position of **the dependent nature as pivot**—this is the structural premise upon which all the later teaching unfolds: the seven parables, the three kinds of prediction of buddhahood, the universal-gate responsiveness, the immeasurable original lifespan. Without the dependent nature as the middle position, skillful means is either grasped as ultimate or discarded as illusory, and the whole operation of the Lotus seizes up.

**The third**: they point out the **complementarity of the three registers**—giving the reader a map of registers, so that when one reads Vasubandhu's devotional passages one does not find them "not precise enough," when one reads Kuiji's analytical passages one does not find them "not moving enough," and when one reads Jizang's ontological passages one does not get stuck in the dispute over "whether or not the substance is a real entity." Each register carries its own portion of the work.

These three things are the whole of what the ten-suchnesses passage of the Skillful Means chapter does.

What the next chapter takes up is why the Buddha appears in the world at all, why he teaches all of this—that is, the structure of the "one great cause" and of "opening, showing, awakening, entering." The ten suchnesses are the **static display** of the true mark of all dharmas; the one great cause is the **dynamic activation** of the true mark of all dharmas. The former tells us how reality shows itself; the latter tells us how awakening happens.
