## 7.8 Devadatta and the Dragon Girl—The Prophecy's Non-Self-Existence Displayed at Both Ends

The garment-jewel of the previous chapter told us this—every sentient being carries a jewel, and every sentient being is the heir of an "aspiration toward all-knowing, still present and never lost."

But once this claim is taken seriously, it raises a question that is hard to sit with—**when we say "every," which "every" do we mean**?

Does it mean everyone? Those whom our society judges worthy? Or does it include even those whom our society rejects at the root?

The Devadatta Chapter answers this question head-on, with two stories. It does not argue in the abstract, and it does not wrap the point in parable. At the level of narrative, it shows it directly—the Buddha, before the assembly, grants the prophecy of buddhahood to two beings whom no tradition could ever have declared to bear the buddha-seed.

One is **Devadatta**—Śākyamuni's cousin, the one who split the saṅgha, drew the Buddha's blood, sent the elephant to kill him, the very Devadatta whom the Buddha pronounced with his own mouth to be "fallen into hell while still living, incurable for an entire kalpa."

The other is **the dragon girl**—eight years old, of the animal realm, non-human, not male.

These two prophecies are the two boldest strokes in the whole of the Lotus Sūtra. If they hold, then the universality of the garment-jewel has been pushed to its limit—**no kind of sentient being can be excluded from what is originally present**. If they do not hold, then the "aspiration toward all-knowing, still present and never lost" carries hidden fine print, and the Lotus is not really gathering the three into the one.

The Lotus chose the first road. And it did not choose it lightly—it chose it with enormous weight, so much weight that this chapter has been debated, contested, and explained away in countless ways throughout the history of the Chinese tradition.

What this chapter sets out to show is how the Lotus holds the weight of these two prophecies without letting them collapse into two separate miracles.

### Act One—Devadatta's Devarāja Tathāgata

The story begins when the Buddha suddenly tells the assembly of an event from the past.

He says that immeasurable kalpas ago, he was a king. In his search for the Dharma, he was willing to give up his throne, give up everything; to anyone who could teach him the Great Vehicle, he was willing to become a servant. At that time a seer named **Asita** (*Ṛṣi Asita*) came before the king and said that he possessed a scripture—the *Lotus Sūtra*—and that if the king were truly willing to make offerings and never disobey, he would transmit it to him.

The king served the seer for a thousand years as agreed. He gathered fruit, drew water, collected firewood, prepared food, and even "**made his own body into a couch and seat**"—used his own body as the seer's bed. Over those thousand years, the king did indeed receive and uphold the *Lotus Sūtra* from the seer.

Then the Buddha said a sentence that stunned the assembly:

> The king of that time was none other than myself. The seer of that time is the Devadatta of today.

That king was me. That seer is the Devadatta of the present.

This recollection instantly rewrites the timeline of the whole narrative. In this lifetime, Devadatta is the evil man who split the saṅgha and harmed the Buddha and the community. But in the remote past, he was the great benefactor who transmitted the Dharma to Śākyamuni's former life—without him, the Buddha would not have reached what he reached today.

Then the Buddha gave the prophecy:

> It is by reason of Devadatta, my spiritual friend, that I have come to be fully endowed with the six perfections, with loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity, with the thirty-two marks and the eighty minor signs, with the deep-gold complexion, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the four means of gathering-in, the eighteen distinctive qualities, and the powers of spiritual penetration; that I have attained perfect awakening and widely delivered sentient beings—all of this is by reason of Devadatta, my spiritual friend.

It is because of this spiritual friend, Devadatta, that I have come to possess all the qualities of a buddha. And then:

> I declare to the fourfold assembly: Devadatta, after immeasurable kalpas have passed, shall attain buddhahood, named Devarāja Tathāgata, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly Awakened, Accomplished in Wisdom and Conduct, Well-Gone, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed, Tamer of Beings, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, World-Honored One.

He tells everyone: immeasurable kalpas in the future, Devadatta will attain buddhahood, named "Devarāja Tathāgata," fully endowed with the ten epithets of a tathāgata. His buddha-land is called "Heavenly Path," the aeon is named "Heavenly King," and his Dharma endures for twenty intermediate kalpas—the complete nine-field format, grammatically identical to the prophecies received by Śāriputra, Kāśyapa, and Maudgalyāyana.

The force of this narrative needs to be read slowly.

The Buddha does not say "Devadatta did not really do those things." From beginning to end, the text nowhere denies his five heinous deeds. He did split the saṅgha; he did draw the Buddha's blood; he did send the elephant to harm the Buddha. These deeds bear their fruit faithfully at the level of manifestation—he will indeed fall into hell.

And yet, within the same narrative, the Buddha says he will become a buddha in the future.

**How is this possible**?

This is the sharpest question the whole Lotus puts to its reader. It is not a philosophical game—it lays open a scene that looks logically self-contradictory and asks the reader to sit there and think it through: **can the reality of karmic retribution and the possibility of buddhahood both hold at the same time**?

### Act Two—The Dragon Girl's Buddhahood in the Present Body

Right after Devadatta's prophecy, the text cuts to another scene—the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī rises up from the depths of the ocean and reports on the fruits of his teaching there. The bodhisattva Prajñākūṭa asks him: are there beings in the ocean who, receiving this scripture, swiftly attain buddhahood? Mañjuśrī says there are, many of them—above all, the daughter of the dragon king Sāgara.

Then the dragon girl herself appears. The text describes her very precisely: "**just eight years old**, of keen and penetrating wisdom, well knowing the faculties and karmic conduct of all beings."

Eight years old.

Śāriputra is present, and he immediately raises an objection. His words are preserved in full in the text, and they carry tremendous weight:

> You say she will soon attain the unsurpassed Way; this is hard to believe. Why? A woman's body is defiled and impure, not a vessel for the Dharma. How could she attain unsurpassed awakening? The Buddha-way is vast and far; only after immeasurable kalpas of diligent toil and accumulated practice, fully cultivating all the perfections, is it at last attained. Moreover, a woman's body still bears five obstructions: she cannot become, first, a Brahmā king; second, Śakra; third, a Māra king; fourth, a wheel-turning sage king; fifth, a buddha.

Śāriputra is foremost in wisdom among the śrāvakas. What he says is not personal prejudice; it is the verdict of the entire sectarian Buddhist tradition he represents concerning a woman's buddhahood—"a woman's body is defiled and impure, not a vessel for the Dharma," "the five obstructions"—these few lines very nearly sum up the whole consensus of Indian sectarian Buddhism on the soteriological standing of women.

The dragon girl does not argue with Śāriputra. She does one thing—**she offers the Buddha her most precious jewel**, worth the entire trichiliocosm. The Buddha accepts it. Then she turns to Prajñākūṭa and Śāriputra and says:

> I offered the jewel, the World-Honored One received it—was this quick or not?

I offered the jewel, the Buddha accepted it; was that fast?

The two answer: very fast.

She says:

> By your spiritual power, watch me attain buddhahood—faster even than this.

With your spiritual power, watch: my attaining buddhahood is faster even than that.

The next line of the text reads:

> At that moment the whole assembly saw the dragon girl, in an instant, transform into a male, complete in bodhisattva conduct, and at once go to the Spotless World in the south, sit upon a jeweled lotus, and attain perfect awakening, with the thirty-two marks and the eighty minor signs, expounding the wondrous Dharma everywhere for all beings in the ten directions.

The whole assembly saw it with their own eyes—the dragon girl, in an instant, transformed into a male, complete in bodhisattva conduct, flew at once to the Spotless World in the south, sat upon a jeweled lotus, and became a buddha. The thirty-two marks, the eighty minor signs, the expounding of the wondrous Dharma.

The whole process took a single instant.

Then the text records the response of the entire assembly:

> The bodhisattvas, śrāvakas, the eightfold company of gods and dragons, humans and non-humans of the Sahā world all saw, from afar, that dragon girl become a buddha and expound the Dharma everywhere for the gods and humans of that assembly; their hearts rejoiced greatly, and from afar they all paid her reverent homage.

Looking across from this Sahā world, the dragon girl over in that world is already a buddha, expounding the Dharma for the beings of that world.

---

The force of these two acts together is the sharpest point in the entire Lotus Sūtra. The first act uses the **most evil being** to stretch open one end of the prophecy; the second uses the **weakest being** to stretch open the other end. Neither is an extreme in the ordinary sense—both are absolute boundaries that the Vinaya and tradition explicitly ruled out.

### The Vinaya's Three Layers of Sealing

Let us lay out the dragon girl's situation. In the Vinaya she is sealed out on **three layers at once**.

**First layer: a dragon (animal realm, non-human).** The *Sarvāstivāda-vinaya* records an incident—a dragon took human form and slipped into the saṅgha to receive ordination; later, when his identity was exposed, the Buddha laid down an explicit rule that thenceforth non-humans could not receive full ordination. The animal realm and the non-human are, in the Vinaya, **excluded from the saṅgha at the root**. The dragon girl, as a dragon, cannot even cross the threshold of the saṅgha.

**Second layer: a woman's body.** The sectarian Vinaya acknowledges that a woman can receive ordination, practice the path, and realize arhatship—the order of nuns was established by the Buddha himself. But the verdict "a woman's body cannot become a buddha" was very nearly a universal claim in the sectarian Vinaya texts and Abhidharma treatises. The "five obstructions" that Śāriputra cites are the teaching version of this claim—a woman's body cannot occupy these five positions (Brahmā king, Śakra, Māra king, wheel-turning sage king, buddha).

**Third layer: eight years old.** The Vinaya explicitly requires that one be at least twenty years of age to receive full ordination. An eight-year-old **does not even meet the formal preconditions for ordination**, let alone for buddhahood.

These are three independent layers of sealing, and each one alone is enough to deny the dragon girl's qualification for buddhahood. Taken together, the dragon girl is, within the framework of the Vinaya, a **completely impossible** being—she cannot leave home, cannot receive ordination, cannot become a buddha, cannot even wait until twenty to consider it.

Then the Lotus has her, in a single narrative, **cross all three layers of sealing at once**.

The density of this narrative pressure is not literary drama. It is structural. The Lotus chose a being impossible along every dimension of the Vinaya and had her attain buddhahood in the present body before everyone's eyes—not in order to stage an astonishing spectacle, but to **show that the universality of what is originally present can pierce through any dimension of the Vinaya's sealing**.

These Vinaya regulations had their legitimacy and function in the sectarian period—there is no intent here to disparage the Vinaya. But one structural fact must be pointed out: **the more severely the Vinaya seals the dragon girl out, the more sharply the unconditionality of the Lotus's prophecy is thrown into relief by the contrast**. Had the Lotus chosen a being only mildly disallowed by the Vinaya, the force of the display would be weak; instead, the Lotus chose precisely the kind of being the Vinaya sealed out most severely—dragon, woman, eight years old—three layers sealed at once, and then, in a single instant, all of them opened.

This is the true meaning of "the weak pole" in "both ends displayed at once."

### A Text-Critical Footnote

Let me note one thing in passing here.

Readers familiar with Lotus scholarship may have heard a claim like this—that the Devadatta Chapter is a later interpolation, an apocryphal addition, and not reliable. At the documentary level this claim is half right and half misleading.

The right half: the "Devadatta Chapter 12" found in the extant Kumārajīva recension is indeed not part of his original 406 CE translation; it was supplemented in around 480 CE by the monks Faxian and Fayi.

The misleading half: this "supplementation" has been read as "adding new content where none previously existed"—when in fact the opposite is true.

Dharmarakṣa's *Zhèng-fǎ-huá-jīng* (286 CE, 120 years earlier than the Kumārajīva recension) **already contains both the Devadatta prophecy and the dragon girl's buddhahood**, joined within the Stūpa Chapter, not set apart as an independent chapter. The *Tiān-pǐn miào-fǎ-lián-huá-jīng* of Jñānagupta and Dharmagupta (605 CE) explicitly records in its preface that the Kumārajīva recension had omitted this material, and so they supplemented it back in—but they still followed Dharmarakṣa's layout, **joining the Devadatta and dragon-girl passages at the end of the Stūpa Chapter, without setting them apart as an independent chapter**.

Three independent documentary witnesses—Dharmarakṣa's recension, the supplemented recension, and the record of Tiantai's Guanding—form an iron triangle: **the original was a single unified whole; the disaggregation in the Kumārajīva recension is the later editorial event**.

This text-critical recovery is not for the sake of correcting a historical detail; it has a direct bearing on how the chapter is read: **the Devadatta prophecy and the dragon girl's buddhahood have always been two symmetrical passages within a single chapter**. They are not two separate miracles; they are the **symmetrical twin wings of one and the same prophecy-event**—a single structure displaying its two ends.

Once this is read clearly, the question shifts from "why are there two extreme cases" to "**why does this structure require both ends to be displayed at once**."

### 95% Continuity, a 5% Break-Point

To answer this "why both ends at once," we first need to look at one thing—relative to the prophecy tradition of the Āgama period, **how much of the Lotus's prophecy grammar is actually innovation**?

The answer may be surprising. Within 95% of its range, the Lotus's prophecy grammar is a **direct continuation** of the Āgama prophecy grammar.

**The format of the prophecy**—the nine-field structure (buddha-name, clan, father's name, mother's name, buddha-land, life-span, assembly, disciples, Dharma-duration), the complete ten-epithet formula (Tathāgata, Worthy of Offerings, Perfectly Awakened…)—all of this is inherited from the *Dīrghāgama · Mahāpadāna-sūtra* and the *Madhyamāgama · Origin-Account Sūtra*. The Lotus's prophecies of Śāriputra and Kāśyapa correspond word for word, in format, to the Āgama's Maitreya prophecy.

**The range of recipients**—the *Ekottarikāgama · Aśva-rakta Devaputra Chapter* records that when the buddha Ratnagarbha granted prophecies by "eight-colored radiance," the beings into whom the light entered from different parts of his body included those of the animal realm and the hells—**the recipients of prophecy already spanned the six realms**. The dragon girl, as a being of the animal realm receiving a prophecy, is not a rootless innovation within Āgama grammar.

**The prophecy to a woman's body**—the *Ekottarikāgama* contains a story in which the buddha Ratnagarbha first refuses, then permits, a woman, deferring the prophecy to a future life after she has transformed into a male body. The dragon girl's "transformation into a male" and "going to the Spotless World in the south to become a buddha" follow exactly the Āgama's twofold procedure of "transform into a male + attain buddhahood in another land." The Lotus's only compression is to **contract the deferred prophecy into a prophecy in the present body**: the timing contracts from "a future life" to "the space of an instant," the space from "a future world" to "the south, immediately."

Together these cover roughly 95% of the Lotus's prophecy grammar—a continuation of the Āgama tradition, only dramatized and magnified in its scales of time and space.

So what about the remaining 5%?

The remaining 5% is a single break-point: **the "incurable"-type negative prognosis is reversed**.

The Āgama's prophecy grammar, broad as its scope is, is explicitly sealed in one place—**Devadatta himself**. In the *Ekottarikāgama · Gain Chapter*, Śākyamuni's judgment on Devadatta is "**incurable, one kalpa in hell**," "not a hair's breadth of good is seen." This is not a vague reproach; it is a structured **negative prognosis**—the being is explicitly and ultimately excluded from any possibility of future buddhahood.

The Āgama Śākyamuni's field of vision regarding Devadatta extends **only to the timeline of Śākyamuni in this life**. Within that field of vision, Devadatta has no roots of good that could serve as the causal basis for a prophecy.

The Lotus's Devadatta Chapter breaks precisely this single break-point.

But the way it breaks it is crucial—**the Lotus does not deny the Āgama's negative prognosis**. It does not say "Devadatta did not really commit those evil deeds," nor does it say "the five heinous karmas can be erased." What the Lotus does is **change the direction of the timeline**—when Śākyamuni recalls the remote original time, Devadatta was, in a former life, the seer Asita, the great benefactor who transmitted the Dharma to Śākyamuni's former self; and on the basis of that former-life cause, he is prophesied to become "Devarāja Tathāgata" in the future.

The reversal of the timeline's direction is the **one genuine innovation** of the Lotus's prophecy grammar relative to the Āgama's. The Āgama prophecy is a "future forecast" (looking forward); the prophecy of the Lotus's Devadatta Chapter is a "**reversal in the remote original time**"—looking backward first, then forward.

This grammatical innovation cannot be derived from within the Āgama. It depends on the Lotus's fundamental-gate vision of time—and the Lotus's fundamental-gate vision of time only unfolds fully in the *Lifespan of the Tathāgata* chapter (Chapter 16, the subject of the chapter after next).

---

Putting these two things together, the argumentative weight carried by the two wings of the Devadatta Chapter becomes clear:

**The dragon-girl wing is a change of quantity**—a temporal-spatial compression of something the Āgama already permitted: from deferred to present-body, from another land to immediate, from transformation into a male in a future life to transformation into a male in a single instant. The Āgama grammar already permitted it; the Lotus merely pulled the timing taut to its limit.

**The Devadatta wing is a change of quality**—a directional reversal of something the Āgama explicitly sealed. The Āgama grammar did not permit it; the Lotus, with the vision of time from the remote original time, opened a dimension the Āgama had never put to use.

The former is a change of quantity, the latter a change of quality.

And what displays these two wings **simultaneously, as a symmetrical structure**, is precisely the Lotus. Not placed side by side at random, but a carefully designed symmetry—one wing using the weak ones sealed out on three Vinaya layers (dragon, woman, eight years old) to stretch open the limit of bodily form; the other wing using the evil one explicitly condemned by the Vinaya (five heinous deeds, hell, not a hair's breadth of good) to stretch open the limit of karmic fruit. Only when both poles hold at once is the **complete spectrum of prophecy** constituted.

### The Two Wings Belong to Two Tracks—The First Confluence of the Tathāgatagarbha Track and the Yogācāra Track

Now we can take up a deeper question—why do the two wings carry asymmetric argumentative weight?

The answer is: **because the two wings belong to two different theoretical tracks**.

**The dragon-girl wing is carried by the Yogācāra track.**

From the Yogācāra standpoint, the dragon girl's buddhahood in the present body is no miracle; it is the **inevitable outcome of uncontaminated seeds that have long since matured and await only their final instant of actualization**. Kuiji's *Fǎ-huá-xuán-zàn* handles it explicitly this way—the dragon girl is already a high-stage bodhisattva (a tenth-stage bodhisattva one lifetime from buddhahood), and her manifesting as a dragon, as a female, as an eight-year-old is a pedagogical display, not literally true ("**it is not literally so**"). Buddhahood in a single instant is simply the standard derivation of seed theory: seeds fully present + conditions present + time compressed to the utmost = instantaneous actualization.

This reading is fully self-consistent within the Yogācāra system. Temporal-spatial compression is a change of quantity, and a change of quantity has a definite structural place in seed theory—when the four conditions are all present (causal condition, immediately-antecedent condition, objective-support condition, contributing condition), actualization is by its nature the work of a single instant. The dragon girl's "eight years old, female body, dragon form" is the manifested setting of her bodily form; the uncontaminated seeds in her ālaya-vijñāna are the real her; at the level of seeds, she differs in no way from any high-stage bodhisattva.

**The Devadatta wing cannot be resolved by the Yogācāra track alone.**

The five heinous karmas cannot be erased at the manifestation level—this is something Kuiji himself concedes: "because of his injurious actions, he has sunk in the present" (because of harming the Buddha and the saṅgha, he will indeed sink into hell in the near term). Hell must be undergone, the karma must bear fruit, the manifest karma-fruit system runs as usual. So how is buddhahood possible?

The Yogācāra answer is "the uncontaminated seed-system at the seed level runs as usual"—but this is not enough. The question is not "are the seeds still there"; the question is **on what grounds he can be prophesied now**. For one whose five heinous karmas have not yet run their course, his roots of good should still be deeply covered by karmic obstruction; even if the seeds are still there, they are in a "subtle, hidden, hard to know" state, not a "fit to bear a prophecy" state.

The Devadatta wing needs support from another level—**the equality of the dharmakāya**.

The equality of the dharmakāya is the core thesis of the tathāgatagarbha tradition: all sentient beings originally possess the tathāgatagarbha nature, and this original possession is unaffected by any external condition. The five heinous karmas are a fact at the manifestation level, but at the level of the dharmakāya's equality, Devadatta, Śākyamuni, and any buddha **differ in no way** in this matter of original possession. What the prophecy states is not "Devadatta's karma has been erased," but "**Devadatta's dharmakāya has always been equal to Śākyamuni's**."

This level only the tathāgatagarbha tradition can provide. The *Mahāparinirvāṇa-sūtra*, in its twentieth fascicle, delivers an explicit parallel reversal upon Devadatta—"Devadatta is no *icchantika*, nor a śrāvaka, nor a pratyekabuddha … but is solely an object known and seen by the buddhas"—using not seed theory but the tathāgatagarbha doctrine of buddha-nature. The *Mahāparinirvāṇa* and the Lotus reach the same conclusion on the Devadatta question independently, by different routes: **Devadatta can be reversed, not because his karma is gone, but because the dharmakāya has never been made unequal by karma**.

So we can see the theoretical structure of the Devadatta Chapter:

| Wing | Being | Argumentative Weight | Track | Language |
|------|------|---------|---------|------|
| Dragon-girl wing | The weakest (dragon, woman, eight years old) | Change of quantity (temporal-spatial compression) | Yogācāra track | Seed-statement |
| Devadatta wing | The most evil (five heinous deeds, hell) | Change of quality (directional reversal) | Tathāgatagarbha track | dharma-body-equality |

The two wings belong to two tracks. The Yogācāra track carries "seed-perfuming, the gradual actualization of manifest states, the pragmatic mechanism of prophecy-as-statement"; the tathāgatagarbha track carries "dharmakāya-equality, originally-already-buddhahood, the metaphysical ground of the reversal." **The Lotus is the first confluence of these two tracks in the history of Mahāyāna Buddhism**—within a single narrative event, two independent theoretical systems each carry one wing and together constitute the complete structure of prophecy theory.

This is also why the two wings of this chapter were a single unit at the documentary level to begin with—not an editorial accident, but **an inner requirement of the theoretical structure**. Lacking either wing, the complete spectrum of prophecy does not hold; only with both wings displayed at once is the spectrum truly opened.

### Prophecy ≠ Prediction

Having come this far, there is one easiest misreading that must be cut off clearly—**reading prophecy as prediction**.

A modern reader encountering prophecy for the first time easily likens it to an oracle or augury—so-and-so will become a buddha at such a time, in such a place, just as so-and-so will win the lottery or such an event will occur. If this reading held, then prophecy would be determinism, and it would lead to a nihilistic inference: **since the prophecy has already been granted, why practice at all**?

In his *Treatise on the Lotus Sūtra*, Vasubandhu made a decisive incision against this misreading. In his three-layer definition of prophecy, the third point states it most plainly:

> A prophecy is granted because of dharmakāya-equality; **it does not mean the practice-merits are already complete**.

A prophecy is granted because of dharmakāya-equality—it does not mean that the recipient already possesses the completed merits of practice.

This sentence seals off the entire structure of "prophecy = prediction." What a prophecy states is not "this person will necessarily reach a certain result in the future"—it states the present fact that "**this person's dharmakāya is equal to that of all buddhas**." A statement of present fact is not a prediction of future necessity. The recipient's merits must still be cultivated by the recipient.

In modern language—prophecy is a **statement** in the pragmatic sense, not a prediction in the cognitive sense. What it states is dharmakāya-equality, the originally-present seeds, a structural fact that holds in the present moment. It does not state a timetable of future events.

This incision is extremely important. For if prophecy were prediction, then Devadatta's "Devarāja Tathāgata" prophecy would amount to saying that **his present five heinous karmas will be mysteriously erased**, that he will become a buddha at some point in time—and this would immediately invite the misreading that "prophecy can exempt one from karmic retribution." But Vasubandhu explicitly says no. The karma bears fruit as usual, hell is undergone as usual. The prophecy merely states that "**at the level of the dharmakāya, nothing has been interrupted by the five heinous karmas**"; it does not state that "at the level of karmic fruit, anything will be cancelled by the prophecy."

---

⚠️ Following directly from this, there is another prophecy-related misreading to correct—**prophecy ≠ exemption from karmic retribution**.

The Āgama has a case that handles this point most thoroughly—the story of Aṅgulimāla (*Aṅgulimāla*). Aṅgulimāla killed ninety-nine people; later he met the Buddha, left home, practiced diligently, and realized arhatship—the highest fruit of liberation. But after realizing arhatship, he was still beaten by people on account of his past killing-karma. The Buddha told him with his own mouth:

> Endure it now. Why? Because this offense should by rights be undergone through aeons without end.

You must endure it now. Because this offense should by rights be undergone over immeasurable kalpas.

If even the fruit of arhatship cannot exempt one from karmic retribution—**liberation does not dissolve karma**—then prophecy certainly cannot. Devadatta's prophecy as Devarāja Tathāgata does not mean he will not fall into hell; he will fall into hell, and after that, in the remote future, he will become Devarāja Tathāgata. Both things are true. The manifest karma-fruit system runs independently, and the uncontaminated seed-system at the seed level also runs independently; both structures hold at once without cancelling each other.

---

⚠️ There are two more misreadings related to this chapter to correct.

**The dragon girl transforming into a male ≠ a gender-ontological claim.**

On the surface, the dragon girl "transforming into a male" before becoming a buddha seems to say that a woman's body cannot become a buddha. But this reading contradicts the Āgama itself—the *Saṃyuktāgama*, fascicle 45, records the story of the nun Somā: when Māra came to provoke her, saying a woman's wisdom is shallow and she cannot realize the Way, she answered on the spot:

> When the mind has entered right concentration, what does a woman's form matter?

The mind has already entered concentration; what has a woman's body to do with it?

The alternate-translation *Saṃyuktāgama* 215 is even more direct: "If there are no marks of male and female, how can discrimination arise?" From the Āgama period, the liberation experiences of female nuns already pointed clearly to one thing—**bodily sex has nothing to do with awakening**.

So how should the dragon girl's "transformation into a male" be read? Tiantai's Zhiyi judged this passage to be "**the samādhi of universally manifesting bodily form, the power of expedient skill**"—not an ontological transformation of sex, but a formal adjustment a bodhisattva makes in displaying herself, in order to reduce the audience's doubts. Kuiji, from the Yogācāra standpoint, says the dragon girl is originally a bodhisattva one lifetime from buddhahood, and her manifesting as a dragon, as a female, as an eight-year-old are all manifestations; her final change into a male is also a manifestation—no bodily form is her "true body."

This reading converges across three traditions—Āgama, Tiantai, and Yogācāra: **the transformation-into-male passage is not a gender-ontological claim, but a form of pedagogical display**. It is a linguistic concession to the listeners of that time (above all to a representative of the śrāvaka tradition like Śāriputra), not a universal sealing against a woman's buddhahood.

**Devadatta's reversal ≠ no distinction between good and evil.**

Since Devadatta can be reversed and prophesied, does this mean there is really no difference between good and evil? Can moral judgment be abolished in the Mahāyāna?

No. The reversal occurs at the **dharmakāya perspective** (remote original time + dharmakāya-equality), not at the **karma-fruit perspective**. At the karma-fruit perspective, the five heinous karmas bear their fruit as usual; Devadatta, at the manifestation level, falls into hell and suffers as usual. The Lotus does not negate the karma-fruit system; rather, beyond the karma-fruit system, it opens **a deeper level**—a level at which the dharmakāya-equality of "no enmity" holds.

The two-layer structure holds at once; this is not the absence of any distinction between good and evil. It is **a deeper level of equality above the distinction between good and evil**. To read the reversal as the abolition of good and evil is to compress the two-layer structure into a single layer—betraying the Vinaya, betraying Vasubandhu, and betraying the structure of the Lotus itself.

### Why "Both Ends Displayed at Once" Rather Than "Limit-Case Prophecy"

The title of this chapter uses "the prophecy's non-self-existence displayed at both ends," rather than the more intuitive "limit-case prophecy."

This wording is a deliberate choice. There are two reasons.

**First**, the word "limit" implies some measurable scale—where there is a "limit" there is "short of the limit," where there is a furthest point there is a near point relative to it. This raises a dangerous question: does that furthest point have its own "limit-ness"? If it does, then prophecy is ontologized—prophecy becomes a thing with self-nature, which can be ranked, compared, and used to establish some hierarchy.

Jizang of the Three-Treatise school warns against precisely this. On the dragon girl's instantaneous buddhahood he deploys Madhyamaka's standard **four-cornered negation**—slow, swift, both slow and swift, neither slow nor swift—using the four corners to dissolve entirely the status of "swift buddhahood" as a proposition with self-nature. Under the four corners, the dragon girl's instantaneous buddhahood and a ten-kalpa sitting at the seat of awakening are both merely expedient, with no higher or lower, no self-nature. By the same token, the "male-and-female four corners" dissolves entirely any ontological difference in changing into a male or female body.

Jizang's treatment of Devadatta is equally brief: "**Devadatta, not evil, displays evil; hence evil is the expedient and good is the real.**" The entire event is absorbed completely by the single phrase "expedient without fixity, adaptation without direction."

This Madhyamaka warning must be taken seriously. If we describe this chapter with "limit-case prophecy," we risk ontologizing the prophecy—prophecy becomes something that can be ranked, an entity with self-nature, an item that can be compared with other prophecies. This violates the Lotus's foundational position of "expedient without fixity."

**Second**, "both ends displayed at once" is a purely structural description that presupposes no ontological scale. It says only this—**a single prophecy-event has two symmetrical display-faces**. The face of evil and the face of weakness, belonging to two theoretical tracks, converge symmetrically in this event.

"Both ends displayed at once" cannot be taken apart by the four corners in a Madhyamaka context—because it is itself a structural statement, presupposing no self-nature for either end, and presupposing no ontological hierarchy between "the ends" and "the middle." It points out only this: what this chapter displays is two theoretical load-bearing faces of the concept of prophecy, present at once.

This revision of wording looks subtle, but it is this chapter's concrete response to Jizang's Madhyamaka warning. **Structure can be displayed; self-nature must not be presupposed.**

Having passed through the Devadatta Chapter, we now have an extremely strong proposition—**what is originally present really is universal**. No kind of sentient being can be excluded: whether the most karmically burdened, the most sealed-out by the Vinaya in bodily form, or the one who violates three layers of tradition at once, what is originally present is there.

But one question has not yet been addressed—**how does this recognition become action**?

To know that every sentient being has a jewel within the garment is one thing. To actually treat every person one meets in daily life with this recognition—including those who annoy you, those who look down on you, those who seem as if they will never practice no matter what—is another.

The next chapter takes up a most unusual bodhisattva in the Lotus Sūtra—his method of practice was only one thing, but this one thing he did for a very, very long time. To anyone he met—whether monk, nun, layman, laywoman, or a stranger he did not know—he would stand by the roadside, bow to them, and say: "**I deeply revere you all and dare not slight you. Why? Because you all walk the bodhisattva path and shall attain buddhahood.**"

For this he had stones thrown at him, was cursed, was struck with staves. But he still stood at a distance and bowed, still said the same words.

This bodhisattva's name—given to him by the very people he bowed to—was **Never-Disparaging**.

What the next chapter takes up is the transformation from the doctrinal proposition "what is originally present is universal" to the practice-gate "revere all sentient beings."
