## 7.12 From Star to Black Hole — The Lotus's Gathering and a Foretaste of the Avataṃsaka's Manifestation

We have come this far, and Part VII is nearly at its close.

Beginning with the *Skilful Means* chapter, we have walked through the four great parables, through the robe-jewel and the topknot-jewel, through Devadatta and the Nāga Princess, through Never-Disparaging, through Avalokiteśvara, all the way to the immeasurable lifespan of the *Lifespan* chapter—**the doctrinal work the Lotus Sūtra had to do was already finished in the previous chapter**.

This chapter sets up no new doctrine.

It has only two things to do—**to gather in** and **to hand on the baton**.

To draw the threads of the eleven preceding chapters, each unfolded on its own, into one final structure; and then to hand you, who have read Part VII to this point, over to the road the next part will walk—the road of the Avataṃsaka.

### How the Lotus Brings Itself to a Close

The structure of the Lotus Sūtra is fascinating. It does not simply end after the *Lifespan* chapter—even though the *Lifespan* chapter is the doctrinal summit of the whole sūtra. After the *Lifespan* chapter, the text spends more than a dozen further chapters on a single matter: **how the Lotus hands itself over**.

This stretch is called the **transmission division**. The work of the transmission division is not to expound new doctrine; it is to perform a series of structural acts that **let the Lotus circulate, continue, and reach into the future**.

At the heart of the transmission division stand four chapters—*Stūpa*, *Tathāgata's Supernatural Powers*, *Entrustment*, and *Encouragements of Samantabhadra*. Add the *Prologue* chapter at the very front, forming a closing clasp of head and tail. These five anchors make up the five acts by which the Lotus draws itself to a close.

**The first act—the *Stūpa* chapter. A past Buddha and the present Buddha, both in the assembly at once.**

When Chapter 6 (the Conjured City) handled the stūpa, it took the longitudinal, temporal face: the remoteness of the Buddha Great-Penetrating-Wisdom-Excellence. This chapter looks at the lateral, structural face of the stūpa.

Prabhūtaratna (Many-Treasures) Tathāgata had entered parinirvāṇa immeasurable aeons ago, yet his whole body-relic remains intact within the stūpa, undispersed. As Śākyamuni preaches the Lotus in the present, the jewelled stūpa of Many-Treasures rises up from the earth, opens out in mid-air, and **Many-Treasures Buddha, from within the stūpa, invites Śākyamuni to enter, dividing half his seat to sit beside him**.

This act is itself a structural declaration—within the temporality of the Lotus, a past Buddha is not "a Buddha who has ended" but "a Buddha simultaneously present in another dimension." The structure of co-presence within the stūpa lays the structural ground for the eternal realization of the *Lifespan* chapter.

**The second act—the *Tathāgata's Supernatural Powers* chapter. The Lotus's self-positioning.**

As the preaching of the Lotus draws toward its close, Śākyamuni manifests the broad-long-tongue mark and emits light from between his brows. Then he himself speaks a line of the greatest weight:

> To state it concisely, **all the dharmas possessed by the Tathāgata, all his sovereign supernatural powers, all his secret essential treasury, all his most profound matters—all are revealed and openly declared in this sūtra**.

This line looks like a claim that "the Lotus is highest"—but it is not a statement of rank; it is a **functional self-positioning**.

The key to "all are revealed and openly declared in this sūtra" lies in the two words **openly declared**. The Lotus positions itself as a sūtra that **declares itself completely and openly to human beings**—not "the highest sūtra," but "**the sūtra that speaks most completely to human beings**."

The function of a star is precisely this: to send its light steadily and receivably in every direction. The Lotus's "open declaration" is exactly this function. And this function is irreplaceable—without it, the Dharma could not be received by the human conceptual mind at all.

**The third act—the *Entrustment* chapter. Stroking the heads, entrusting the Dharma.**

The *Entrustment* chapter is the centre of gravity of the transmission division. Śākyamuni rises from his Dharma-seat and, **with his right hand, strokes the heads of innumerable bodhisattva-mahāsattvas**. Three times he does this, and three times he entrusts in these words:

> For innumerable hundreds of thousands of myriads of *koṭis* of *asaṃkhyeya kalpas* I have cultivated this hard-to-attain Dharma of supreme, perfect awakening; now I entrust it to you. You should single-mindedly spread this Dharma abroad, causing it to increase widely.

The supreme, perfect awakening I have cultivated across countless aeons—this Dharma I now hand over to you. You are to spread this sūtra single-mindedly, and let it grow in the world.

"Stroking the heads and entrusting" is the most physically embodied appearance of the whole **character "gathering"** in Part VII. From beginning to end, Part VII has been handling "gathering"—drawing all sentient beings, all capacities, all skilful means into the one Buddha-vehicle. And this work of gathering, carried to its very end, becomes one utterly concrete bodily act—**the Buddha's hand actually laid upon each bodhisattva's head**.

Gathering is not abstract doctrine; it is contact at the level of the body. It is not "I hand over the *Lotus Sūtra* as a text," but "**I hand over the continuum of this Dharma, person by person, into your hands**."

Notice the rhetoric of the text—the object of "stroking the heads and entrusting" is "**innumerable bodhisattva-mahāsattvas**," but the act is **one by one**. This structure of "innumerable × one-by-one" is itself the defining mark of stellar radiation: light shines upon innumerable beings at once, yet what each being receives is "**the single ray of light sent for that one being alone**." Here the Lotus's gathering structure reaches its highest density.

**The fourth act—the *Encouragements of Samantabhadra* chapter. Samantabhadra takes the stage.**

The Lotus's final chapter is *Encouragements of Samantabhadra*. Samantabhadra Bodhisattva comes from the eastern Buddha-land of Ratnatejābhyudgatarāja Tathāgata, riding a six-tusked white elephant, enters the Sahā world, and arrives before Śākyamuni. He asks Śākyamuni:

> World-Honoured One! After the Tathāgata's parinirvāṇa, how can good men and good women obtain this *Lotus Sūtra*?

After the Tathāgata's passing, how are good men and good women to obtain this *Lotus Sūtra*?

Śākyamuni answers with four dharmas, and Samantabhadra takes up the role of guardian of the Dharma—"I shall guard them, removing their afflictions and granting them ease"—and at the same time grants prediction to those who hold the Lotus: "**Such a person shall not long thereafter proceed to the place of awakening, destroy the host of Māras, and attain supreme, perfect awakening**."

This is the fact that the Lotus ends with Samantabhadra taking the stage. We will return to what this fact means—but first the four preceding acts must be drawn together with the clasp of the *Prologue*.

**The echo of the *Prologue*.**

When the Lotus's *Prologue* opens, Mañjuśrī, answering Maitreya's question, cites the precedent of the Buddha **Candrasūryapradīpa** (Sun-Moon-Lamp): immeasurable aeons ago, this Tathāgata too had preached the Lotus Sūtra, and had too manifested these very six auspicious signs we see today. With this Mañjuśrī encourages faith—as Śākyamuni today manifests these six signs, he too will surely preach the Lotus.

From the *Prologue*'s "a past Buddha has already preached the Lotus," to the *Entrustment* chapter's "I now entrust the Lotus to you," to the *Encouragements of Samantabhadra* chapter's "in the future age of the Dharma's decline, those who hold the Lotus have Samantabhadra to guard them"—see this line clearly, and **the Lotus itself is a single beam of light running through the three times**. The Lotus preached by Sun-Moon-Lamp, the Lotus preached by Śākyamuni, the Lotus entrusted forward into the future—**these are the same beam of light, the same single emission across different dimensions of time**.

A star's light is not a light emitted at one moment and then gone out; it is a steady radiation continuing from the immeasurable past into the immeasurable future. The Lotus locks itself into a closed stellar system.

### Two Seeds from the Āgamas

Proposition Two—that the Lotus and the Avataṃsaka form a kind of dual structure—is not an impression a modern reader picks up from the outward difference between two sūtras. This duality already has its seeds in the Āgamas.

**The first seed—"the one."**

The opening of the *Satipaṭṭhāna-sutta* in the *Madhyama-āgama* holds this line: "**There is a one path that purifies beings, that carries them beyond sorrow and fear, that extinguishes suffering and affliction, that severs weeping, that attains the right Dharma—namely, the four foundations of mindfulness**."

"One path" is the most ancient Āgama seed of the Lotus's "one Buddha-vehicle." Chapter 2 (7.2) has already handled this line—from the Āgama's "one path" to the Lotus's "one Buddha-vehicle" is **the same "one" at two intensities of disclosure**. The Āgama's "one" is aimed at the individual meditator; the Lotus's "one" is aimed at the common destination of all sentient beings throughout the cosmos. The scale differs; the "one" is the same "one."

**The second seed—Brahmā's entreaty.**

This one runs deeper. Both the *Mahāparinibbāna-sutta* of the *Dīrgha-āgama* and the *Adhyeṣaṇā-vagga* of the *Ekottarika-āgama* record this episode:

Śākyamuni has just attained awakening beneath the bodhi tree. He contemplates that the Dharma he has realized is too profound to be readily grasped, that beings are "attached to the five desires" and hard to bring to faith. And so he forms a thought—**at first he does not wish to preach**. He intends simply to enter, directly, the silent nirvāṇa.

At this point the great Brahmā Sahāmpati descends from the heavens and, before the Buddha, three times entreats him: "World-Honoured One! We beg you, preach the Dharma for the sake of sentient beings."

Only then does Śākyamuni **consent**, and go to the Deer Park to preach the Four Noble Truths to the five bhikṣus.

The structure of this passage repays a careful look.

The Āgama records it of itself—**the first act of Śākyamuni after his awakening is not preaching, but silence**. At first he did not wish to speak.

The Avataṃsaka Sūtra, in its *Lokadhātu-vyūha* chapter, declares—the first act of Śākyamuni after his awakening was, **within the Ocean Seal Samādhi, to preach the Avataṃsaka to the great bodhisattvas and the devas, nāgas, and the eightfold assembly**. Human beings simply could not hear it, and so the Buddha "stepped back" to the Deer Park to preach the Āgamas. This Avataṃsaka claim has long been read in scholarly circles as "a myth the Huayan school fabricated later to elevate the Avataṃsaka."

But **the Brahmā-entreaty passage is the Āgama's own record**—the Āgama itself admits: the first act of Śākyamuni after awakening was silence, was the initial wish not to preach.

Read the two passages side by side:

**The Āgama side says "at first he did not wish to preach."**
**The Avataṃsaka side says "he had already preached, in the Ocean Seal Samādhi, and humans could not hear it."**

These two statements **point to the same fact**—what happened immediately after the awakening was not Āgama-level preaching, but something the human conceptual mind cannot receive. The Āgama records it as "silence"; the Avataṃsaka records it as "the soundless broadcast of the Ocean Seal Samādhi." **Two recordings of the same event, on different channels.**

This is the most ancient underlying evidence for Proposition Two at the level of the text. The star–black hole duality is not a metaphor read into the sūtras by later hands—**it is the structural agreement between the Āgama's own record of temporal sequence and the Avataṃsaka's own claim about content**.

What is striking is that this structure is also the Lotus's own core narrative—at the opening of the *Skilful Means* chapter, Śākyamuni too says something similar: "**Stop, Śāriputra! There is no need to say more**"—there is a Dharma, first and rarest and hardest to understand, that only Buddhas with Buddhas can fully exhaust. And only then does he unfold the ten suchnesses. The Lotus is clear about this structure, and the Lotus's way of handling it is "to speak out the part that can be spoken, and leave standing the structure that cannot"; the Avataṃsaka's way of handling it is "to let the unspeakable structure manifest itself." The same fact, two ways of handling it.

### Star and Black Hole — The Structure of the Duality

Now we can lay the duality out plainly, face on.

**The Lotus is the outward-radiating star**—emitting the content of reality in a form sentient beings can receive.
**The Avataṃsaka is the inward-collapsing black hole**—letting the structure of the dharma-realm unfold within itself, with no adjustment of channel to suit any outside receiver.

These are two utterly different teaching postures.

|    | Lotus (Star) | Avataṃsaka (Black Hole) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Outward radiation | Inward collapse |
| Teaching posture | A father speaking to children | Reality speaking to itself |
| Signal type | Emitted in a form beings can receive | Infinite information density, unprocessable by the conceptual mind |
| Intra-textual act | Stroking heads, person-by-person entrustment | Silent and unmoving, the dharma-realm self-manifesting |
| Temporal structure | Continuous emission across the three times (Prologue–Entrustment clasp) | One thought-moment containing the three times (Ocean Seal Samādhi) |
| Audience | All sentient beings | The dharma-realm itself |
| One character | Gathering | Manifesting |

This set of contrasts calls for care—⚠️ it is easily misread as "two independent supreme objects" or "two opposed teaching systems." **It is neither.**

In physics, a star and a black hole are **not two independent objects**; they are **two states of the same gravitational structure under different mass conditions**. A star of sufficiently great mass, at the end of its life, collapses into a black hole; they are "**two chapters of the same gravitational story**."

The Lotus and the Avataṃsaka are like this too—**two teaching-manifestations of the same dharmakāya story**. Toward sentient beings it manifests as the Lotus (star); toward the dharma-realm it manifests as the Avataṃsaka (black hole). The previous chapter (7.11) handled this—the Śākyamuni who can preach the Lotus and the Vairocana silent and unmoving in the Ocean Seal Samādhi **are the same Buddha**. The Huayan school takes Vairocana as the dharmakāya-Buddha and Śākyamuni as the response-and-transformation body, but the two are not "two Buddhas"—they are two presentations of the same dharmakāya at two levels of manifestation.

⚠️ The physical analogy must stop at structural suggestion. Star and black hole are physics' description of the relation between mass and gravity; the Lotus and the Avataṃsaka are Buddhism's unfolding of the dharmakāya's teaching function. All the two share is the abstract formal feature of "**two manifestation forms of the same structure**." The physical analogy cannot be pressed any further down than this.

### Samantabhadra's Double Appearance

The strongest internal evidence for Proposition Two lies not in the exposition of Pointing at the Moon, not in the patriarchs' classifications, but in a single fact of the sūtras themselves—**Samantabhadra Bodhisattva's double appearance**.

**The Lotus's *Encouragements of Samantabhadra* chapter**—Samantabhadra comes from the east on a six-tusked white elephant, asks the Tathāgata "how can one obtain this *Lotus Sūtra*?", and takes up the work of guarding the Dharma, granting prediction, and guiding "shall not long thereafter proceed to the place of awakening." Samantabhadra's act here is **escort**—escorting the holder of the Lotus from the Lotus's venue on to the next station.

**The Forty *Avataṃsaka*'s *Bhadracarī-praṇidhāna* chapter**—having finished extolling the Tathāgata's merits, Samantabhadra proclaims to Sudhana and the assembly of bodhisattvas the **Ten Great Practice-Vows**: to revere all Buddhas, to praise the Tathāgatas, to make extensive offerings, to repent of karmic obstructions, to rejoice in merit, to entreat the turning of the Dharma-wheel, to entreat the Buddhas to abide in the world, to constantly follow the Buddhas in study, to perpetually accord with sentient beings, to universally transfer all merits. Samantabhadra's act here is **disclosure**—proclaiming, to those who have already arrived at the next station, the ten paths to accomplishing the gate of merit.

Read these two acts side by side—

**What Samantabhadra does in the Lotus's final chapter is "escort to the next station."**
**What Samantabhadra does in the Avataṃsaka's final fascicle is "issue the map at the next station."**

These are not two acts performed by two Samantabhadras; **they are two consecutive acts of the same Samantabhadra**.

Between the Lotus's act of escort and the Avataṃsaka's act of disclosure, **there is nothing for any scholarly interpreter to insert—the texts themselves complete the handoff**.

Pointing at the Moon's response to this fact is one sentence only—**point it out**.

Proposition Two does not need this book to prove it. Proposition Two is already self-evidenced in Samantabhadra's double appearance. This book need only not block the way.

### Three Misreadings to Guard Against

Having come this far, there are three misreadings to guard against.

**The first: gathering ≠ supremacy.**

The character "gathering" in Part VII is most liable to slide into a "king of sūtras" reading of higher and lower—the Lotus "gathers" all the scriptures, therefore the Lotus is supreme over all scriptures, and the Avataṃsaka too falls within the range of what the Lotus "gathers."

The remedy need not be sought from outside. Zhiyi of Tiantai, in the *Profound Meaning of the Lotus*, has already blocked this reading from within. Zhiyi classifies the teachings by the **five flavours**—the five stages in the transformation of milk: milk, cream, fresh butter, ripened butter, ghee—and assigns the Lotus to the "ghee" flavour. But Zhiyi's explicit formulation is:

> In that Buddha's lifetime of teaching, the Avataṃsaka was preached first and the Lotus preached last … in every case the Lotus is taken as the **final teaching, the final flavour**.

"**Last preached, last flavour**"—the structure of this line is **temporal**, not evaluative. The five flavours are the five stages of milk's transformation; ghee is the final stage, but "final" does not equal "highest." **The five flavours are five necessary stages, and ghee could not exist without the foundation of the four flavours before it.**

Bring this Tiantai usage to the "gathering" of Part VII—**the Lotus's gathering is the gathering of the ripening stage**. It can gather because the prior stages have all completed their respective work. The Āgama's seeds, the Prajñā's dismantling, the fine-grained analysis of Yogācāra, the content the Avataṃsaka broadcasts simultaneously on another channel—not one of these can be spared. Gathering is not supremacy; it is **ripening**.

Further: the Lotus and the Avataṃsaka are the star–black hole duality. Between star and black hole there is no relation of higher and lower, only a division of function. **The star lacks the black hole's density; the black hole lacks the star's receivability—each stands in its own irreplaceable position.** The Lotus gathers all sentient beings, because it is the star; the Avataṃsaka manifests the dharma-realm itself, because it is the black hole. Neither replaces the other, neither defeats the other.

**The second: duality ≠ dualism.**

The star–black hole duality is not a Buddhist version of dualism—it is not the positing of two independent supreme objects.

In physics, star and black hole are two states of the same gravitational structure. The Lotus and the Avataṃsaka are **two teaching-manifestations of the same dharmakāya**. At the fundamental level, the Buddha who writes the Lotus and the Buddha who writes the Avataṃsaka are the same Buddha, only expressing himself on different channels.

The duality is a **duality of function**, not a **bifurcation of essence**.

**The third: functional-duality reading vs. hierarchical classification.**

The reading this book chooses is the **functional-duality reading**—reading the Lotus and the Avataṃsaka as two teaching functions of the same dharmakāya.

But among the patriarchs across the ages, some adopt a **hierarchical classification**—for example, Fazang of the Huayan school, in the *Treatise on the Five Teachings of Huayan*, explicitly distinguishes the "**distinct-teaching one-vehicle**" (the Avataṃsaka) from the "**shared-teaching one-vehicle**" (the Lotus), the former ranking, in his classification, above the latter.

This book does not adopt Fazang's hierarchical classification, but neither does it oppose Fazang. The reason is that the two readings address different problems—

**Fazang addresses the problem of doctrinal classification**—within the internal teaching system of the Huayan school, how are the various sūtras to be ranked? Within this frame, "distinct-teaching one-vehicle vs. shared-teaching one-vehicle" is an internally consistent and rigorously logical answer.

**This book addresses the problem of functional duality**—within the doctrinal architecture of the eight-part work, what function does the Lotus, and what function does the Avataṃsaka, each carry? Within this frame, hierarchical classification is not a valid tool, because the question of "who is higher and who is lower" immediately triggers the pressure of the first correction, "gathering is not supremacy." What this book needs is a **functional description**: who does what, for whom, and how. The star–black hole duality is exactly such a functional description.

The relation between the two readings is one of **standing side by side**, not of opposition. Fazang's hierarchical reading is valid within the Huayan school's internal teaching system; this book's functional-duality reading is valid within the doctrinal architecture of the eight-part work.

### The First Handoff — The Venue Is in Place

Part VII having come this far, the work that remains is two handoffs.

**The first handoff** is to hand the venue over to Part VIII.

The Lotus's venue is the **Sahā world**—the world sentient beings can see. From the *Prologue*'s Dharma-assembly on Vulture Peak, to the Many-Treasures Stūpa rising in mid-air in the *Stūpa* chapter, to the eternal realization of the *Lifespan* chapter, to the stroking of heads in the *Entrustment* chapter—the entire venue of the Lotus is built always within **the spacetime sentient beings can perceive**. A star's light must have a dimension along which it can be seen.

The Avataṃsaka's venue is the **dharma-realm itself**—a dimension the human conceptual mind cannot directly receive. The opening passage of the *Lokadhātu-vyūha* chapter:

> The Buddha was in the *araṇya* Bodhimaṇḍa of Magadha, having just attained complete awakening … entering the samādhi of the Buddhas in the instant of the present moment.

The Buddha sits silent and unmoving; the dharma-realm manifests itself. This venue is not a venue for speaking to human beings; it is the venue in which **the dharma-realm unfolds to itself**. The next part (8.X) will unfold the doctrinal structure of this venue in full. This chapter points out here only one thing—**that venue is already in place**.

And here is a subtle but important observation. **At the moment the Lotus ends, the Avataṃsaka's Ocean Seal Samādhi does not "begin"—it has never been interrupted; the reader has only now arrived at the position where it can be heard.**

To go from the end of the Lotus toward the opening of the Avataṃsaka is not "leaving one room and entering another"; it is "**switching, in the same room, to another way of listening**."

The venue is in place.

### The Second Handoff — The Person Is in Place

**The second handoff** is Samantabhadra Bodhisattva.

In the *Encouragements of Samantabhadra* chapter, Samantabhadra comes from the eastern Buddha-land of Ratnatejābhyudgatarāja Tathāgata on a six-tusked white elephant, taking up the duty of guarding the holder of the Lotus and guiding such a person to "shall not long thereafter proceed to the place of awakening." The Lotus ends with this act—**not a contingent choice**, but an intra-textual structural one. Samantabhadra is the bodhisattva about to take up the next stage of teaching.

In the *Bhadracarī-praṇidhāna* chapter of the Forty *Avataṃsaka*, having finished extolling the Tathāgata's merits, Samantabhadra proclaims to Sudhana and the assembly the Ten Great Practice-Vows. These ten vows—to revere all Buddhas, praise the Tathāgatas, make extensive offerings, repent of karmic obstructions, rejoice in merit, entreat the turning of the Dharma-wheel, entreat the Buddhas to abide in the world, constantly follow the Buddhas in study, perpetually accord with sentient beings, universally transfer all merits—are the very structural skeleton that Part VIII will walk next.

The Lotus ends with Samantabhadra taking the stage; the Avataṃsaka unfolds with Samantabhadra making his vows. **Two consecutive acts of the same bodhisattva**—this is a fact at the level of the text, not an academic arrangement fabricated by interpreters.

The person is in place.

### The Deepest Meaning of Gathering

The character of Part VII is "gathering."

Having come to this point of closure, we can now speak the deepest meaning of the character "gathering."

Gathering looks like "taking everything in"—drawing the three vehicles into the one vehicle, drawing sentient beings into the one Buddha-vehicle, drawing skilful means into the ultimate. From the *Skilful Means* chapter's gathering of the three into one, to the curing of the two-vehicle illness in the burning house and the destitute son, to the responsive manifestation of the universal gate, to the simultaneous display of both extremes in Devadatta and the Nāga Princess, to the universal reverence of Never-Disparaging—every chapter is performing the act of "drawing the scattered into one."

This understanding is correct, but it is only the surface of gathering.

The deepest meaning of gathering is not "**I take everything in**"—it is "**I hand everything out**."

The Lotus ends with the stroking of heads and the entrusting: the Buddha's hand actually laid on each bodhisattva's head, handing the Dharma out. The Lotus ends with Samantabhadra taking the stage as its very last act: handing the onward road to another bodhisattva. Both of these acts **are "handing out."**

Taking in is only for the sake of handing out the better. To gather all things into the one Buddha-vehicle is so that the one Buddha-vehicle, as a single whole, can be **handed on to the next station**. **The end of gathering is delivery, not possession.**

What Part VII does here is the same act—handing out the doctrine, the propositions, the structure accumulated across the eleven preceding chapters, all of it, in the posture of "**to Part VIII, to you**."

You have walked through the final chapter of the largest part of the eight-part work. From the burning house to the *Lifespan* chapter, you have followed the Lotus's father and looked upon the fire of the three realms; followed the Lotus's elder and received back the son who shovelled dung for twenty years; followed the Lotus's clouds and rain and watched one rain fall on all while the three grasses and two trees each received its own portion; followed the Lotus's guide into the conjured city and out again; followed the Lotus's kinsman and recognized the jewel sewn into the robe; followed the Lotus's wheel-turning king and beheld the jewel in his topknot; followed the Lotus's assembly through the living moment of Devadatta's prediction and the Nāga Princess's awakening; followed Never-Disparaging in bowing, from afar, to the very people who picked up stones to strike him; followed Avalokiteśvara in answering and manifesting at the sound of a cry; followed the Buddha in the *Lifespan* chapter and looked upon a lifespan without measure.

These are not matters that end once you have finished reading them. All of these—**from the very moment you have read them, are already a thread of causes and conditions in the continuum of your own mind**. They go on with you, downstream.

### The Question of the Next Part

From Part VIII onward, "gathering" becomes "**manifesting**."

"Gathering" is the posture of speaking to all sentient beings. "Manifesting" is the posture of letting the dharma-realm manifest itself. There is no longer the tone of a father to a child, no longer the parables, the narratives, the predictions—these forms tuned so that you could receive. In their place is a language that unfolds directly at the density of the dharma-realm itself.

This is a completely different kind of reading experience. When you were reading the Lotus, there was a Buddha speaking, all along. When you read the Avataṃsaka, **there is no longer a "speaker"**—there is the dharma-realm's own unfolding. The southward journey of Sudhana through fifty-three teachers, the dharma-realm structure of the ten profound gates, Indra's net, the Ocean Seal Samādhi, Samantabhadra's Ten Great Vows—these are not things the Buddha tells you; they are the mutual manifestation, within one and the same structure, of you yourself, as one portion of the dharma-realm, together with all the other portions of the dharma-realm.

It will be hard to read. There is a feeling, in the reading, of "what on earth is this about?" This is not a problem with the text; it is a matter of structure—**the Avataṃsaka does not speak to you, the Avataṃsaka lets you hear the dharma-realm**.

The preparation for reading Part VIII is just to carry with you this position, the position of having read Part VII to its close. You have already passed through every dimension of the star—from the coarsest skilful means to the deepest remoteness. What you are now to enter is the inner structure that remains after the star has burned out and collapsed into a black hole.

### The True Mark of All Dharmas, Just So from the Beginning

One last word, to seal Part VII.

The whole of Part VII's twelve chapters has handled, from first to last, only one object—**the true mark of all dharmas**. The *Skilful Means* chapter's line, "only Buddhas with Buddhas can fully exhaust the true mark of all dharmas," is Part VII's first line of scripture, and also the object Part VII has been drawing toward all along.

Having walked through the burning house's diagnosis, the destitute son's recognition of his father, the medicinal herbs' universal nourishing, the conjured city at the midway point, the original possession of the robe-jewel and topknot-jewel, the two extremes of Devadatta and the Nāga Princess, the practice gate of Never-Disparaging, the responsive manifestation of the universal gate, the original lifespan of the *Lifespan* chapter—these are not eleven different matters being spoken of. **These are eleven different angles of approach to one and the same matter**—the true mark of all dharmas.

What is the true mark of all dharmas?

It is not a "what." It is not an object that can be pointed at by a name. It is your every thought in this present moment, your every breath, every pain, every joy—**the very fact that they are happening, just as they are**. It is not waiting somewhere far off for you to attain it; it **is already complete in the present moment in which you read this sentence**.

All sentient beings have the jewel in the robe—**precisely because the true mark of all dharmas is just so from the beginning**.
All sentient beings have the tathāgatagarbha, are equally the dharmakāya—**precisely because the true mark of all dharmas is just so from the beginning**.
All the Lotus's teachings flow out from the true mark of all dharmas—**which is to say, the true mark of all dharmas is just so from the beginning**.
Avalokiteśvara answering and manifesting at the sound of a cry, the Buddha realizing the eternal in the remote past, you being able to read this very line at this very moment—**all of it is the true mark of all dharmas, just so from the beginning**.

The true mark of all dharmas is not a proposition Part VII discusses; the true mark of all dharmas is **the ground that has been operating, in the present moment, from first to last throughout Part VII**.

After you close the book, walk to the kitchen and pour a glass of water, hear the birds outside the window, feel the weight of your feet on the floor—**every one of these instants is a present-moment manifestation of the true mark of all dharmas**. Not because Part VII makes it so, but because it has always been so. All Part VII does is to point this out, from the position where it has always been, **in language you can understand**.

The place where it stops is not an oversight; it is reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where the pen is laid down—so that the reader knows that, **beyond this line, one must rely on one's own Dharma-eye**.

Part VII's place of laying down the pen is here: the venue is handed over, the person is handed over, the gathering–manifesting duality is pointed at, original awareness is pointed to. What Part VII can do ends here.

The truly complete understanding of the gathering–manifesting duality must wait until you yourself have finished reading Part VIII—and that understanding is not here, not under the pen of any single chapter of Part VIII, but **in your own Dharma-eye, in the present moment**.
