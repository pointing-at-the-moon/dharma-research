## 7.9 Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging—Universal Reverence and Prophecy Put into Action

The previous chapter laid open a single proposition: **the inherent is truly universal**. No kind of sentient being can be excluded from the jewel sewn into the robe. Not even Devadatta, not even the eight-year-old dragon girl, can be left out; those beings in saṃsāra who look the darkest, the most alien, the most beyond saving—on the level of the equality of the dharmakāya, they stand equal to Śākyamuni.

Once this proposition is truly held, it triggers a question at the next layer down: **knowing this is one thing. Actually treating every person you meet in daily life with this recognition is another thing entirely.**

To know that all sentient beings have buddha-nature is easy. To bow deeply to the colleague who just cursed at you, thinking in your heart *"you will become a Buddha,"* is hard. To bow deeply to the person you have disliked since childhood, thinking *"you will become a Buddha,"* is harder. To bow deeply to a stranger who seems to have nothing to do with the Dharma—who may even be full of contempt for the Dharma—thinking *"you will become a Buddha,"* is hardest of all.

The chapter "The Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging" deals with exactly this step. What it shows us is a bodhisattva whose entire practice is compressed into a single gesture: **on seeing any person at all, he bows to him deeply and says to him, "you will become a Buddha."** He did this his whole life—did it when reviled, did it when struck, did it still after being struck and fleeing to a distance.

This is the gentlest, and also the hardest to do, of all the chapters in the *Lotus Sūtra*.

### The Story of Never-Disparaging

First we have to set the scene.

The Buddha says to Bodhisattva Mahāsthāmaprāpta: countless *asaṃkhyeya* kalpas ago there was a Buddha named Bhīṣmagarjitasvara-rāja, the Tathāgata Awesome-Sound-King. After this Buddha entered nirvāṇa, **the True Dharma had perished and the Semblance Dharma was current**, and there appeared a body of "overweening monks, possessed of great power."

This is an age of the Dharma's decline—the Buddha is gone, the True Dharma is gone too, and those in authority are the overweening (people who think they have attained much in practice while in fact they have attained nothing). **It is in this age that Never-Disparaging appears.**

The scripture says:

> At that time there was a bodhisattva-monk named Never-Disparaging. Mahāsthāmaprāpta! For what reason was he called Never-Disparaging? This monk, with everyone he saw—whether monk, nun, layman, or laywoman—would bow and praise them, saying: "I deeply revere you and dare not slight you. Why? Because you all walk the bodhisattva path, and you shall attain Buddhahood."

The content of his practice was extremely simple. On seeing people, he bowed—no distinction of monk, nun, layman, laywoman; no distinction of acquaintance or stranger; no distinction of good person or bad person—**everyone he saw**, he bowed to. And as he bowed he said one sentence: "I deeply revere you and dare not slight you. Why? Because you all walk the bodhisattva path, and in the future you will all become Buddhas."

The name "Never-Disparaging" was not one he gave himself. The scripture goes on to explain—**it was a nickname others gave him.** Because every time he met someone he said this same sentence, over time everyone came to call him "Never-Disparaging."

Then the scripture tells what happened to him next. This passage is the key to the whole story, and must be read slowly:

> And this monk did not specialize in reciting the scriptures, but only practiced making obeisance, and **even when he saw the fourfold assembly from afar, he would still go up to them and bow and praise** them, saying: "I dare not disparage you, for you are all to become Buddhas." Among the fourfold assembly were those who gave rise to anger and to impure minds, and reviled him with harsh words... So it went for many years, constantly reviled, never giving rise to anger, always saying: "You shall become a Buddha." When he spoke these words, some among the people would strike him with sticks or hurl tiles and stones at him, and **fleeing to a safe distance, he would still call out aloud**: "I dare not disparage you, for you are all to become Buddhas."

There are two details in this passage that we should single out.

**The first detail**: "did not specialize in reciting the scriptures." Never-Disparaging was not a scholar steeped in the scriptures, not a Dharma-master of boundless eloquence, not a practitioner of deep meditative absorption. His practice was **this one thing only**—on seeing people, he bowed. He did not specialize in the scriptures, did not specialize in meditation, had no supernatural powers. He just bowed.

**The second detail**: two active gestures.

**"Seeing the fourfold assembly from afar, he would still go up to them and bow and praise"**—seeing people in the distance, **he deliberately walked over** to bow. This is not "I bow when I happen to run into someone"; it is "I see them and I walk over." The two words **go up to** are the key—it is **deliberate, purposeful** action.

**"Fleeing to a safe distance, he would still call out aloud"**—when someone took up sticks, tiles, or stones to strike him, he **fled**; and once he reached a safe distance, **he kept on declaring aloud**: "I dare not disparage you, for you are all to become Buddhas."

Put the two gestures together, and Never-Disparaging's behavioral pattern is this: **actively draw near to those who can be revered, actively withdraw from scenes of violence, and from a safe distance keep on declaring.**

This is by no means a "non-resister who stands still and takes the blows." He flees. When struck, he flees. But once he reaches a distance, he does not stop—he keeps saying that sentence.

### He Is Not One Who Passively Takes the Blows

Here we must immediately deal with the misreading most likely to arise—reading Never-Disparaging as a **weak, meekly submissive, easily bullied** figure.

This misreading is especially easy to fall into today. Many readers picture Never-Disparaging as a lowly monk, head bowed and back bent, gaze gentle, never striking back when struck, never answering back when reviled—a kind of "Buddhist version of docility." This reading is wrong, and wrong at a deep level.

The scripture itself gives us two active gestures—**go up to** (actively drawing near) and **flee to a safe distance** (actively withdrawing). The three Chinese translations (Kumārajīva's *Lotus Sūtra*, Dharmarakṣa's *Correct-Dharma Lotus*, and Jñānagupta's *Supplemented Lotus*) agree completely on these two gestures—Dharmarakṣa renders "when struck with tiles and stones, he continued to **raise his voice from a distance**, instructing them," and the supplemented version reads "**fleeing to a safe distance, he would still call out aloud**." A dynamic preserved in common by three independent translations cannot be the embellishment of any single translator. **This is the most original core of the Never-Disparaging narrative.**

This structure was already present in the Āgamas. The *Ekottarāgama* "Sūtra to Mahā-Kauṣṭhila" sets two acts side by side: "If others give rise to anger, we shall **train in forbearance**; if others harbor jealousy, we shall **withdraw**." **Forbearance** is the inner posture (the mind gives no rise to anger); **withdrawal** is the outer action (the body does not stay). The two stand side by side without obstructing one another.

This two-track structure of **inward forbearance, outward withdrawal** is a standard practice-combination Buddhism has held since the time of the Āgamas—**no hatred arising in the mind, the body leaving when it ought to leave.** Never-Disparaging's "fleeing to a safe distance, still calling out aloud" fits this structure exactly: in the mind he keeps revering the other (inward forbearance), with the body he moves to a safe distance (outward withdrawal), and with the mouth he never stops declaring (the heart of universal reverence).

Deeper still is the Tiantai master Zhiyi's reading of this passage. He reads Never-Disparaging's "enduring beating and reviling" as **"wearing the robe of the Tathāgata"**—that is, the "robe of gentle forbearance" spoken of in the *Lotus Sūtra*'s "Chapter on the Dharma Teacher." The robe of gentle forbearance is the bodhisattva's **inner adornment**, not an **outer constraint**. A bodhisattva wearing the robe of gentle forbearance may flee or advance, may speak or stay silent; what matters is not what the body does, but the **gentleness in the mind that does not retreat**. Zhiyi's reading aligns perfectly with the scripture's "fleeing to a safe distance"—it does not matter that the body flees, so long as the reverence in the mind has not retreated.

This clarification matters especially for the contemporary reader. **Universal reverence is an inner stance, not an outer constraint.** The content of universal reverence is "to bow deeply, in the mind, to all sentient beings, deeply trusting that they inherently possess buddha-nature." The outward form universal reverence takes must be adjusted to the situation—it can be a face-to-face bow, it can be a spoken declaration, and it can also be fleeing to a safe distance when someone is about to strike you, and continuing to call out aloud from afar.

While Never-Disparaging is fleeing, his mind is still revering the very people who want to strike him—this is the heart of universal reverence; where his body happens to be is a **secondary question**.

### Two Hundred Koṭi Kalpas Later

The story goes on.

Never-Disparaging spent his whole life this way—bowing, praising, being reviled, being struck, fleeing, going on saying it.

At the end of his life, the scripture says something exceedingly beautiful—**"in the air he heard in full the *Lotus Sūtra*, twenty thousand koṭi gāthās, previously spoken by Awesome-Sound-King Buddha**, all of which he was able to receive and hold." The *Lotus Sūtra* spoken long ago by Awesome-Sound-King Buddha suddenly unfolded in the air at the moment of his death, and he heard every verse, one by one, and held them all. On the spot he "**immediately attained the purification of the eye-faculty as above, and the purification of the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind faculties**"—the six faculties were purified.

Then the scripture says: "**His lifespan was extended by two million koṭi nayuta years**, and he expounded this *Lotus Sūtra* widely to others." His lifespan was lengthened. With this extended lifespan, he expounded the *Lotus Sūtra* widely for the sake of sentient beings.

This structure must be read carefully. Never-Disparaging's attainment **was not completed in an instant during the years of his universal reverence.** The scripture divides it into three stages:

**Stage one—the period of universal reverence and bowing.** Reviled, struck, pelted with sticks, tiles, and stones. This is the stage of planting seeds. For himself and for the other, he plants uncontaminated seeds.

**Stage two—the deathbed period of hearing the sūtra and purifying the six faculties.** The seeds planted in past lives, at this moment with conditions ripe, become manifest activity. This is the stage of the seeds coming into function.

**Stage three—the period, after his lifespan is extended, of widely expounding the *Lotus Sūtra*.** A stretch of "two million koṭi nayuta years." This is the stage in which, in the posture of one who has attained, he plants seeds for others on a far larger scale.

The temporal structure is perfectly clear—**planting the seed, maturation, manifest broadcasting**, three distinct stages. This is by no means the magic of "one sentence and a Buddha."

Then, at the story's close, the Buddha reveals two things.

The first: **"The Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging of that time—was he some other person? He was none other than my own self."**

That Never-Disparaging of long ago, reviled, struck, fleeing, still calling out aloud—he was me. Śākyamuni, in a past life, once walked this very road.

The second: those four assemblies who once slighted Never-Disparaging, reviled him, struck him—what became of them afterward? The scripture writes:

> Because of slighting me with minds of anger, **for two hundred koṭi kalpas they constantly failed to meet a Buddha, hear the Dharma, or see the Saṅgha**; **for a thousand kalpas they suffered great agony in the Avīci hell.** Having exhausted that retribution, they again encountered Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging, who instructed them in supreme perfect awakening.

The weight of this passage is immense. Two hundred koṭi kalpas of not meeting a Buddha, not hearing the Dharma, not seeing the Saṅgha—the conditions for the Dharma severed. A thousand kalpas of great agony in the Avīci hell—the deepest hell, the longest suffering. Only after the karmic retribution was exhausted to the end did they **encounter Bodhisattva Never-Disparaging again, and through his teaching arouse the mind for awakening.**

Who did these people finally become? The scripture's next line:

> Bhadrapāla and the rest, the five hundred bodhisattvas... were they some other persons? They are these very ones.

They are the five hundred bodhisattvas, Bhadrapāla and the rest, listening to the sūtra in today's *Lotus* assembly.

This is how the whole story is gathered up—**the seeds that were planted all eventually became manifest**, **even those who slighted Never-Disparaging not excepted.** But the cost of becoming manifest **was two hundred koṭi kalpas plus a thousand kalpas in the Avīci hell.**

### Nor Is This a Cheap Universal Buddhahood

Reading this far, we have to deal with another easy misreading—**reading this story as a cheap promise.**

One form of cheapening is: since all sentient beings will become Buddhas anyway, why bother practicing? It will happen regardless.

Another form is: to say "you will become a Buddha" to anyone at all is the same as saying it to no one—the sentence contains no specific content.

Both of these cheapenings misread the story.

The Never-Disparaging chapter itself already supplies the counter-evidence—**two hundred koṭi kalpas plus a thousand kalpas in the Avīci hell.** The seeds that are planted do indeed all become manifest, but the road in between can be cruelly long. A person who today, full of anger, curses at you—even if the jewel in his robe is real, even if the "you shall become a Buddha" you say to him is real, even if that sentence does in truth plant in him one uncontaminated seed—all of this holds, and yet he may still have to toss and turn in the Avīci hell for a thousand kalpas before he can meet Never-Disparaging again and arouse the mind.

**Planting seeds does not exempt anyone from karmic retribution.** This is what the previous chapter already handled—even the fruit of arhatship cannot dissolve karma, and prophecy can do so still less. The seed Never-Disparaging plants is real; the karmic fruit of the slighters' anger is real too; the two hold at once without canceling one another.

Deeper still: **universal reverence is not cheap, because it is built on the floor of no-self.**

This layer has to be spoken from the language of the earlier Part Five, "Cutting"—the *Diamond Sūtra*. The way the *Diamond Sūtra* handles compassion is this:

> Thus liberating immeasurable, innumerable, boundless sentient beings, in truth there is no sentient being liberated. Why? Subhūti! If a bodhisattva has the mark of self, the mark of person, the mark of sentient being, the mark of life-span, then he is not a bodhisattva.

True salvation must be built on the empty floor of "in truth there is no sentient being liberated." If there is a "self" doing the saving and a "sentient being" being saved, then it is no longer true salvation.

This structure applies directly to Never-Disparaging's universal reverence.

If, in Never-Disparaging's mind, there is the objectifying structure "**I** revere **him**; **he** is a sentient being; **he** will become a Buddha," then he has fallen into the four marks the *Diamond Sūtra* dismantles. His universal reverence immediately becomes a self-satisfying performance—"see how compassionate I am, how willing I am to revere every sentient being." This is not universal reverence; this is **self-conceit in disguise.**

True universal reverence requires that Never-Disparaging, **in the very moment of saying "you shall become a Buddha," have in his mind neither a revering self nor a sentient being to be revered.** This is structurally identical to the *Diamond Sūtra*'s "no sentient being is in truth liberated"—**both take the absence of marks as the prerequisite of compassion.**

The reason Never-Disparaging could persist for many years, never retreating in the face of revilement and beating, **is not that he had enormous moral willpower.** It is not that he gritted his teeth and thought, "endure a little more, endure a little more." He had no need to endure at all. **Because his universal reverence rested from the very start on the floor of no-self, he had no "offended self" that could be offended.**

This is the key to understanding the Never-Disparaging dharma-gate—Never-Disparaging is not the most morally steadfast of people; **Never-Disparaging is the one who first put the *Diamond Sūtra*'s no-self thoroughly into practice.** He could keep going not because he had more patience than others, but because he had less "self" than others that could be harmed.

The *Diamond Sūtra* dismantles the four marks at the level of language; Never-Disparaging displays, at the level of action, what action without the four marks looks like. The two sūtras are inside and outside of one another—**the *Diamond Sūtra* supplies the linguistic apparatus for breaking grasping; the *Lotus Sūtra* supplies the personal exemplar without grasping.** Without the *Diamond Sūtra*'s cutting, there would be no Never-Disparaging's reverence; without Never-Disparaging's reverence, the *Diamond Sūtra*'s cutting would not know where to land.

### Loving-Kindness Pervading All—The Seed in the Āgamas

Universal reverence, too, was not conjured out of nothing by the Mahāyāna. It has a deep seed in the Āgamas—*mettā*, loving-kindness pervading all beings.

*Ekottarāgama*, fascicle forty-one, records the Buddha's own first-person account:

> You should practice loving-kindness, broadcast loving-kindness; by practicing loving-kindness, all hatred in the mind will dissolve of itself... In days past, **for seven years I continually cultivated loving-kindness**, passing through seven kalpas of formation and dissolution without returning to birth-and-death.

The Buddha says that his bodhisattva path began with seven years of cultivating loving-kindness alone. Seven years is not seven days; seven kalpas of formation and dissolution is no brief stretch of time. **Loving-kindness is a core practice that bears the temporal span of seven kalpas of cosmic formation and dissolution, that holds the direction unbroken across the turning of birth-and-death.** It is not an auxiliary practice; it is a root practice.

Never-Disparaging's posture of "everyone he saw, he bowed to and praised" and the Buddha's posture of "for seven years I continually cultivated loving-kindness" are, in their temporal structure, **completely identical**—both are **unconditional, uninterrupted facing of every object.**

*Ekottarāgama*, fascicle forty-seven, is even more direct. It mentions the eleven fruits of the loving-kindness liberation—sleeping in peace, waking in peace, seeing no evil dreams, protected by devas, beloved by humans, untouched by poison, untouched by weapons, untroubled by water, fire, or thieves—and then comes a key sentence:

> If sentient beings practice the liberation of loving-kindness, **broadcasting its meaning and expounding it for others**, they will obtain these eleven fruits.

"Broadcasting its meaning and expounding it for others"—**actively externalizing** the inner loving-kindness into a declaration to others. A verse in the same fascicle adds: "**Practicing loving-kindness pervading all**, never bearing a hateful mind... **swiftly attaining nirvāṇa**, eternally reaching the unconditioned."

These few lines correspond, structurally and completely, to the story of Never-Disparaging:

| Āgamic *mettā* | Never-Disparaging |
|---|---|
| For seven years continually cultivating loving-kindness | So it went for many years, constantly reviled, never giving rise to anger |
| Broadcasting its meaning, expounding it for others | Seeing the fourfold assembly from afar, still going up to bow and praise |
| Practicing loving-kindness pervading all | Everyone he saw... he bowed to and praised |
| Swiftly attaining nirvāṇa | Deathbed purification of the six faculties, lifespan extended, widely expounding the *Lotus* |

From the loving-kindness pervading all of the Āgamas, to the Universal Reverence dharma-gate of Never-Disparaging—**this is not a new invention; it is a dramatized completion.** The Āgamic *mettā* is a mind-practice—pervading all sentient beings with the inner loving-kindness of the mind. The Lotus universal reverence is **the externalization of this mind-practice into a concrete practice mobilizing the three karmas of body, speech, and mind in unison.** It is the same practice, raised from an inward state to an action that can be seen, recorded, and imitated.

This Āgamic root has one important supplement—loving-kindness is never **blind, undiscriminating accommodation.**

The *Saṃyuktāgama*, fascicle forty-six, records that when Śakra fought the asuras, "**by the power of loving-kindness, with majestic strength he subdued the army of asuras.**" Loving-kindness runs in parallel with majesty. The *Madhyamāgama* "Sūtra to Velāma" says that one who cultivates loving-kindness must "**contemplate all dharmas as impermanent, suffering, empty, and not-self**"—loving-kindness operates in tandem with the wisdom-eye.

The loving-kindness of the Āgamas was never "the unprincipled niceness of a pushover"—saying yes to everyone, refusing nothing, making oneself a machine of undiscriminating accommodation. The loving-kindness of the Āgamas is **loving-kindness and wisdom in tandem**—coexisting with majesty, coexisting with wisdom.

Never-Disparaging's universal reverence inherits exactly this tradition. His universal reverence toward the fourfold assembly, and his wise judgment of scenes of violence (flee when one must flee), belong to **two faces of one and the same practice structure.** Revere when it is time to revere; withdraw when it is time to withdraw. No hatred arising in the mind, but the body protecting itself when it ought to. Loving-kindness and wisdom are one.

### The Threefold Inversion of Prophecy

Having come this far, we can state clearly what the Never-Disparaging chapter contributes structurally to the whole of Part Seven.

Walking down through the earlier chapters—from the burning house, the destitute son, the medicinal herbs, the conjured city, the jewel in the robe and the jewel in the topknot, on to Devadatta and the dragon girl—prophecy has all along been **a speech-act of the Buddha**. The Buddha prophesies for Śāriputra, the Buddha prophesies for Kāśyapa, the Buddha prophesies for Devadatta, the Buddha prophesies for the dragon girl. The agent of prophecy is the Buddha, the form of prophecy is a sentence, the object of prophecy is some particular chosen person.

The Never-Disparaging chapter accomplishes a threefold inversion.

**First inversion—the agent of prophecy is democratized.**

Never-Disparaging is an **ordinary bodhisattva-monk**. He "did not specialize in reciting the scriptures," he had no supernatural powers, no special standing. His sole qualification is "contemplating that the fourfold assembly possess the cause of buddha-nature"—he saw the fact that sentient beings inherently possess buddha-nature. This contemplation **any practitioner who correctly understands "the inherent" can establish.**

Before the Never-Disparaging chapter, prophecy was essentially the Buddha's exclusive speech-act. After the Never-Disparaging chapter, **prophecy becomes something any practitioner can do.** This does not make prophecy cheap; it relocates prophecy from "an exceptional event in doctrine" to "a daily practice on the path."

**Second inversion—the form of prophecy is put into action.**

Zhiyi's reading of the Never-Disparaging chapter has a precise formulation: "**Deep reverence is the karma of mind, the words of non-disparagement are the karma of speech, deliberately approaching to bow is the karma of body**; these three, joined with compassion, are the **Vow-and-Ease practice**."

Body-karma, speech-karma, mind-karma operating all at once—the act of bowing is body-karma, saying "you shall become a Buddha" is speech-karma, the deep reverence in the mind is mind-karma. **The three karmas in unison + the mind of compassion = the Vow-and-Ease practice**, one of the four bodhisattva practices in the "Chapter on Practice in Ease and Joy."

This structured reading means that "you shall become a Buddha" **is not merely a sentence.** It is a **complete action.** The bowing is this sentence, the praising is this sentence, the deep reverence is this sentence, the deliberate going-up to bow is this sentence, and the fleeing to a safe distance and calling out aloud is this sentence too. **Never-Disparaging's entire existence is this sentence.**

Prophecy is no longer a **statement**; prophecy is an **act**.

**Third inversion—prophecy is practice itself.**

This is the deepest of the three inversions.

In the structure before the Never-Disparaging chapter, prophecy was the Buddha's pedagogical act, and practice was the sentient being's act of enactment—the two belonging to different agents. In the Never-Disparaging chapter, **the act of prophecy is itself the bodhisattva's practice.** Never-Disparaging's bodhisattva path *is* the act of "prophesying for all sentient beings"—he has no other "real practice" standing behind it. Bowing and praising are his entire practice.

For every reader who has come this far, this carries a direct and very concrete implication—

**Universal reverence can be "done"; it is not only something to be "thought."**

Many people, on reading "all sentient beings have buddha-nature," treat it as a concept to be understood, agreed with, internalized. The Never-Disparaging chapter points to another road—**treat it as an action that can be performed.** Walk toward someone, bow to him deeply in your mind, and think: "**You will become a Buddha.**"

This is universal reverence. This is prophecy. This is one complete act of the bodhisattva path.

You do not need to have mastered Yogācāra first, do not need to have understood tathāgatagarbha first, do not need to have established a perfect right view first. You only need to do this action. **The doing itself is the practice.**

### A Contemporary Adjustment of Form

⚠️ This operationalized road needs one contemporary qualification.

Never-Disparaging's "bowing"—bending the body in a face-to-face bow—was a natural body-language in the setting of ancient India or China. But reproduced literally in contemporary society, it could cause its object distress. A stranger who suddenly bows to you on the street and says "you will become a Buddha" would unsettle most people rather than benefit them.

The contemporary execution of the Universal Reverence dharma-gate needs to **preserve the integrity of the inner stance while making appropriate adjustments to outer form.**

**Body-karma** need not be a face-to-face bow. It can be **reverence in the gaze**—looking at the other without appraisal, without contempt; it can be **respect in how you deal with them**—not cutting them off, not looking down on them, not objectifying them into a tool for your own ends; it can be **the basic posture of refusing to objectify the other**—whether they stand high or low, strong or weak, in the social order, in your presence they are simply themselves.

**Speech-karma** need not be the four words "you shall become a Buddha." It can be **any verbal act that does not demean, does not insult, does not mock, does not treat the other as an object.** It can be a "thank you for your hard work," it can be an ordinary greeting, it can be a listening that does not interrupt—the key is that the speech **acknowledges the other's fundamental dignity.**

**Mind-karma, however, must be preserved in full**—the contemplation in the mind that "**you fully possess buddha-nature**" cannot be discounted. This layer cannot be adjusted. However the outer form changes, the mind-karma's "I deeply revere you, you shall become a Buddha" must be there. Without the mind-karma, the most refined body- and speech-karma is mere performance; with the mind-karma, the body- and speech-karma can be utterly simple and lose none of their force.

The core principle of this contemporary adjustment is—**the form may change; the stance may not.**

### Kuiji's Confluence of Two Tracks—On a Single Sentence

This chapter must close with one doctrinal gathering-up.

Earlier chapters established the distinction between the *Lotus Sūtra*'s two great theoretical tracks—the tathāgatagarbha track and the Yogācāra track. The Devadatta chapter was the first confluence of these two tracks, the dragon-girl wing carried by the Yogācāra track (quantitative change, the compression of space and time), the Devadatta wing carried by the tathāgatagarbha track (qualitative change, the reversal of direction).

In the Never-Disparaging chapter, when Kuiji, in his *Profound Praise of the Lotus Sūtra*, comes to gloss this core line of scripture, he does something special—**on one and the same sentence, he mobilizes both tracks at once.**

His gloss on "I deeply revere you and dare not slight you; you all walk the bodhisattva path and shall attain Buddhahood" runs like this:

> "I deeply revere you and dare not slight you"—because they possess the inherent nature of a Buddha and abide in the lineage; **this reveres the tathāgatagarbha as saṃbhogakāya.** "You walk the bodhisattva path and shall attain Buddhahood"—because for those possessing the lineage, if they activate habituated nature and arouse the mind to practice, Buddhahood is necessarily attained. Furthermore, on the basis of the **tathāgatagarbha as dharmakāya**, all beings have my deep reverence.

The first half, "I deeply revere you and dare not slight you"—Kuiji reads as "revering the **tathāgatagarbha as saṃbhogakāya**." The object of reverence is the other's inherent tathāgatagarbha nature; this is the language of the **tathāgatagarbha track.**

The second half, "you walk the bodhisattva path and shall attain Buddhahood"—Kuiji reads as "for those possessing the **lineage** (*gotra*), if they activate **habituated nature** (*vāsanā*), and arouse the mind to practice, Buddhahood is necessarily attained." The lineage already present (cause), the habituated nature if aroused (condition), Buddhahood necessarily attained (fruit)—this is the complete cause-condition-fruit reasoning of the **Yogācāra track.**

Then Kuiji adds a line—**"Furthermore, on the basis of the dharmakāya tathāgatagarbha, all beings have my deep reverence"**—returning to the tathāgatagarbha track to gather it up.

One sentence of scripture, both tracks operating at once, each carrying its clearly divided task:

| Track | The question it carries | The answer it supplies |
|------|---------|---------|
| Tathāgatagarbha track | On **what grounds** may Never-Disparaging say "you shall become a Buddha" to all sentient beings? | Because all sentient beings inherently possess the tathāgatagarbha |
| Yogācāra track | Once the sentence is spoken, **what happens to the being**? | Hearing-perfuming arises, activating the uncontaminated seeds within the lineage |

The two tracks ask different questions, supply different answers, but **point to the same object**—Never-Disparaging's Universal Reverence practice.

Lacking the tathāgatagarbha track, universal reverence would be challenged as "groundless effusion"—on what grounds do you tell a stranger he will become a Buddha?
Lacking the Yogācāra track, universal reverence would be misread as "the magic of one sentence and a Buddha"—you say it and he becomes a Buddha?

Only when both tracks operate at once can the Universal Reverence dharma-gate stand.

The previous chapter, the Devadatta chapter, proved that the confluence of the two tracks exists—but that was a limit case, and might be read as a temporary patch-up for special circumstances. The Never-Disparaging chapter proves further—**confluence is not a temporary device, but the *Lotus Sūtra*'s standard structure for handling any utterance of "universal Buddhahood."** An ordinary monk's ordinary practice, and Kuiji, in glossing it, must automatically and densely mobilize both tracks. This proves that **the doctrinal structure of the *Lotus Sūtra* itself requires the dual track**, not as a commentator's after-the-fact patch.

This is the book's second confirmation of Thesis One of the Part Seven series.

The Never-Disparaging chapter handles the **universal gate as inner stance**—a practitioner who takes "universally revering all sentient beings" as his own inner practice. The direction of the universal gate is **reverence radiating outward from the practitioner.**

But the concept of "universal gate" has another face—**the universal gate as outward responsive manifestation.**

Not "I radiate reverence toward all sentient beings," but "the dharmadhātu radiates response toward all sentient beings in need of help." Not a subject radiating outward, but a field responding inward.

The next chapter handles the most widely circulated of the twenty-eight chapters of the *Lotus Sūtra*—the "Universal Gate of Bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara." Avalokiteśvara, responding to sound, manifesting according to the occasion, in thirty-two responsive bodies, reaching everywhere.

Never-Disparaging is "**I universally revere all sentient beings.**"
Avalokiteśvara is "**the dharmadhātu universally responds to all sentient beings.**"

Reading the two chapters together is what opens up the full spectrum of the concept of "universal gate."
