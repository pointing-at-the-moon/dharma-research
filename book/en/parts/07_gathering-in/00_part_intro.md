# Part Seven · Gathering-In · The Lotus Sūtra

The demon-revealing mirror has finished its work.

The previous part, the Śūraṅgama, walked all the way to the end and left behind a complete map of the terrain—fifty conjured cities along the way, a fine-grained genealogy of the five kinds of delusion, and one governing principle running through all five aggregates: *establish no ultimate position anywhere*. On that map, every place that looks like a destination is marked "not the end." The luminous signs are not it. The lightness and ease are not it. Even the clear comprehension of the dharma-body is not it. One conjured city after another is passed through.

And so a natural question rises to the surface—**if none of the way-stations is ultimate, then where is the ultimate?**

The Śūraṅgama does not open this question head-on. It leaves the answer to the next sūtra.

Among the Mahāyāna scriptures, the one that answers this question directly is the Lotus Sūtra.

The Lotus's answer is not another inventory of "which positions still need to be broken." It **positively establishes the one and only**. It unfolds the ultimate through two narrative structures: **opening the provisional to reveal the real**—the three vehicles once taught to beings of differing capacity (the *śrāvaka*, the *pratyekabuddha*, the bodhisattva) were all skillful means; in truth there is only the one Buddha vehicle. And **the conjured city and the treasure-place**—the towns the travelers see along the road are not the destination; the real treasure-place lies further ahead, and there is only one of it.

The Śūraṅgama says, in the negative form, "no way-station is ultimate." The Lotus says, in the affirmative form, "there is only one ultimate." The former breaks attachment; the latter reveals the real. Without the Śūraṅgama's negation, the reader would halt inside a conjured city. Without the Lotus's affirmation, the reader would lose the bearing of the treasure-place. These two sūtras are one set.

The word "gathering-in" is the name of this two-directional motion.

Turned inward, it is **gathering the three back into the one**—drawing the divided three vehicles back into the one Buddha vehicle, drawing every kind of skillful speech back into the same true mark of all dharmas, drawing the thousandfold differences of immeasurable beings back into the same buddha-nature. This is the gesture of contraction, of focusing, of coming home.

Turned outward, it is **radiation**—the same true mark unfolding in whatever form each being can receive. To this child the Buddha speaks of the burning house and the three carts; to that child, of the robe-jewel and the topknot-jewel. To this group he shows the wide reach of the medicinal herbs; for that group he conjures a city in which to rest. Each outward unfolding is not a drift away from the center. It is the same single appearing of the center along different radii.

**Gathering-in and radiation are two faces of one motion—both at work so that "the one" can become a destination every being is able to reach.**

The way this twelve-chapter part moves is an alternation between these two directions.

We enter through the ten suchnesses of the Skillful Means chapter, into the structure of the true mark itself. Then we come to the one great cause and to *opening, showing, awakening to, and entering*—the single reason the Buddhas appear in the world, the awakening loop of the one Buddha vehicle. After that come the Lotus's great parables—the burning house, the prodigal son, the medicinal herbs, the conjured city, the robe-jewel and the topknot-jewel—and each parable is a teaching shown forth upon the dependent nature. Skillful means is not a stopgap. It is the unfolding of the true mark itself.

After the parables come three chapters of prophecy and universal reverence—the Devadatta chapter, where the prophecy granted to an evil-doer and the dragon-girl's attainment of buddhahood are completed in "both wings displayed at once"; the bodhisattva Never-Disparaging, who turns prophecy from a statement in the text into an act of practice; and the Universal Gate chapter of Avalokiteśvara, which unfolds the responsive structure of compassion. The last two chapters close on the immeasurable lifespan of the Tathāgata in the Lifespan chapter, and on the handoff from Part Seven into Part Eight—**the Lotus is a star radiating outward; the Avataṃsaka is a black hole collapsing inward; the same true mark of all dharmas, presented as a pair in two directions.**

The posture of this part differs plainly from the one before it.

The Śūraṅgama's posture was **diagnosis**—pointing out that each place which looks like the end is in fact not the end. The Lotus's posture is **acknowledgment**—acknowledging, all at once, the provisional character of every way-station, and also naming directly that one ultimate position. The Lotus does not negate the Śūraṅgama's diagnosis. It only adds a layer on top of it: **skillful means, though not ultimate, is genuinely skillful means**—it arises out of the true mark of all dharmas, it carries the work of leading beings toward the ultimate, and once that work is done it naturally disappears.

Whoever has read the first six parts may easily come to read "form no notion of holiness" as a permanent suspicion—every experience must be questioned, every position must be broken down. That posture is a necessary protection while one is still passing through the fifty skandha-demons. But linger in that posture too long and it becomes an attachment of its own. What the Lotus does is, after you have walked through the Śūraṅgama's doubt, to **hand your confidence in the destination back to you**—not a return to the naïve misperception of "I have already arrived," but the confidence that, even after seeing clearly the provisional character of every single way-station, the one treasure-place truly exists, is truly reachable, and is truly the direction you have been walking toward all along from your own original awareness.

The true mark of all dharmas: inherently so.

The map in your hands, from the first part to the sixth, drew the shape of the road and the locations of its wrong turns. What this part draws is the road's direction—a direction that was always there within your original awareness, only that the first six parts, to keep you from halting on a wrong turn, left it for a while unpointed.

Now it can be pointed out.

The demon-revealing mirror has finished its work. The next mirror reflects the direction of the road home.
