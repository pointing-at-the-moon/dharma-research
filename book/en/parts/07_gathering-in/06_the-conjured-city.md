## 7.6 The Conjured City — Provisional Nirvāṇa and Ultimate Nirvāṇa

Anyone who has practiced for a long time eventually meets a particular stretch of road. It is not the bright excitement of the first aspiration, nor the clarity that follows some breakthrough. It is the middle stretch, the one where **you simply cannot go any further**.

In meditation the mind has settled, the body has loosened, and sometimes a very deep stillness arrives. And then a voice in the heart says: "This is enough. Stop here. The road ahead is too far. I have already gotten what I came for."

This is not laziness, and it is not backsliding. It is something subtler — something wearing the mask of "already arrived."

The "Parable of the Conjured City" chapter treats exactly this moment.

### A Dangerous Road of Five Hundred Yojanas

The setting of the story is extremely spare, but it is written with great composure.

The Buddha says:

> Suppose there is a dangerous, deserted road of five hundred *yojanas*, empty, without people, a place of terror. A great company wishes to cross this road to reach a place of treasure.

A group of travelers must cross five hundred *yojanas* — an enormous distance — and along the way lies "a dangerous, deserted road, empty, without people, a place of terror." They are going to fetch the treasure that lies ahead. They have a guide, of whom the sūtra says he is "intelligent, discerning, who knows well the passable and impassable signs of the dangerous road."

Halfway through, the travelers cannot bear it any longer. The sūtra puts it this way:

> The people he leads grow weary at mid-journey and say to the guide: "We are exhausted, and we are afraid; we cannot go further. The road ahead is still long. We wish to turn back."

This sentence can be read slowly. "Exhausted" — worn out to the very limit. "Afraid" — fear rising in the heart. "We cannot go further" — there is no walking on. "The road ahead is still long" — and there is still so far to go. "We wish to turn back" — I want to go home.

Every practitioner who has seriously walked a stretch of road has said something like this to himself at some point. It is not dramatic despair; it is utterly real exhaustion — the exhaustion of "I know the treasure is ahead, but I simply cannot go on."

The guide stands there, looking at this weary company. The sūtra says he is "**skilled in many expedients**," yet he pauses and reflects. And then:

> How pitiable they are! How can they abandon the great treasure and wish to turn back?

These people are so pitiable — how can they give up the great treasure like this and walk back?

That word "pitiable" is the emotional key of the whole passage. It is not reproach, not a stern demand, not "you should be stronger." It is **looking at the real exhaustion they feel in this very moment, and not wanting to let them simply turn back**.

### The Appearance of the Conjured City

The guide makes a decision:

> Having thought thus, by the power of expedient means, at the three-hundred-*yojana* mark on the dangerous road, he conjures a great city and tells the company: "Do not be afraid; do not turn back. Here now is a great city where you may rest and do as you please. If you enter this city, you will swiftly find ease. If you are able to go forward to the place of treasure, you may also depart."

On the five-hundred-*yojana* road, at the three-hundred-*yojana* mark, the guide uses the power of skillful means to **conjure a city**. He tells the company: Do not be afraid, do not turn back; here now is a great city, you may go in and rest, do as you please. Enter this city and you will swiftly find ease. And once you have rested enough, if you wish to continue on to the treasure-place, you may go.

Notice two details.

**The first**: the appearance of the conjured city is not a coerced command. The guide does not say "you will go in." He gives an option — you may go in, or you may keep walking; and once inside, whether or not you wish to set out again is also for you to decide.

**The second**: the city stands at the three-hundred-*yojana* mark — exactly three-fifths of the five hundred. Not at the four-fifths point near the end, not at the one-fifth point near the start, but landing precisely on the middle stretch of the journey, the place where collapse is most likely.

The company's response is this:

> At that moment the exhausted company rejoices greatly, marveling: "We are now delivered from this evil road; we have swiftly found ease." They then enter the conjured city, giving rise to the perception *we have crossed over* and the perception *we are safe*.

They rejoice, they marvel as at something never seen before, they give rise to the perception "we have crossed over" and the perception "we are safe." They believe they have finished the journey, that this is the destination. They enter the city, they really set down their burden, they really breathe out in relief, they really believe they have arrived.

This passage matters greatly. The sūtra does not say "they believed they had arrived, but in fact they had not," in some lofty, overhead tone of commentary. What the sūtra records is the real feeling of that very moment — rejoicing greatly, swiftly finding ease. **That joy is real, that ease is real, that rest is real.**

Then comes the pivotal turn of the story:

> At that time the guide, knowing the company has rested and is no longer weary, dissolves the conjured city and tells them: "Come, let us go. The place of treasure is near. The great city you saw, I conjured it, only for your rest."

The guide waits until the travelers have truly rested enough and their exhaustion is gone, and only then **dissolves the conjured city**. He tells them: Let us go; the treasure-place is just ahead, not far. That city you saw a moment ago, I conjured it — only to let you catch your breath.

"**The great city you saw, I conjured it, only for your rest**" — this one line is the axis of the whole parable. **It was conjured for the sake of rest, and the rest being done, it could be dissolved.** Not to deceive you, not to test you, not to provoke an attachment and then break it — simply to let you catch your breath. The breath being caught, you can walk on.

Then the Buddha joins the parable to its meaning:

> The Tathāgata is likewise. Being now your great guide, he knows that the evil road of birth-death and afflictions is long and dangerous, and that you must traverse and cross it. If beings hear only of the one Buddha-vehicle, they will not wish to see the Buddha, will not wish to draw near, and will think: "The Buddha-path is long; only after long and painful practice can it be accomplished." The Buddha, knowing their timid and inferior minds, **by the power of expedient means, for the sake of rest on the middle way, speaks of two *nirvāṇas***.

The key line in this joining is "**by the power of expedient means, for the sake of rest on the middle way, he speaks of two *nirvāṇas***." The two *nirvāṇas* are the *nirvāṇa* with remainder and the *nirvāṇa* without remainder, realized by the arhats. The reason the Buddha speaks of two *nirvāṇas* is not that there really exist two independent *nirvāṇas* — **it is that beings need a place where they can rest their feet**.

The verse passage compresses this thesis into five characters:

> The Buddhas, by the power of expedient means, distinguish and teach three vehicles; there is only one Buddha-vehicle — **two are spoken of as a place of rest**.

"Two are spoken of as a place of rest" — in order to provide a place to rest, two *nirvāṇas* are spoken of as a skillful means. Were it not for that rest, there would only ever have been the one Buddha-vehicle.

### The Raft — A Structural Seed Left by the Āgamas

The conjured city has no direct precursor in the Āgamas. You may comb through the entire canon for the two characters "conjured city" — in the Āgama corpus they appear not even once. The proposition "nirvāṇa is a conjured city" is itself a Mahāyāna innovation, treating a discursive task the Āgamas never explicitly undertook.

But the Āgamas do leave a seed that is structurally very close — **the raft**.

In the *Ariṣṭa-sūtra* of the *Madhyamāgama*, the Buddha says to the monks:

> Suppose there is a vast stretch of deep water, its current swift, carrying much along; there is no boat, nor is there a bridge. A man comes who must cross for some business on the far shore… He thinks: "Let me gather grass and wood on this bank, bind them into a raft, and cross by riding it." He gathers grass and wood, binds them into a raft, crosses and reaches the far shore safely.

A man must cross a river, and from this bank to the far one there is no boat and no bridge. So he gathers grass and wood along the bank, binds them into a raft, sits on it, and crosses over.

Then the Buddha asks a question: once across, what should this man do?

> He thinks: "This raft has served me well; riding this raft I have come safely from that shore to this one. Let me now carry it on my right shoulder, or bear it on my head, and go on."

The man thinks: this raft helped me greatly, it carried me across the river, so I should hoist it onto my shoulder, or balance it on my head, and take it along.

The Buddha's verdict is utterly composed:

> I have long taught you the dharma as a raft, meant to be let go, not to be grasped. If you understand that I have long taught the dharma as a raft, then **even the dharma must be let go — how much more so what is not dharma**?

I have always taught you this parable of the raft in order that you might **let go**, not in order that you might cling. If you have truly understood this parable of the raft, then even "the dharma" should be let go — how much more "what is not dharma"?

The corresponding passage in the *Ekottarāgama* compresses the conclusion into a single line: "**Even wholesome dharmas should be let go, how much more the unwholesome.**"

The raft and the conjured city are perfectly parallel in structure — the tool (raft / city) is an expedient provision toward a goal; the goal reached, the tool should be set down; to keep clinging to the tool becomes a new burden.

But there is one decisive difference between them. The raft breaks **attachment to teachings** — do not cling to the teaching itself. The conjured city breaks **attachment to attainment** — do not cling to the stillness of a fruit. The "letting go of the raft" is performed by the practitioner himself; the "dissolving of the conjured city" is performed by the Buddha. In other words — **the conjured city is the raft's logic specialized onto the deeper structure of "fruition."** The Āgamas leave the structurally isomorphic line of the raft, and the Mahāyāna pushes that line to its limit: not only teachings may be let go, but even a fruit may be conjured.

### Vasubandhu's Seven Parables — the Conjured City as the Precise Remedy for the "Person of Meditative Absorption"

Place the conjured city back inside Vasubandhu's seven-parable system in the *Lotus-śāstra*, and it has an extremely precise object of correction.

Vasubandhu maps the seven parables of the Lotus onto seven kinds of conceit, listed one by one. The conjured city is the **fourth parable**, correcting the fourth kind of being: the "**person of meditative absorption**." In Vasubandhu's own words:

> Fourth, the conceit of attainment where there is none: having worldly *samādhi* and *samāpatti*, **which are in fact not nirvāṇa, they give rise to the perception of nirvāṇa**, a mistaken grasping. To correct this, the parable of the conjured city is taught.

"Person of meditative absorption" — one who has a foundation in meditation. "Worldly *samādhi* and *samāpatti*" — worldly meditative experience and the states of entering absorption. "Which are in fact not nirvāṇa, they give rise to the perception of nirvāṇa" — there is as yet no nirvāṇa, yet the judgment arises, "I have already realized nirvāṇa."

This object of correction is exceedingly precise. The conjured city does not loosely correct "clinging to fruits," does not correct everyone who clings to liberation; it treats a **concrete and subtle blind spot in practice** — the person who has meditative experience, who has tasted a very deep stillness within an absorption, and who then mistakes that stillness for nirvāṇa.

Vasubandhu then gives a crucial definition: "**The nirvāṇa city is the city of the meditative absorptions.**"

In Vasubandhu's system the conjured city is directly equated with the "**city of the meditative absorptions**." What it corresponds to is not the broad fruit of the two vehicles but the concrete event of "**a meditative state being mistaken for nirvāṇa**." The corrective movement has two steps: "**Having passed that city, they are then led into the great nirvāṇa city**." First enter the city — taking meditation as a rest stop; then pass the city — transcending the subtle inversion of "taking absorption for nirvāṇa."

This precision matters especially for the modern practitioner. Anyone who sits for long will, at some stage, meet a very deep stillness — the body releases, thoughts subside, the sense of time grows vague, a certain "just-this" quality appears. At that moment there are two forks: one is to keep walking, treating this stillness as a rest stop on the road; the other is to stop, treating this stillness as the destination. **What the conjured city corrects is precisely the subtle clinging on this second fork.**

### The Seven Parables Are a Parallel Toolbox, Not a Cumulative System

Having come this far, we can look back at the structure built up in the previous chapters.

The burning house (7.3) corrects the "seeker of power" — the ordinary being who pursues worldly reward within saṃsāra and takes a better worldly position as the exit. The prodigal son (7.4) corrects the "śrāvaka of fixed determination" — the śrāvaka who is already liberated but believes his liberation equals the Buddha's. The medicinal herbs (7.5) corrects the "Mahāyāna of fixed determination" — the Mahāyāna practitioner who believes the three vehicles are fundamentally undifferentiated. The conjured city (7.6) corrects the "conceit of attainment where there is none" — the person of meditative absorption who has meditative experience but mistakes the absorbed state for nirvāṇa.

Reading this far, a natural tendency is to read these four parables as a **cumulative system of breaking attachment** — from the ordinary being, to the śrāvaka, to the Mahāyāna practitioner, to the person of absorption, as though there were an axis running deeper and deeper.

**But this reading is wrong.**

The structure of Vasubandhu's seven parables is not cumulative; it is parallel. The seven parables do not correct "seven ever-deepening levels of one and the same attachment"; they correct **seven entirely different types of attachment**. They do not sit on one axis; they are seven different drawers, each aimed at a different ailment.

| Parable | Object of correction | Type of problem |
|------|---------|---------|
| Burning house | Seeker of power | Taking saṃsāric reward as the exit |
| Prodigal son | Seeker of śrāvaka liberation | Taking the śrāvaka vehicle as ultimate (vehicle-clinging) |
| Medicinal herbs | Mahāyāna person | Using the Mahāyāna to dissolve all distinctions (teaching-clinging) |
| Conjured city | Person of meditative absorption | Taking a meditative state as nirvāṇa (state-clinging) |
| Bound jewel | Distracted, unsettled person | Forgetting the Mahāyāna roots (distraction-clinging) |
| Topknot jewel | Merit-accumulating person | Having merit but grasping in the wrong direction |
| Physician | Non-merit-accumulating person | Lacking the foundation of roots |

The first three parables treat **vehicle and teaching** — which vehicle am I in? Is the teaching I understand the universally-moistening one? The last three treat **merit and roots** — do I have the provisions? In what state are my provisions? The conjured city stands in the middle, treating an entirely different dimension — **state**: this absorbed state I experience, is it nirvāṇa?

This parallel character matters greatly for locating the conjured city precisely. **It is not the next stage after the prodigal son or the medicinal herbs; it is a correction in another dimension altogether.** Its intended reader is not the two-vehicle practitioner who "does not know the Mahāyāna exists," but the person of absorption who "has experience in meditation, has experience of absorbed states, yet mistakes the absorbed state for nirvāṇa." The first kind of person has not even sat down in that place yet; the second is truly seated in that place and needs to be gently raised up to walk on.

### The Conjured City Is a Real Expedient

Now we come to the deepest doctrinal position of the conjured city.

Someone who hears this parable will naturally ask a question: **if the conjured city is "conjured," does the nirvāṇa the śrāvakas realize still count as real?**

This question has two dangerous forks.

One is the **cruel reading**: the conjured city is a deception, the śrāvakas practiced for nothing — as though the Buddha lured people into the city to rest and then came to tell them, "sorry, that was fake." This reading writes off the entire labor of the śrāvaka path, and turns the Buddha into a well-meaning liar.

The other is the **erasure reading**: the conjured city just is the destination, śrāvaka nirvāṇa just is the ultimate — as though the act of "dissolving" could be ignored and only the prior "entering the city" preserved. This reading cancels the entire Mahāyāna teaching.

In the history of Lotus interpretation, the three most different traditions — Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, Tathāgatagarbha — arrive by separate roads at one and the same answer: the conjured city is **neither a deception nor a destination**. It is a **real expedient**.

---

**Jizang's Madhyamaka Path — "Nonexistent yet Suddenly There"**

In his *Fǎhuá yìshū*, Jizang glosses the word "conjured" in just six characters: "**Because it is nonexistent and yet suddenly there, it is called 'conjured.'**"

These few characters compress a complete Madhyamaka argument:

1. All dharmas are intrinsically without self-nature — this is the basic Madhyamaka position;
2. Because they are without self-nature, they can be provisionally established in accord with conditions — this is the "possibility of provisional establishment" within emptiness;
3. The conjured city, as such a provisional establishment, has no self-nature and yet has function — it really lets the company enter and rest;
4. Therefore the conjured city possesses **functional reality**, not **substantial reality**.

Jizang goes on: "**It is not really the ultimate, yet it is spoken of as ultimate — hence it is called 'conjured.' Because it is the same in the meaning of cutting off delusion and the same in realizing the unconditioned, it is called 'one.'**" The "provisional" of the conjured city does not lie in its function but in its position. The afflictions the two vehicles cut off are really cut off; the unconditioned they realize is really realized. The only place the conjured city is not real is in its "ultimacy" — it is not the final destination.

Seen through Madhyamaka's logic of provisional establishment, "provisional designation" is never a synonym for "lie." A provisional designation can be really functional — "cart" is a provisional name upon the assemblage of wheels, axle, and frame, but the cart named upon them really carries people. The conjured city is the same: it has no "conjured-city-essence" independent of the Buddha's power of skillful means, yet it really lets the weary travelers rest.

---

**Kuiji's Yogācāra Path — "Provisional Cessation, No Real Entity"**

Kuiji, from the angle of Yogācāra, gives the conjured city a more precise definition:

> Hence the Buddha speaks of establishment; **because it has no real entity, it is said to be "conjured"**… this is the parable of *nirvāṇa* with remainder. The two-vehicle *nirvāṇa* is a **provisional cessation**, hence "dissolving the conjured" — **it is not that the conjured city is really dissolved by supernatural power**.

This passage has several layers to unpack.

**First**: "the Buddha speaks of establishment; because it has no real entity" — the conjured city is not a physical city conjured up by psychic power, but a skillful means established by the Buddha through speech. It has no independent substance.

**Second**: "the two-vehicle nirvāṇa is a provisional cessation" — "analytical cessation" (*pratisaṃkhyā-nirodha*) is a Yogācāra term, meaning the truth of cessation disclosed through the discernment of wisdom. "Provisional cessation" means this: it is really disclosed by the wisdom of person-emptiness, and it really cuts off the afflictions of birth-death-by-distinctions; but its "cessation" reaches only to this level and has not yet reached the unabiding great nirvāṇa disclosed by dharma-emptiness. **The "provisional" is not "false"; it is "a positing in the ultimate"** — in the ultimate dimension it is functional and real, but it is not the final substance.

**Third**: "it is not that the conjured city is really dissolved by supernatural power" — this line is especially important. The "dissolving" of the conjured city is not the Buddha really making the city vanish by psychic power. **The "dissolving" of the conjured city is the very utterance of the Buddha's words "this city is conjured."** The moment the company hears "this city is not real, I conjured it," their grasping at the conjured city is deconstructed on the spot; this deconstruction needs no supernatural power, only the truthfulness of the speech. **The dissolving is not an action but a speech; not a retrieval but a disclosure.**

---

**The Awakening of Faith's Tathāgatagarbha Path — "Delusively Grasping at Nirvāṇa, Originally-Already Nirvāṇa"**

The *Awakening of Faith* gives a third path from the angle of Tathāgatagarbha:

> As for the view of a dharma-self: because of the dull faculties of the two vehicles, the Tathāgata taught them only person-emptiness. Because the teaching was not ultimate, they see the five aggregates as arising-and-ceasing dharmas, fear birth-and-death, and **delusively grasp at nirvāṇa**. How is this corrected? Because the self-nature of the five-aggregate dharmas is unarisen, there is then no ceasing; they are **originally-already nirvāṇa**.

The structure of this passage is perfectly isomorphic with the conjured city. Because the two vehicles are of dull faculties, the Buddha taught them only the level of "person-emptiness." As a result, having heard this, the two vehicles believe there really are five aggregates, arising and ceasing, fearful and to be fled, and so they **delusively grasp at nirvāṇa** — delusively grasping a "cessation" as the place they must go.

The correction in the *Awakening of Faith* is not "do not pursue nirvāṇa." The correction discloses a more fundamental fact — **the self-nature of the five aggregates is unarisen, hence there is no ceasing; they are originally-already nirvāṇa**. The self-nature of the five aggregates is itself neither arising nor ceasing, so there is no nirvāṇa that needs to be "attained"; nirvāṇa just is the beings' own original suchness-nature.

This structure runs perfectly parallel with the conjured city:

| Conjured city | Awakening of Faith |
|--------|--------|
| The Buddha, knowing beings are weary and timid, sets up a conjured city for their rest | The Tathāgata, knowing the two vehicles are of dull faculties, teaches only person-emptiness |
| The conjured city is a skillful establishment "for the sake of rest" | The two-vehicle nirvāṇa is the result of "teaching the not-yet-ultimate" |
| Beings enter the city, giving rise to the perceptions "we have crossed over" and "we are safe" | The two vehicles "fear birth-and-death, delusively grasp at nirvāṇa" |
| Dissolving the city leads toward the real treasure-place | Correction: self-nature unarisen, **originally-already nirvāṇa** |

The key in the *Awakening of Faith* lies in those words "**originally-already nirvāṇa**" — **the real treasure-place is not at some distance further ahead; it just is the beings' own original suchness-nature.** The two-vehicle nirvāṇa symbolized by the conjured city is "delusively grasped," not "delusively false" — the object of the grasping can itself be real (the stillness of fully cut-off distinctions), but the grasping that "takes this as the ultimate" is itself delusive. The correction does not cancel the stillness; it discloses the real treasure-place the beings have been carrying all along.

---

The three traditions — Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, Tathāgatagarbha — set out from three utterly different metaphysical starting points, and yet they completely agree on the conclusion "**the conjured city is a real expedient**." Jizang confirms its functional reality (nonexistent yet suddenly there); Kuiji confirms its positing in the ultimate (provisional cessation, no real entity); the *Awakening of Faith* confirms its structural isomorphism in the mode of correction (delusive grasping / originally-already). This cross-traditional convergence is itself worth marking — it shows that this thesis is not one school's biased reading but a structure confirmed in common by the Mahāyāna across its three major traditions.

### The Cart of the Burning House Is a Pointing; the City of the Road Is a Doing

Here we must draw a key distinction — the conjured city and the earlier burning house are not on the same level in the meaning of "skillful means."

In treating the burning house (7.3) we established the thesis that "skillful means is not deception." One of the main supports there was Jizang's judgment — **there were never any three carts outside the gate**. After the children emerge, they still have to ask their father for the three carts, and this act of "asking" proves there were never any physical three carts outside the gate. The three carts were a sentence the father spoke, not a promise of actual objects. The skillful-means structure of the burning house is a **pointing** — using words to point at a goal that gets the children to move.

The skillful-means structure of the conjured city is different. The conjured city is **really manifested** — the company really enters, really gets rest, really rejoices, really believes they have arrived. The skillful means of the conjured city is not a pointing but a **doing**. The guide really performs the act of conjuring the city. The conjured city bears a real function — letting the weary company really rest. Only after the rest is done does the Buddha tell them "this city is conjured."

This difference looks subtle, but it has enormous significance for understanding the concept of "skillful means."

| Parable | Form of skillful means | Difficulty of skillful means |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Burning house | Pointing (gestural fiction) | To make you move, I point at a goal |
| Conjured city | Doing (operative provision) | To let you rest, I really give you a functionally effective experience along the way, then dissolve it |

The skillful means of the burning house needs only a "point" — the father says "there are carts outside," and the children move. The skillful means of the conjured city needs a "doing" — the guide really manifests a city, the company really enters, really rests, really gives rise to the perception "we have crossed over." **The conjured city represents a higher-difficulty version of the concept of skillful means.** It requires of the Buddha's power of skillful means not merely a pointing at something, but the bearing of a two-step movement: "doing, then dissolving."

This also answers the question left at the close of the previous chapter, 7.5 — **does that stretch of rest still count as real practice?**

The answer is: it does. And not only does it count — **that stretch of rest is itself a necessary link in the practice.** The exhaustion is real, the need for rest is real, the rest the conjured city provides is real. Without that rest, the company would have turned back and never reached the treasure-place at all. The **functional effectiveness** of the conjured city is real — this is confirmed by all three traditions, Madhyamaka, Yogācāra, and Tathāgatagarbha. The non-ultimacy of the conjured city is also real — but "non-ultimate" does not equal "non-real."

### The Limit of a Single Restriction Strategy — Once More the Tension Between the Lotus and Yogācāra

The previous chapter treated one restriction strategy Kuiji applied to the Lotus-Yogācāra tension — limiting the addressees of the Lotus's one-vehicle teaching to "indeterminate-lineage beings who fell back and rose again with great-bodhi mind."

This restriction strategy holds with high consistency across the prodigal son, the medicinal herbs, and the conjured city. In the commentary on all three parables, Kuiji uses the same phrasing: "to draw in one class of indeterminate-lineage śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, causing them to enter parinirvāṇa by way of the Mahāyāna" — the Lotus does not universally embrace all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, but is spoken for those who **fell back and rose again with great-bodhi mind**. This maintains the internal consistency between the separate establishment of the five lineages and the Lotus one-vehicle: the five lineages still stand separately (the determinate-lineage śrāvaka never becomes a Buddha), and the Lotus one-vehicle is only a hidden intent spoken for one class among them (the indeterminate-lineage fallen-and-risen).

But there is a passage in the conjured city chapter that pushes this restriction strategy to its limit. In the section joining the dharma to the parable, the Buddha says:

> After my parinirvāṇa, there will be other disciples who have not heard this sūtra, who do not know and do not awaken to what the bodhisattvas practice, who will give rise to the perception of *parinirvāṇa* toward the merits they have obtained, and will enter *nirvāṇa*. **In other lands I shall become a Buddha, under a different name**…

The Buddha says: after my parinirvāṇa, there will still be disciples who have not heard this sūtra, who do not know the bodhisattvas' practice, who take themselves to have already attained extinction and entered nirvāṇa. **I will, in other lands, under a different name, again become a Buddha for them.**

This passage pushes the conjured city's promise to a very far place. These disciples who "give rise to the perception of parinirvāṇa toward the merits they have obtained" are, from the standpoint of the narrative, not necessarily the indeterminate-lineage "fallen-and-risen" — they may be determinate-lineage śrāvakas who never gave rise to the great mind at all, who walked only the śrāvaka path, and who believe they have already entered nirvāṇa. The Buddha's promise to them is "**in other lands I shall become a Buddha**" — even if in this lifetime they do not turn their minds, the Buddha will continue to guide them by skillful means across subsequent spans of space and time.

This passage poses an internal challenge to Kuiji's restriction strategy — **if the conjured city's referent is limited to the indeterminate-lineage fallen-and-risen, then which group are the disciples of the "in other lands I shall become a Buddha" passage?** If they too are indeterminate-lineage, then why must it wait for the more distant space and time of "becoming a Buddha in other lands"? If they are determinate-lineage, then the determinate-lineage two vehicles are also enclosed within the conjured city's promise — which is equivalent to saying that **the "determinacy" of the determinate-lineage two vehicles is also relative, and can be opened across a larger scale of space and time.**

Kuiji of course did not miss this passage. His way of handling it is "**speech with hidden intent**" — the Lotus one-vehicle is a hidden intent, not a definitive teaching; the separate establishment of the five lineages still holds; the conjured city's promise is an expedient with hidden intent and does not break the lineage doctrine. This is a logically self-consistent response, but its price is that the Lotus one-vehicle is read as a kind of "expedient upon an expedient" — the one vehicle is itself only a teaching device, not an ultimate position.

Continuing the stance of the previous chapter — **present the divergence without pretending to dissolve it**. Kuiji's internal consistency is real; with "speech with hidden intent" he maintains the coherence of his system. But the price this coherence must pay (reading the Lotus one-vehicle as hidden intent rather than as definitive) does stand in real tension with the Lotus's own discursive tone ("there is only one Buddha-vehicle; there are no two, nor are there three").

The conjured city is the point of deepest contact for this tension within the Lotus — with the single line "in other lands I shall become a Buddha," it stretches the scale of space and time to a place where Kuiji's restriction strategy strains. The reader must decide for himself: accept "speech with hidden intent" and preserve the separate establishment of the lineages, or let the Lotus's own universal statement rewrite the lineage doctrine. The work of this book is to set the conditions of that choice plainly on the table.

### That Very Deep Stillness — For the One Sitting Inside It

Having come this far, we can return to the moment named at the very start of this chapter.

Anyone who sits for long will, at some stage, meet a very deep stillness — the body releases, thoughts subside, the sense of time grows vague, a certain "just-this" quality appears. In that moment, the guidance the conjured city gives can be condensed into two sentences:

**First, that stillness is real; do not doubt its reality.**

The conjured city is a real expedient — the rest it provides is real rest, the relief it brings is a real breathing-out. Do not, because you have heard that "the conjured city is not the destination," begin to doubt whether the deep absorption in your sitting is "only a hallucination" or "actually useless." Jizang's "nonexistent yet suddenly there," Kuiji's "provisional cessation," the *Awakening of Faith*'s "originally-already nirvāṇa" — what all three traditions protect in common is exactly this: **what you gained in that stillness is real.** The afflictions the two vehicles cut off are really cut off, the stillness they realize is really realized, and the breath you caught in your sitting is really caught.

**Second, that stillness is not the destination; do not take up residence there.**

The conjured city is not a deception, but it is not the destination either. That "just-this" feeling will at some point say to you: "This is enough; stop here; the road ahead is too far; you have already gotten what you came for." — This voice is not an enemy; it is the gravity of the conjured city itself. It will not harm you, but if you believe it, you will settle down here and miss the bearing of the treasure-place.

The two sentences together make up the skillful means of "passing through the conjured city without clinging to the conjured city." The first protects you from falling into the nihilistic reading of "I gained nothing"; the second protects you from falling into the conjured-city reading of "I have already arrived."

⚠️ Here we must also correct a misreading especially easy to generate in modern times — reading the conjured city as a **psychological placebo**. This misreading likens the Buddha to a well-meaning therapist who prescribes a pleasant-feeling medicine and, once the patient has recovered, tells him it was a placebo. But the conjured city is not a psychological operation at the level of subjective feeling. The two-vehicle *nirvāṇa* with remainder to which the conjured city corresponds is — in the Yogācāra classification of the four *nirvāṇas* — the unconditioned of **analytical cessation**, with a real ontological status. What the conjured city corrects is not psychological distress but **precise positional guidance on the path of practice**. The cutting-off of delusion and the stillness you experience in that stretch of quiet are a real realization, not a hallucination; it is only that this position is not the highest position.

⚠️ Another misreading to correct — reading the dissolving of the city as "**the Buddha taking back a gift he gave earlier**." This reading reads the Buddha as a personal subject who "gives" and "takes back," and reads the conjured city as an object granted and then revoked. But the conjured city was never an object; it is an establishment. **The dissolving is not an action but a speech; not a retrieval but a disclosure** — the very sentence "this city is conjured" is the entire content of the dissolving. The breath you caught inside the conjured city will not vanish because of this sentence; the only thing that changes is the illusion that "the conjured city is the destination."

The conjured city treats a **provisional destination on the road** — a rest that is real but not ultimate, offered to the weary traveler.

But there is a deeper question still. If the conjured city is the mid-road expedient the guide provides, then **what of the treasure-place itself**? The treasure-place is where we are going, but what is its relation to us? Are we **going to fetch a treasure we did not have before**, or **going to recognize a treasure that has always been ours**?

The *Awakening of Faith* has already given a hint — "originally-already nirvāṇa." The real treasure-place is not at some distance further ahead; it is the beings' own original suchness-nature. But this hint is only conceptual. To display it dramatically requires another parable.

The next chapter treats the two gentlest of the Lotus's seven parables — **the jewel in the robe and the jewel in the topknot**. In one, a friend, drunk, does not know there is a jewel sewn in his robe; in the other, a wheel-turning king has a jewel in his topknot and, only at the critical moment, takes it down to bestow. Both parables point at one and the same thing — **it was already there; you simply did not know.** The prodigal son treated, once, the recognition of an identity; the jewel in the robe and the jewel in the topknot treat the disclosure of the **thing already possessed** behind that identity.

This disclosure is the last layer of the Lotus's parables.
