## 2.11 Three Practitioners, One Sūtra: The Integration of Exoteric and Esoteric

The previous chapter brought us all the way to the bodhisattva's fruition—mind without obstruction, traversing terror, ultimate nirvāṇa. The individual arc of awakening had closed.

Then the Heart Sūtra did not stop. It rose one level higher:

> All Buddhas of the three times, relying on prajñāpāramitā, attain anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi.

A bodhisattva relying on prajñā to attain nirvāṇa is one person's awakening. All Buddhas of the three times relying on prajñā to attain supreme, perfect awakening is the shared testimony of every being who has ever become fully enlightened. Kuiji divided these two passages into "causal reliance" and "fruition reliance"—the bodhisattva severing the two obstructions to attain nirvāṇa is the benefit at the causal stage; the Buddhas perfectly realizing bodhi is the benefit at the fruition stage. Nirvāṇa is contained within bodhi. This is not switching to a different road; it is the same prajñā road walked to greater depth.

### Prajñā Is the Mother

In the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*, Nāgārjuna answered a challenge: there are so many Buddhist teachings—why single out prajñā as the most important?

His answer was not sentimental. Prajñā is the "mother"; samādhi is the "father." The father can collect the mind and bring about concentration, but he cannot independently perceive reality. The mother can comprehensively contemplate the true marks of all dharmas; there is nothing she cannot reach. In the project of awakening, prajñā's merit is the greater, and so it is called the mother.

The same treatise pushes the metaphor further: prajñā gives birth to Buddhas, and Buddhas benefit beings—so prajñā is "the grandmother of all sentient beings." This is not a metaphor of bloodline but a causal chain: if there were no prajñā, there would be no Buddhas; if there were no Buddhas, beings would have no opportunity to hear the Dharma. At a deeper level, prajñā is not only the mother but also the teacher—the mother gives birth, the teacher guides.

Finally, the verdict of the treatise's closing fascicle is the most ultimate of all: "When prajñā exists in the world, it is as if the Buddha exists." Prajñā is present, and so the Buddha is present—because prajñā is the root of all benefit. The Buddha's physical body enters parinirvāṇa, but prajñā does not cease. As long as someone hears it, retains it, and reflects on it with right attention, prajñā remains.

Note carefully: "mother" here is a functional judgment, not a metaphor of gender. Samādhi is called "father" because of its specific function (collecting the mind into concentration), not because it is "male." To read "prajñā, mother of all Buddhas" through frameworks of reproduction or gender obscures the actual doctrinal content of the text.

This circles back to the very first words of the Heart Sūtra, *prajñāpāramitā* (which we took apart in 2.1). Prajñā is not merely the subject of Part II; it is the single guide of the entire path from beginning to end—from the first thought of a person's initial aspiration to the perfect awakening of all Buddhas of the three times, the tool is the same.

### The Four Mantras—Four Functions of One Prajñā

"The great spirit mantra, the great illumination mantra, the unsurpassed mantra, the unequalled mantra"—these are not four different mantras but four dimensions of the same prajñā.

The great spirit mantra—prajñā's function is boundless; ask it anything and it illuminates. The great illumination mantra—prajñā is light, and no darkness escapes its reach; the ignorance discussed in 2.8 is precisely what it counteracts. The unsurpassed mantra—no dharma is higher than prajñā; giving, morality, patience, vigor, and meditation, the other five pāramitās, are merely conditioned merit once they leave prajñā. The unequalled mantra—"Buddhas are called the unequalled; they are born from prajñāpāramitā, and therefore it is called the unequalled-equalling"—prajñā equals the Buddha.

The comprehensive contemplation of 2.4 through 2.9, the illumination of ignorance in 2.8, the "without wisdom and without attainment" of 2.9, the ultimate nirvāṇa of 2.10—the four mantras compress all of Part II into four phrases.

### Gate—Exoteric and Esoteric in the Same Sūtra

The final utterance of the Heart Sūtra is not doctrinal analysis but a Sanskrit mantra:

> gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā.

Fazang's attitude toward this line is fascinating. He first says it "cannot be interpreted"—this is the secret speech of all Buddhas, beyond the understanding of those at the causal stage; one should simply recite and retain it. Then he proceeds to "forcibly interpret" it word by word: *gate* means to cross; *pāra* means the other shore; *saṃ* means together and universally; *bodhi* means the shore of great awakening; *svāhā* means swift accomplishment.

**It cannot be translated, yet he translated it anyway**—and this very tension is the relationship between exoteric and esoteric. Doctrinally it can be explained; functionally it should not be. Reading it word by word is "exoteric"; reciting it in a single breath, so that it plants itself directly into the ālaya-vijñāna, is "esoteric." Fazang even gave a clear division of labor: "Explicit and clear speech generates wisdom-understanding and extinguishes the obstruction of afflictions; the secret words of the mantra, through recitation, generate merit and extinguish the obstruction of karmic offenses." Exoteric teaching extinguishes the affliction-obstruction; esoteric teaching extinguishes the karmic-offense-obstruction—the twofold adornment of wisdom and merit, both completed by the single Heart Sūtra.

A misunderstanding easily arises here: the supposition that esoteric teaching is higher than exoteric. It is not. Exoteric and esoteric are two gates, not two storeys—the underlying principle is one and the same; only the entries differ. Some people awaken through contemplating the doctrine; some enter through reciting and retaining the total formula. The roads differ; the destination is one and the same place.

### Three Practitioners

The same Heart Sūtra of 260 characters does three different things in the hands of three different people.

**The beginner: to recite is to plant seeds.** Someone recites the Heart Sūtra today for the first time. They do not understand what the five aggregates are, do not understand what "form is emptiness" is saying. When they finish, they only feel their heart is a little more at ease. It looks as though nothing happened.

In fact something of great importance happened—in the ālaya-vijñāna, a dharmakāya seed was planted. The *Mahāyānasaṃgrahabhāṣya* defines it: "The ear-consciousness hears sounds, triggering the mind-consciousness to arise; relying on words and sentences, it discriminates their meaning; through repeated practice, a capacity is generated; holding and not forgetting—this is called hearing-perfuming." Recite once, and a batch of seeds enters the ground. Regardless of whether one "understands," the ear-consciousness is already hearing, the mind-consciousness is already engaging. To recite is merit; to recite is to plant seeds. To have the conditions meet, the fortune to hear this teaching, the wisdom to be willing to come and recite this once—this in itself is no small thing.

**The intermediate practitioner: untying one's own knots.** The intermediate practitioner begins to contemplate the meaning of the text. "Form is emptiness"—are the things I cling to really so solid? "Feeling, perception, formations, consciousness are also like this"—are my emotions and habitual reactions also just conditions assembled? The whole series from 2.1 through 2.10 was the road paved for the intermediate practitioner. But the road is the road; whether you walk it is your own business. The knots were tied by oneself and must be untied by oneself—no one can illuminate the emptiness of the five aggregates on another person's behalf.

**The advanced practitioner: liberating while reciting.** The advanced practitioner no longer "uses" the Heart Sūtra but "lives" it. When they recite "Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva," contemplation is already happening; when they recite "practicing deep prajñāpāramitā," prajñā is already operating; when they recite "illuminated the emptiness of the five aggregates," illumination is occurring in that very moment. Each recitation is a clearing of dust from the mirror's surface—not removing something foreign, but allowing what has always been pure to appear.

⚠️ The three practitioners are not three identities but three states. The same person, at different times, flows among the three. Someone who has practiced for years, meeting an intense affliction, may temporarily return to the beginner—reciting only for the sake of peace. Someone newly come to the Heart Sūtra, with exceptionally favorable conditions, may in a single instant enter the advanced practitioner's contemplation directly. Do not treat the three as a staircase; treat them as three gates.

### The Dharma-Eye

Imagine something. You have a pair of eyes. Every day you open them, and the world is there—light, color, the faces of others. You never think this is remarkable, because you have never lost it.

Now imagine: your eyes fail. You know what the world looks like, you remember every face, but you cannot see. At that moment, how much would you pay to restore your sight? Everything you have. Because suddenly you know—eyes are priceless.

Prajñā is the eye of the mind. In ordinary times you do not sense its value. When suffering arrives—aging, illness, loss, confrontation with death—the deep terror in your heart cannot be escaped, and you realize that what you lack is not external protection but the capacity to see reality as it is.

The Diamond Sūtra makes this point repeatedly through a single structure: is the merit of giving seven treasures in charity great? It is. Yet if someone receives and retains even four lines of verse and explains them to others, "that merit surpasses the former."

Why? Vasubandhu's verdict is precise—the merit of seven-treasure charity has only "accumulative meaning," while receiving and retaining the teaching has "progressive meaning." Seven treasures can only be piled up; however high the pile, it will not walk itself toward awakening. The dharma-eye, however, carries a person forward. When a beginner recites the Heart Sūtra once, they are planting a pair of dharma-eyes.

People have eyes to see the world and do not know their worth. Only in losing them do they know they are priceless. Prajñā is the eye of the mind. All three practitioners are polishing the same dharma-eye—the beginner plants the seed in the ground, the intermediate practitioner lets the seed sprout, the advanced practitioner lets the dharma-eye open fully.

### Gate, Gate

From 2.1 to 2.11, eleven chapters have walked through all 260 characters of the Heart Sūtra.

What is the starting point? Not ability, but the power of aspiration—are you willing to recite the Heart Sūtra this once? In the instant you are willing, the conditions are complete, the seed enters the ground, the dharma-eye begins to focus. This is exactly what 1.1 said—a single aspiration, and the shared karma is already in motion. From the trust of Part I to the understanding of Part II, from initial aspiration to prajñā, from "can I walk?" to "what do I see?"—a single line.

Cross over, cross over, cross over to the other shore; let us all cross over to the other shore together; awakening, accomplished.

---

The Heart Sūtra gave us a pair of eyes. But eyes only see—and after seeing, how are the feet to walk?

The five aggregates have been illuminated, but how many parts are at work inside them? The twelve links of dependent origination have been illuminated and broken open, but how do the mental factors—the parts of the mind—at each link cooperate and come apart? Even prajñā itself has been set down—and then what?

The "then" requires a precise map of mind and consciousness. Yogācāra takes the mind apart into eight layers, sorts its functions into fifty-one, and gathers all dharmas into one hundred—not to show off, but to let the practitioner know where each step lands and how each thought operates. Part II gave the perspective; Part III will give the coordinates. Spread that map open, and it is the world of the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*.
