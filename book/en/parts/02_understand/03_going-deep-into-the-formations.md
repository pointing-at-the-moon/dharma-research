## 2.3 Going Deep: Inward, Into the Formations

On the bus, when someone else's child is being noisy, most people feel annoyed.

Back home, when their own child is being noisy, the very same person may not feel annoyed at all—more likely they'll set aside whatever they were doing and go see what's wrong. The same sound, the same scene, yet the response is completely different.

The difference is not in the child. It is in the layer where the response arises. Caring for one's own child is already running at a deeper, more automatic level; there is no need to first run through the whole program of "I should be patient with my child"—it is simply already there. With someone else's child, that layer is missing, so irritation rises first, without ceremony.

This is a worldly example, but it points to something crucial: **a person's response patterns have layers.** Some responses live in the shallow layer and need the will to step in before they fire; some live in the deep layer and come out directly, with no will required. One of the core concerns of practice is to let the correct response sink, little by little, from the shallow layer down to the deep—to make prajñā no longer a thought that must be remembered, but a default way of seeing the world.

The previous chapter noted that "going deep" has three dimensions: practice-depth, object-depth, and transformation-depth. The first two were already settled by Kuiji; the third is the book's extension—the depth to which prajñā permeates the practitioner's own being. What this chapter sets out to do is open up that third dimension and look inside.

---

### What the Aggregate of Formations Is

To understand this chapter, we first have to get clear on this term: the aggregate of formations.

Among the five aggregates, form, feeling, perception, and consciousness are relatively easy to identify: the body is form, sensations are feeling, thoughts are perception, the act of cognizing is consciousness. And the aggregate of formations (*saṃskāra*)? It is the subtlest of all—it is the response made before you have time to think, the leaning of the will, the momentum of habit, that thing where "I haven't decided yet, but my body has already moved this way."

The *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* sums it up in a single word: **creation**. The aggregate of feeling is "position" (the present state of a sensation), the aggregate of perception is "discrimination" (sorting sense-data into categories), the aggregate of formations is "creation" (creating, driving, setting in motion), and the aggregate of consciousness is "grasping and holding." The aggregate of formations is the creative face of the mind.

The aggregate of formations is governed chiefly by **cetanā**, the mental factor of volition. In Yogācāra, "cetanā" does not mean "to think something over" in the everyday sense; its nature is "causing the mind to create"—the very momentum that drives the mind to do a particular thing. In the instant of giving, cetanā is doing the driving; in the instant of anger, cetanā is doing the driving too. Wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral creation are all the work of cetanā.

And here is the crucial point: cetanā is one of the five omnipresent mental factors, and it **definitely corresponds with the ālaya-vijñāna**. This means that every time it operates, it perfumes a corresponding seed into the ālaya-vijñāna. Every act of creation rewrites the deep leanings. Hold your temper once today, and the anger-seed weakens by a fraction; indulge it once today, and the anger-seed strengthens by a fraction.

This is what the aggregate of formations is—a stream of creation that is ceaselessly perfuming seeds.

---

### The Ceiling of Perception-Level Prajñā

Once the aggregate of formations is clear, we can see where most practitioners get stuck.

For the great majority of people, prajñā stops at the perception level. They can read the sūtras, they can discuss them, and afterward they can even reflect, "Ah, that was my attachment." But every time prajñā needs to be put to use, it must first be started up in the head: "Time to be compassionate," "Don't get angry," "This is impermanent, don't cling." Every time it has to be remembered; every time it is a small battle of willpower.

The problem is that the sudden situation does not wait. The phone rings, a message flashes, someone cuts in line, the child cries—and the response is often already out the door before the thought "I should be compassionate" has even risen. By the time perception starts up, the deed is already done, and all that's left is to regret it afterward.

This is the ceiling of perception-level prajñā: however deep the understanding, so long as it still has to be deliberately remembered before it can be used, it will always run slower than that response which had no time to think. It is on a different track—the one deeper than perception, called the aggregate of formations.

---

### The Hijacking by Manas

Perception-level prajñā harbors one more crisis, more hidden still, that can pull a person in deep without their noticing.

After practicing for a long while, something deeper takes over—**manas** (the seventh consciousness), the consciousness that perpetually deliberates and grasps "self" as "me." Manas hijacks wholesome dharmas.

How does it happen, concretely? The motivation quietly slips its position. The compassionate act is still being done, but the underlying aspiration drifts from "for the sake of sentient beings" to "to prove that I am a compassionate person." On seeing someone in suffering, the first thought is no longer "what's wrong with them?" but "this is an occasion to display compassion." Conceit has put on the robes of compassion, so seamlessly that one can't even recognize it oneself.

Why is this so especially hard to detect? Because perception clearly knows "one should not grasp the self"—it can lay out the reasoning perfectly. But manas's perpetual deliberation is a creative pattern at the formations level; it is not on the same line as perception. However strong perception-level prajñā becomes, so long as it has not yet descended into the aggregate of formations, manas's self-grasping keeps running underneath, digesting the surface wholesome deeds as so much raw material.

This is the second tier of the perception-level ceiling: **however strong the wholesome thought, so long as it is still deliberately launched by consciousness, it will be captured by manas.**

To get around this barrier, there is no other road—prajñā must sink down into the aggregate of formations.

---

### Transformation-Depth: Prajñā Sinking Into the Formations

Back to the opening scene. A mother's care for her own child needs no summoning—tell her "you should take good care of your child," and she'll likely reply, "I don't need reminding, does that even need saying?" Her attentiveness to that child is already in her bones, running at the formations level, with no need for perception to first start up the program "I should be patient."

⚠️ This is a worldly example, and it must be read with care. In Buddhist psychology, a mother's instinctive response to her own child is in fact usually classed under "craving" (because it is circumscribed by manas to "my child," operating only toward one's own child and not necessarily toward others'). It is not compassion in the ultimate sense. But this example carries one important structural meaning: **it proves that the creative patterns of the aggregate of formations can be rewritten.** Once a given response sinks down to the formations level, it runs automatically, with no need for the will to intervene. So it is in the worldly sphere, and so it is in the supramundane—this is exactly the third dimension of going deep, and exactly the structure prajñā means to make use of.

The ultimate measure of "going deep," then, is not how precise the understanding is at the perception level, but whether the aggregate of formations has been steeped through with prajñā. When "form is emptiness" is no longer a formula to be silently recited but the perspective that automatically comes along when you look at things; when the contemplation of impermanence is no longer an after-the-fact consolation but a reading that appears in sync the instant a situation arises; when compassion is no longer "I should" but is already there in the very second the response rises—that is what deep truly means.

---

### The Eighth Bhūmi's Effortless Action: The End Point of Transformation

How far does this road run? The *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s Ten Stages chapter gives the answer: **the eighth bhūmi, the Immovable Ground (Acalā).**

The sūtra records the marks of the eighth bhūmi: "**Relinquishing all effortful practice, attaining effortless dharma; the activities of body, speech, and mind all cease; abiding in retributive action.**" The most crucial phrase is "abiding in retributive action"—compassion, wisdom, and the bodhisattva's conduct are no longer will-driven behavior but the natural operation of the karmic fruit of aeons of practice. Not "I am going to do it," but "it simply happens this way."

From the first bhūmi to the eighth, compassion travels a long arc. At the first bhūmi, the Joyful Ground, "seeing beings amid such masses of suffering, one thereupon gives rise to great compassion"—there is still a "seeing" first, and only then "thereupon gives rise to great compassion": the trace of cognitive triggering remains. At the sixth bhūmi, the Directly-Facing Ground, compassion and wisdom begin to fuse. The seventh bhūmi, the Far-Reaching Ground, is the threshold: "though well cultivating the three samādhis of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, yet compassion does not abandon sentient beings"—already longing for effortlessness, but not yet arrived. At the eighth bhūmi, the qualitative change is complete.

The *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* uses a seafaring simile to make this qualitative change plain: "It is like boarding a ship to enter the great ocean: before reaching the ocean, much effort is required; once at sea, one simply goes with the wind, requiring no human effort." Before reaching the open sea, the oars and the paddling all depend on human strength; once on the open sea, the sail unfurls and the ship runs on its own. One day of effortless practice surpasses a hundred years of effortful practice—this is not rhetorical exaggeration but a precise description of the qualitative change: when prajñā has gone so deep that the act of "I am practicing" is no longer needed, the internal friction vanishes, and efficiency undergoes a fundamental shift.

The *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*'s chapter on ear-faculty perfect penetration describes Avalokiteśvara's state after attaining the fruit: "Above, I merge with the fundamental, wondrous awakened mind of all Tathāgatas of the ten directions, **sharing the same power of compassion**; below, I merge with all beings of the six destinies in the ten directions, **sharing the same longing of compassion**." Those two words, "the same," say through to the end what the end point of "going deep" is—compassion is no longer something "done," but a oneness, at the level of being, with the compassion of all Buddhas and the suffering of all beings. Avalokiteśvara does not feel he is doing compassion, just as water does not feel itself flowing downward.

---

### The Reverse Side of Freedom: The Contaminated Seeds Are Exhausted

But here there is a reverse side, easily overlooked yet utterly crucial.

The reason Avalokiteśvara is "free" is **not that he has cultivated some power for suppressing afflictions, but that every seed capable of harming beings has been thoroughly cleared away.**

The precise logic of seed-theory: a manifestation can arise only if it has a seed as its direct cause. If the ālaya-vijñāna no longer holds the seed of anger, no seed of harm, then however strong the external conditions—someone berating, attacking, threatening—they cannot stir up the corresponding negative manifestation. Not "holding it back from going off," but simply having nothing left that could be stirred up.

Back to the mother example: someone else's child crying brings up irritation because an external condition stirs the anger-seed. One's own child crying brings up no irritation because, within this specific range, that seed is overridden by the seed of care. Avalokiteśvara feels no irritation toward any being at all, because that seed simply no longer exists in him.

This states "going deep" precisely from the reverse side: prajñā penetrating the aggregate of formations is not only "installing" the new firmware of compassion but, at the same time, "deleting" the old firmware of contaminated seeds. The two faces of transformation of the basis—relinquishing *parikalpita* and attaining *pariniṣpanna*—must be seen together. To look only at "attaining" and not at "relinquishing" is to mistakenly suppose that practice lays a layer of prajñā on top of the afflictions already there; the real situation is that, in the very process of prajñā going deep, the afflictive seeds are weakening in step, until they are exhausted.

---

### How to Walk It

⚠️ Don't picture this road as a one-time sudden-enlightenment event. The sūtras and treatises say the first bhūmi to the eighth takes the second incalculable great aeon—not to frighten you, but to say that each correct act of creation is only a minuscule shift in the ālaya-vijñāna: one fraction of contaminated seed lost, one fraction of pure seed gained. Only accumulation day by day reaches the qualitative change. Sudden enlightenment is the fruit of gradual cultivation, not a substitute for it.

⚠️ And don't go astray. The "deep" of going deep is the penetration-depth of prajñā, not the intensity of suffering. Asceticism is a deviation that Mahāyāna Buddhism explicitly rejects—the Buddha attained awakening beneath the Bodhi tree precisely after abandoning six years of austerities. There is no need to make yourself suffer on purpose in order to "temper" the aggregate of formations; what is needed is correct contemplation and sustained right awareness, letting prajñā rewrite, bit by bit, the default patterns of the formations within daily creation.

The entry point for practice is actually quite plain—**observe the very instant the thought "I want to do this" arises.** If you can see it in that instant, prajñā begins to seep into the aggregate of formations. If you can't, the creation goes straight past, and there is no time for anything.

The next chapter takes up the following link in the loop. Once going deep has truly descended into the formations, what is then seen? "Illuminated the emptiness of the five aggregates"—this is exactly the question that line of the Heart Sūtra sets out to answer.
