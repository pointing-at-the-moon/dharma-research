## 2.6 The True Mark of All Dharmas — This Mind Is Unborn and Unceasing

The four-line teaching of form and emptiness laid the entrance bare. But arriving here, the Heart Sūtra makes a fundamental pivot.

Everything before this—from Avalokiteśvara's practice, to the illumination that the five aggregates are empty, to the four lines on form and emptiness—has been deconstruction. The substantiality of form is dissolved; the self-nature clung to in feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness is broken; one appearance after another is seen through. But deconstruction is not the goal of prajñā. If the terminus of prajñā were merely "everything is empty," that would be just one more attachment, with emptiness swapped in for form. What prajñā truly points to is **that which cannot be deconstructed.**

Here the Heart Sūtra pivots. The Buddha calls out Śāriputra's name—in the oral tradition, calling someone's name is the most solemn form of awakening—and then delivers the sūtra's most essential pronouncement:

> Śāriputra, the true mark of all dharmas is: no arising, no ceasing; no defilement, no purity; no increase, no decrease.

Six negations, three pairs, eighteen characters. These eighteen characters are not describing a philosophical concept. **They are pointing directly at the original face of this very mind.**

---

### The Empty Mark Is Not the Appearance of Emptiness

The first thing easily misread is the phrase "empty mark."

Most readers, meeting these words, picture "the look of emptiness"—as if "emptiness" were a thing with an appearance, and the Buddha were telling them what it looks like.

The Yogācāra tradition cleans up this misreading beautifully. In the *Youzhan*, Kuiji cites an old verse to make it clear:

> Because there is no duality yet there is its absence, it is neither existent nor non-existent, neither different nor identical—this is called the empty mark.

"Empty mark" is not the shape of emptiness; it is **the true mark of all dharmas**—which in Yogācāra terms is tathatā, suchness. All dharmas are manifested by consciousness, and the "suchness" of consciousness—that nature in it which does not change, does not lean, is not stained by any appearance—is precisely the empty mark.

To put it another way: **empty mark = true mark of dharmas = tathatā.**

The six negations that follow are all describing the qualities of this empty mark, this tathatā. Three pairs of negation, from three different angles, make clear what this empty mark is.

---

### No Arising, No Ceasing — Tathatā Was Never Made

The first pair answers a question of existence: **what is** this mind-essence?

The answer: it was not made. From the very beginning it does not arise, and so it can never cease.

Picture a beautiful analogy for this line—**outer space.** In space, form-things arise and pass away every day: stars are born, stars die; clouds gather, clouds scatter; all things rise and fall within that vastness. But space itself—the very room that holds all of this—has never been born and has never perished.

Tathatā is this space. The dependently arisen dharmas—all the phenomena born of causes and conditions—arise and cease within it, but tathatā, as the true nature of all those phenomena, **was from the very beginning unborn, and so the question of its ceasing never even arises.**

Said from another angle, it is the same: when form gathers from conditions, true emptiness is not born along with it; when form scatters from conditions, true emptiness is not destroyed along with it. The arising and ceasing of form is form's own affair; from start to finish, true emptiness was never written into the script of arising and ceasing.

⚠️ Here there is a crucial Middle Way correction. The trap most easily fallen into on reading "no arising, no ceasing" is to hear it as "a permanently unchanging entity"—a "constant." This is exactly the eternalist view that Buddhism most severely criticizes. The Heart Sūtra's "no arising, no ceasing" does not say there is something that "exists forever"; it says there is something that **never entered the game of arising and ceasing in the first place.** Not "deathless longevity," but "never within the domain of birth, aging, sickness, and death." The former is eternalism; the latter is prajñā. The space analogy avoids this trap precisely: space is untouched by arising and ceasing not because it is "so solid it cannot break," but because **there is nothing there to break.**

---

### No Defilement, No Purity — The Essence Transcends the Defilement-Purity Polarity

The second pair answers a question of value: does this mind-essence **have any good or bad, any dirt or cleanness**?

The answer is no. Defilement and purity are functional states of consciousness, not the nature of tathatā.

This is the line of the six that practitioners most easily misread. The misreading is one nearly every practitioner has committed: supposing that one's mind is now defiled, and that after practice it will become pure—as if washing a dirty rag, scrubbing and scrubbing until the rag turns clean.

Extend the same space analogy here: in space there are defiled and pure form-things—this cloud is clean, that cloud is dirty—but space itself is neither defiled nor purified. Likewise: dharmas may have defilement and purity, but the empty mark has neither.

Then comes a verse that recurs throughout the sūtras and treatises:

> Neither defiled nor not defiled, neither pure nor not pure—because mind-nature is originally pure, [yet] defiled by adventitious dust.

Hidden between these four lines is a precise distinction of essence and function. The first two lines speak of the **essence**—tathatā itself is neither defiled nor pure, transcending the polarity entirely. The latter two lines speak of the **function**—mind-nature is originally pure (this is the essence), yet defiled by adventitious dust (this is the defiled aspect within function). The adventitious dust is called a "guest" precisely because it is not the host.

⚠️ The Dharmapāla-Xuanzang line guards this boundary strictly. The ālaya-vijñāna (the name of the eighth consciousness in its causal stage) does, on account of perfuming, have a functional defilement and purity—when defiled seeds are strong it shows a defiled aspect, when pure seeds are strong it shows a pure aspect. But **tathatā** (the perfected nature, the true nature of the ālaya-vijñāna) transcends this defilement-purity polarity. What practice accomplishes—the transformation of the basis—transforms the *state* of the basis, not the *nature* of the basis. The nature has always been pure—this is the level "no defilement, no purity" is speaking of.

Return to the mirror analogy from 2.1: wiping off the dust is real, and the dust is real, but the mirror's reflecting nature has never changed from the start. Practice is not turning a dirty mirror into a clean one; it is wiping away a layer of dust that **never belonged to the mirror in the first place.**

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra, describing the state of a practitioner who realizes the patience of the unborn, gives an analogy even more precise than the mirror—**lapis lazuli with a bright moon suspended within:**

> Like lapis lazuli with a bright moon suspended within—body and mind at ease, wondrously complete and equal, attaining great peace. All the Tathāgatas' secret, complete, pure, and wondrous [dharmas] are all manifested within.

A piece of transparent lapis lazuli, with a bright moon hanging inside it. This single image displays all three pairs of negation at once: the lapis lazuli is **unborn and unceasing**—it was not made, and it cannot be destroyed; the lapis lazuli is **undefiled and unpurified**—dust may cling to its surface while its nature stays transparent; the lapis lazuli **neither increases nor decreases**—"all the Tathāgatas' secret, complete, pure, and wondrous dharmas are all manifested within," and yet limitless reflection does not make it more, nor does ceasing to reflect make it less.

The lapis lazuli never changes. What changes is only the depth to which the practitioner can see.

---

### No Increase, No Decrease — Mind Is Not Material, Therefore Unlimited

The third pair answers a question of quantity: does this mind-essence **have any amount**?

The answer is no. Only matter has amount; mind is not matter.

The most direct scriptural ground for this teaching is the *Anūnatvāpūrṇatva-nirdeśa Sūtra*. The Buddha tells Śāriputra:

> **Those who hold the great wrong view are those who see the realm of beings as increasing, or see the realm of beings as decreasing.**
> Beings are the dharma body; the dharma body is beings. The meaning is one, only the name differs.

Notice that the Buddha judges this mistaken view directly as "the great wrong view"—not a minor misunderstanding, but a fundamental inversion. Why? Because "apart from the dharma-realm of tathatā, the fixed characteristic of 'beings' cannot be found." Beings and the dharma body differ in name and appearance, but in substance they are one and the same thing. So no matter how many beings attain buddhahood, the buddha-realm does not increase, and the realm of beings does not decrease.

The *Ratnagotravibhāga* makes this clear with a vivid analogy—**earthenware, silver, and gold vessels**: the self-nature-pure mind is equal and without difference in ordinary beings (earthenware), sages (silver), and Buddhas (gold). The vessels' materials differ, but the space they can hold is one and the same. Space does not increase because of the vessel's material, nor decrease because of it.

Brought down to an everyday scale: the ālaya-vijñāna has stored, from beginningless time, all the karma, habitual tendencies, and seeds of all beings, never finding it too much, never overflowing. This is not because its "capacity is large"; it is because it was never within the category of "capacity" at all. Mind is not matter, and so it **has no boundary and no quantity.**

The same thing can be seen clearly from the angle of the stages of practice. At the pre-path stage of the ordinary being, this essence is "not yet disclosed"; at the on-path stage of the bodhisattva, it is "gradually disclosed"; at the post-path stage of buddhahood, it is "fully disclosed"—but the essence disclosed is one and the same. For teaching convenience the ancients gave the same essence three names across the three stages: the buddha-nature abiding in itself (pre-path), the buddha-nature drawn forth (on-path), and the buddha-nature attained as fruit (post-path)—three names, one essence. Mapped onto a single practitioner: the mind at the moment of first aspiration and the mind at buddhahood **have neither increase nor decrease in essence**—every difference lies in the single point of "disclosed or not," never in the essence itself.

---

### The Progressive Structure of the Six Negations

Set the three pairs together and you see that they are not six isolated denials but one set of progressive illumination:

- First it illuminates existence—this mind-essence **was not made** (no arising, no ceasing)
- Then it illuminates value—this mind-essence **has no good or bad** (no defilement, no purity)
- Finally it illuminates quantity—this mind-essence **has no amount** (no increase, no decrease)

From "what is it?" to "is it good or bad?" to "is there more or less?"—three layers, each one thoroughly negating ordinary beings' three false reckonings about the mind-essence. By the end of the six negations, the three root misunderstandings (that the mind is made, that the mind has dirt and cleanness, that the mind has amount) are pulled up by the root.

What remains is the tathatā that was there all along.

---

### A Cave Dark for Ten Thousand Years, a Single Point of Flame

The doctrinal density of the six negations is extremely high, but their ultimate import is in fact very practical—it is **confidence in practice.**

Picture a cave dark for ten thousand years. It has been dark for ten thousand years, dark for a hundred thousand years. But the moment a single person lights a candle and walks in, in that one second the cave is bright. The light does not first need to solve the problem of "how long has this cave been dark"—the length of time the darkness has existed has no relation whatsoever to the time the light takes to illuminate it.

However deep the karma, however thick the habitual tendencies—that is the cave. What illuminates it is the light of prajñā. And what gives a person absolute confidence in that light is precisely the mind the six negations point to—**it is unborn and unceasing, so karma has not truly defiled its essence; it is undefiled and unpurified, so the thickest habitual tendencies do not truly grow on it; it neither increases nor decreases, so no matter how long one has been deluded, the original face has never been short by a single fraction.**

The moment prajñā arises, it illuminates at once—not gradual grinding, not peeling layer after layer, but seeing right here, right now.

This mechanism of "right here, right now" can be understood this way: darkness itself has no substance; it is only another way of saying "light is absent." So the moment light appears, darkness does not need to be "dealt with," "driven out," or "destroyed"—it was never a thing that could be dealt with. In the same way, ignorance, affliction, and habitual tendencies, seen at the ultimate level, have no real substance; they are only different ways of saying "prajñā is absent." The moment prajñā is present, they lose at once the object they had clung to. This is why "deeply practicing prajñāpāramitā" is so critical: what it ignites is this single point of flame, and what that flame is to illuminate cannot, by its very nature, resist the coming of the light.

The six negations establish the original face of this mind. The next line of the Heart Sūtra continues: "**Therefore, in emptiness there is no form, no feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness; no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind**"—and that word "therefore" carries great weight. Precisely because the mind-essence is originally thus, the systematic deconstruction that follows—the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen elements, peeling back in three layers the illusion that "I am seeing the world"—now has its ground.

The next chapter begins from "therefore."
