## 2.9 Even the Practice Must Be Released—No Wisdom and No Attainment

The previous chapter emptied the twelve links of dependent origination—the causal chain of suffering is empty, and even "the road that ends suffering" is empty. The Heart Sūtra's next move is more radical still:

> No suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path; no wisdom and no attainment, with nothing to be attained.

This line crosses a watershed.

The earlier negations targeted the *things being looked at*—the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the eighteen elements, the twelve links. All of these are dharma-marks held up for contemplation. What is now being negated is the *method of looking* (the four noble truths, the prescription the Buddha wrote for us), the *tool of looking* (prajñā wisdom itself), and the *result of looking* (the belief that something is waiting at the end). The route of practice, the tool of practice, the destination of practice—all emptied at once.

From emptying the other to emptying the self—this is prajñā's sharpest edge.

---

### The Four Noble Truths Are Addressed to a "Self"

To understand this cut, we first need to understand where the four noble truths originally stood.

The four noble truths were the content of the Buddha's very first teaching after his awakening. At Deer Park, to the five bhikṣus, he taught them in his own voice through three turnings and twelve aspects: **this is suffering, this is the origin of suffering, this is the cessation of suffering, this is the path that leads to cessation.** The scripture records: "This is the Noble Truth of suffering—a dharma not previously heard—which should be correctly contemplated. Then there arise vision, knowledge, clarity, and awakening." Every word directly transmitted—a map left behind by one who had walked the road himself.

So why does the Heart Sūtra say "no suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path"? Is this denying the Buddha's teaching?

No. The Heart Sūtra has seen something deeper: **the entire structure of the four noble truths presupposes a "self."**

Suffering—*I* am suffering. Origin—*my* suffering has a cause. Cessation—*I* want to stop the suffering. Path—*I* must walk this road. Every truth hides a subject, and that subject is "I." Without "I," whom does suffering oppress? Who calls origination forth? For whom does cessation appear? Who is there to walk the path?

The previous chapter established a key insight: the core of avidyā is the construction of "I"—mistaking what flows for what is fixed, mistaking what arises dependently for what truly exists. When prajņā contemplation truly penetrates the illusion of "I," the entire framework of the four noble truths **loses its service-object**—like a letter, however beautifully written, that has nowhere to be delivered because the recipient does not exist.

It is not that the four truths are wrong. It is that the recipient is no longer there.

In the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*, Nāgārjuna records a conversation between the Buddha and Subhūti that pushes this principle to its limit. The Buddha says: "It is not through the Noble Truth of suffering that one reaches nirvāṇa, nor through the Noble Truth of the path." Subhūti asks: then what counts as the equality of the four noble truths? The Buddha answers: "**When there is no suffering, no wisdom-of-suffering, no origination, no wisdom-of-origination, no cessation, no wisdom-of-cessation, no path, no wisdom-of-path—this is called the equality of the four noble truths.**" All eight (the four truths plus their four wisdoms) must be "no" before there is equality.

"Equality" here does not mean "equal in value"—it means **single-flavored**. Under prajñā's illumination, suffering, origination, cessation, and path equally lack self-nature. It is not that the four truths have become false; it is that prajñā sees a layer deeper and earlier than the four truths—a layer where the four compartments have not yet even been drawn.

---

### The Raft Simile—A Precise Definition of Dharma-Attachment

Where the four noble truths stand can be put in a more down-to-earth image.

The four noble truths are a medical protocol. Knowing you are ill—suffering. Finding the cause—origin. Confirming the state of health—cessation. Writing the prescription—path. As the Great Physician King, the Buddha designed this teaching to be precise, practical, and exactly suited to the cure.

But what about after the cure?

A patient who has recovered, yet walks around every day carrying the doctor's prescription, keeping the empty medicine bottle on hand, telling everyone he meets, "Look, this is the medicine that cured me"—he is no longer a patient, but he has become a "former patient," a person defined by the experience of being treated. The illness is gone, but his attachment to the act of "being cured" has itself become a new obstacle.

This is the precise meaning of **dharma-attachment** (*dharma-grāha*).

Self-attachment takes the "self" as real; dharma-attachment takes the "dharma" as real—grasping the Buddha's teaching, the method of practice, and the route to awakening as if they were entities with self-nature. This is the trap practitioners most easily fall into. Before the "unborn and unceasing" reality of thusness established in 2.6, even the method of reaching that reality cannot be grasped as truly existent—because method and reality are not two things.

In the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*, Nāgārjuna invokes the raft simile directly: once across the river, even the raft must be released. "The dharma must still be abandoned, how much more non-dharma." This line from the Diamond Sūtra meets Nāgārjuna's prajñā contemplation head-on in these five words of the Heart Sūtra: "no suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path."

---

### "No Wisdom and No Attainment"—Even the Tool Is Released

"No suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path" empties the Buddha's prescription. "No wisdom and no attainment" empties the practitioner himself.

Lay out the Heart Sūtra's whole sequence of "dispelling" and it becomes clear:

- 2.7 dispelled the five aggregates, twelve sense-bases, eighteen elements—emptying "the coarse dharma-marks being looked at"
- 2.8 dispelled the twelve links—emptying "the causal chain of suffering"
- The first half of this chapter dispelled the four noble truths—emptying "the Buddha's prescription"
- Now "no wisdom and no attainment"—emptying "the wisdom that looks" and "the result of looking"

Every earlier "no X" emptied the object being looked at; this cut goes far deeper. "No wisdom" empties the subject that looks; "no attainment" empties the result of looking. **Both subject and object vanish**—and this is precisely the final step of "dispelling marks to realize the nature," the Yogācāra method of contemplation.

But a lethal paradox surfaces here: **if even prajñā is emptied, what do you use to empty prajñā?**

Nāgārjuna's answer is utterly clean: "**The equality of 'something to be attained' and 'nothing to be attained'—this is called 'nothing to be attained.'**" Non-attainment is not derived from attainment—that would presuppose a "something" to be negated; nor is it derived from non-attainment—that would be circular reasoning. When a person stops taking sides between "getting" and "not getting," prajñā is already there. **It is not that you use prajñā to empty prajñā; it is that you stop grasping at anything at all.**

Nāgārjuna elsewhere distinguishes precisely two senses of "nothing to be attained." The first is worldly—a person wants something, fails to get it, and calls this "nothing attained"; it is frustration, disappointment. The second is prajñā's—in the true mark of all dharmas, there simply is no determinate entity that can be "attained"; not "cannot get it," but "there is no such gettable thing." Then Nāgārjuna immediately adds a crucial line: "**this does not mean there is no increase of merit, wisdom, and wholesome roots.**" Prajñā's non-attainment does not say that merit and wisdom do not exist—it says they have no self-nature of "being possessed." A practitioner cannot tuck prajñā into his pocket like a collector's item—it is simply not that kind of thing.

---

### "With Nothing to Be Attained"—The Heart Sūtra's Passcode

These five words, "with nothing to be attained," are the Heart Sūtra's most concentrated passcode.

**Looking backward**—they explain every "no." Why is there no form in emptiness? With nothing to be attained. Why no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind? With nothing to be attained. Why no suffering, origination, cessation, path? With nothing to be attained. Why no wisdom and no attainment? With nothing to be attained. Kuiji judged it plainly: "**With nothing attained provides a comprehensive explanation for all the preceding negations.**" The negations do not each have their own separate reason—they share a single root: in the true mark of all dharmas, there has never been a self-nature that could be obtained.

**Looking forward**—they are the foundation of every "yes" that follows. The very next line, "the bodhisattva, relying on prajñāpāramitā, has a mind without obstruction," is possible precisely because of "with nothing to be attained." Obstruction presupposes an "I" clinging to a "thing"; non-attainment dismantles that presupposition.

"With nothing to be attained" is not a closing negation but **an opening affirmation**—it does not say "after all that practice, you got nothing," it says "from the very beginning there was nothing to get, and therefore the mind can be without obstruction." This reversal is the Heart Sūtra at its most precise: **it turns the seemingly defeated "nothing attained" into the very basis of liberation.**

---

### Not "Don't Practice," But "Practice Without Attachment"

⚠️ This section harbors several extremely dangerous misreadings that must be blocked.

**"No suffering, no origination, no cessation, no path" does not equal denying cause and effect.** What is emptied is the *self-nature* of the four truths, not their *function*. Suffering really does exist at the conventional level; cause and effect really do operate at the conventional level. The "no" negates grasping the four truths as fixed, real entities—it does not say there is no suffering, no path, no nirvāṇa. To hear "no" as "does not exist," Nāgārjuna bluntly judged to be **nihilistic emptiness**—more dangerous than grasping at existence.

**"No wisdom" does not equal anti-intellectualism.** What prajñā negates is the "grasping-as-real" of wisdom—imagining there is a thing called "wisdom" that can be possessed, turned into capital, into identity, into pride. But prajñā does not deny the growth of merit and wisdom. Study, reflection, and meditation must still be done; the six pāramitās must still be cultivated. The only difference is attitude: **study with a mind of "nothing to be attained," and what you learn will not become an object of attachment.**

**"With nothing to be attained" does not equal passive inaction.** The worldly "nothing attained" is passive (seeking, failing, so giving up); prajñā's "nothing to be attained" is active—seeing clearly that no self-nature can be obtained in the true mark of all dharmas, and therefore practicing every wholesome dharma with a pure mind. Precisely because no dharma is taken as "mine," nothing is impossible to release, and so one can give without limit and never tire. **Not "don't act," but "act without attachment"**—this is prajñā's highest expression.

---

"With nothing to be attained" sounds light, but doing it triggers the deepest fear on the whole path of practice.

When that thing which has, from beginningless time, perpetually deliberated and taken itself to be "I"—the *manas*, the seventh consciousness—is suddenly told "you are not real," its response is not compliance but terror: **with no me holding on, who will protect us?**

What is this like? It is like a parent who has cared for a child full-time for twenty years, and now the child is leaving home for university. The mind knows it should let go, knows the child has grown, knows this is a good thing; but in the moment the hand opens, what rises in the body is not joy but a deep fear: with me not at his side, what if something happens to him? This fear is not a lack of care—**it is precisely because the care runs so deep.** The *manas* grasping at "I" is just like this—it has been doing, far too earnestly, a thing that never needed doing, believing that without itself holding on, everything would collapse.

The Heart Sūtra's next line picks up exactly at this spot:

> The bodhisattva, relying on prajñāpāramitā, has a mind without obstruction. Because of no obstruction, there is no terror; leaving far behind inverted dream-thinking, ultimate nirvāṇa.

"There is no terror" does not appear by accident—it is the necessary passage that letting go must travel. The next chapter follows the Heart Sūtra through this fear, all the way to ultimate nirvāṇa.
