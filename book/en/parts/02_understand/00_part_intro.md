# Part Two · Understand · The Prajñāpāramitā Heart Sūtra · The Eyes That See the World

What Part One walked through was the question: *can I even set out?* Over ten chapters—from a single aspiration to the thirty-seven factors of awakening—it fixed the direction, repaired the instrument, and laid the methods out on the table. Someone willing to practice is no longer short of a map.

But the road is under your feet, and that does not mean your eyes are open. As you walk, sooner or later you run into a deeper question—**what, exactly, am I walking toward? What is it that I am seeing?**

This is what Part Two sets out to answer.

---

The Buddha taught for forty-nine years; the prajñā teachings alone occupied twenty-two of them. The *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra* by itself, in Xuanzang's translation, runs to six hundred fascicles—close to five million characters. The breadth, the fineness, and the depth of this unfolding of wisdom have no second system to match them in the whole history of Buddhism.

And then, among all the prajñā scriptures, there is one that is extremely short—**only two hundred and sixty characters**.

This is the *Prajñāpāramitā Heart Sūtra*.

What gives two hundred and sixty characters the right to stand on the shoulders of six hundred fascicles? Not abbreviation, not excerpting, not a "highlights edition." **The Heart Sūtra is short because it has seized the entire structure of prajñā.** The other sūtras and śāstras are unfolding, elaborating, arguing back and forth; the Heart Sūtra is gathering in, setting the form, raising the skeleton up for you to see.

So the Heart Sūtra is not a piece of reading—it is a **structural map**.

Precisely because it is a structural map, its place in Chinese Buddhism is so unusual. From the Tang dynasty onward—Chan, Tiantai, Huayan, Pure Land, Esoteric—every school, every monastery, every Dharma assembly, every morning and evening service has chanted these two hundred and sixty characters. Over fourteen hundred years, no other sūtra has been so widely adopted, so widely recited, so widely returned to by traditions this diverse. The reason is not that it is the most famous; it is that it is the most **universal**—the structure it seizes is the shared skeleton of every prajñā teaching. Anyone who is truly practicing will, sooner or later, return to stand before this structural map.

---

Part One gave a methodology; Part Two gives a way of seeing. To practice is not merely to perform a few movements—it is to look at the world with a different pair of eyes. Lifting the same cup of tea, an ordinary person sees "I am drinking water," while the bodhisattva practicing the profound prajñāpāramitā sees "the five aggregates at work"; hearing the same sentence, an ordinary person sees "he is insulting me," while the practitioner sees "the arising and ceasing of form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness."

The difference is not in the action. It is in those eyes.

And once those eyes have been trained, what they look out upon is not only tea and insults—it is the whole world. The comings and goings of people on a street, the joy and sorrow inside a phone call, an illness, a loss: to untrained eyes these are fate grinding down upon oneself; to trained eyes they are causes and conditions arising and ceasing, the five aggregates flowing on. The same world, the same events—but what is seen is different, and so a person's response is wholly different.

The Heart Sūtra is the shortest and most complete course there is for training those eyes.

---

Part Two's eleven chapters follow the text of the Heart Sūtra downstream.

It begins with the title (2.1)—just the few words "Prajñā · Heart · Sūtra" already say, in full, everything the whole sūtra has to say. Then comes the protagonist of the sūtra's first sentence—Avalokiteśvara (2.2), a closed awakening loop in which the causal level is already the fruition level. Next is "when practicing the profound prajñāpāramitā" (2.3)—how the verb "practicing" (*xíng*) reaches down into the noun "formations" (*xíng-aggregate*) is the pivotal turn of the contemplative method. "He illuminated the emptiness of the five aggregates" (2.4), "form is emptiness" (2.5)—the layer-by-layer taking-apart of the five aggregates. Then comes the string of negations—"no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind"—the twelve sense-fields, the eighteen elements, the twelve links of dependent origination, the four noble truths, attachment broken through layer upon layer, all the way to the turn at "no wisdom, and also no attainment" (2.9)—where, having broken through to the very bottom, one instead stands up. Last come "ultimate nirvāṇa" (2.10) and the great mantra (2.11), drawing the entire sūtra back into a single shortest spell.

The whole of Part Two is a descent from theory into contemplation. Having finished Part One, a person knows "what to do"; having finished Part Two, a person begins to see "what is being done."

---

Prajñāpāramitā—great wisdom gone to the other shore—sounds like the description of a distant destination.

What Part Two means to say is this: **the destination has been right here all along, only you have not seen it.**

Let us begin with the title.
