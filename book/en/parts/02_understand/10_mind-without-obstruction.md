## 2.10 The Mind Without Obstruction — Crossing Through Terror to Ultimate Nirvāṇa

The last chapter ended in an uncomfortable place. "Because there is nothing to attain" comes down like a single stroke of a blade, and the manas consciousness—the part of us that perpetually deliberates and clings to an "I"—is told it isn't real. Its first response will not be relief. It will be fear.

The next four lines of the Heart Sūtra are exactly what it takes to walk the rest of this road:

> The bodhisattva, relying on prajñāpāramitā, has a mind without obstruction. Because there is no obstruction, there is no terror. Far from inverted dream-thinking, [the bodhisattva attains] ultimate nirvāṇa.

These four lines are not four parallel items. They are **a single causal chain**. Relying on prajñā → the mind is without obstruction → there is no terror → far from inverted dream-thinking → ultimate nirvāṇa. The completion of each station is the condition for the next. This is also where the whole arc of Part II draws to a close—from Avalokiteśvara at the causal ground all the way to the mind without obstruction at the fruition.

---

### Where Terror Comes From

Common sense says that after letting go, you should feel light. The Heart Sūtra does not say light. It says **terror**. Why?

The answer lies in the seventh consciousness, the manas. Its function is perpetual deliberation—it never stops working, always taking the subjective aspect of the eighth ālaya-vijñāna as its object and grasping it as "I." Chapter 2.3 explained how "going deep" means turning inward into the formations aggregate, and the manas is the deepest layer of those formations—it is already "doing self" on your behalf before you even know it.

As prajñā's illumination presses forward—seeing the five aggregates as empty (2.4), deconstructing the eighteen dhātus (2.7), seeing the emptiness of the twelve links (2.8), releasing both the Four Noble Truths and prajñā itself (2.9)—the manas retreats step by step, but there is always something left to grasp. At the very least there is still "emptiness" to understand, "practice" to do, "wisdom" to attain. And then, at "because there is nothing to attain," **the last straw is taken away too**.

What the manas emits at this point is not cognitive doubt—that belongs to the sixth consciousness. It is existential dread: **if "I" am not real, then who is experiencing all of this? With no "I" in charge, what is left that is safe?**

So terror is not a sign that practice has failed. It is a sign that practice has reached the right depth. The Heart Sūtra places "no terror" **after** "mind without obstruction" and not **before**, precisely because terror is part of the crossing, not a mistake to be avoided.

---

### The Four Stations of the Causal Chain

In his commentary, Kuiji unfolds these four lines into a precise timeline of practice—a **dual mapping of the five paths and the ten bhūmis**.

**First station: the mind without obstruction** (from the stage of resolute understanding through the path of preparation, up to the first bhūmi). "Obstruction" (罣) is the afflictive obstruction; "hindrance" (礙) is the cognitive obstruction. A mind without obstruction means both have been quieted. But here they are *suppressed*, not yet *severed*—like a stone pressed down onto water: while the pressure holds, it sinks; the moment the pressure slackens, it floats back up.

**Second station: no terror** (the path of seeing, the eighth bhūmi). Prajñā-wisdom comes to the fore, the conceptually discriminated forms of both obstructions are truly severed, and only then does terror genuinely dissolve. Notice this: **from the first bhūmi to the eighth bhūmi lie seven grounds in between**. The crossing of terror is not a single dramatic instant but a long stretch of the path of cultivation.

**Third station: far from inverted dream-thinking** (the path of cultivation, the tenth bhūmi). The conceptual grasping has been cut, but innate grasping still remains. The path of cultivation purifies the four inversions—taking the impermanent as permanent, suffering as pleasure, the impure as pure, the selfless as self—layer by layer.

**Fourth station: ultimate nirvāṇa** (the path of no-more-learning, the Buddha stage). Both obstructions are wholly severed; all four kinds of nirvāṇa are complete.

There is one more vivid image along this road—the **three levels of coarseness**: skin, flesh, bone, the three depths of obstruction. From the surface to the deep marrow, practice does not cut through in one stroke. It peels away one layer at a time.

---

### The Buddha's Whole Life Is This Same Arc

The preceding nine chapters analyzed doctrine. In this chapter we do something different—**we let the Buddha himself walk in**. The Buddha did not teach "because there is nothing to attain" with words. He lived out "because there is nothing to attain" with his entire life.

**The four-gate excursion — the causal ground of longing for freedom.** The prince goes out and sees an old man, a sick man, a dead man, and a renunciant, and he "awoke with vast clarity." Aging, sickness, and death were nothing new—everyone in the palace could see them—but only the prince truly *saw into* them. That single thought of seeing into is the causal ground of Avalokiteśvara: the very loop described in 2.2, switching on in the Buddha for the first time.

**Renunciation and asceticism — going deep, turning inward.** At twenty-nine the Buddha set down the throne—the greatest object of worldly "I"-attachment—and renounced the world for six years of austerity. The asceticism was trial and error. He tried the extreme of severe austerities and discovered that **this, too, was grasping**—not grasping at pleasure, but grasping at "practice" itself. The discovery of the Middle Way was the first rehearsal of "because there is nothing to attain" in 2.9: even the tool of practice must be released.

**Subduing Māra under the Bodhi tree — the site of crossing through terror.** He sat down and vowed, "Until I attain omniscience, I shall never rise." Then Māra's army came—1.8 billion troops, every form of terror. The Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra names what that army contained: Māra has ten armies—desire, worry, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth, **terror**, doubt, malice, profit-seeking, arrogance. **The sixth army is precisely terror.** Māra is not some demon arriving from outside; on the Yogācāra reading, these ten armies are the ten afflictions projected by the manas with the sixth consciousness as their stage. The way the Buddha faced all of it comes down to two words: "**silent**" and "**unmoving**." Māra's army was not defeated; it dissipated of its own accord in his stillness. At the end he "extended his right hand and pressed the earth with his fingers," and the earth deity rose up to bear witness—this is the origin of the earth-touching gesture. What the earth witnessed was not the Buddha's "power" but the fact that, in the face of terror, he did not waver.

⚠️ Here we must block a common misreading: subduing Māra was not the Buddha using superpowers to "defeat" an external demon. In the sūtra his response is "silence" and "stillness"—Māra is a psychological event, not a military one. The earth-touching gesture is not a plea to heaven and earth for help; it is **taking the earth as witness, confirming his own immovability in the face of terror**. The Buddha-biography literature does include some descriptions of supernatural feats (such as "turning Māra's weapons into garlands"), but those belong to the rhetoric of the narrative tradition, and they should not be used to establish a doctrinal claim that "the Buddha possessed supernatural combat powers." The doctrinal core of subduing Māra, on the Yogācāra reading, is this: the final counter-assault of the manas retreats of its own accord when prajñā comes to the fore. **No one defeats anyone. There is only one person, seeing his own immovability.**

**Forty-five years of the alms-round — the daily display of non-abiding nirvāṇa.** After his awakening, the Buddha did not leave. For forty-five years, every day he put on his robe, took up his bowl, entered the city to beg for food, and "begging in sequence, returned to his place." Those three words "begging in sequence" are read very deeply in the Yogācāra commentaries: "from one lane to the next, from one household to the next, to demonstrate that in each and every dharma one realizes the principle of signlessness throughout." No selecting, no skipping; not begging an extra bowl from a wealthy house, not passing over a poor one. Every step, every doorway, is the bodily and verbal manifestation of **equality wisdom** (*samatā-jñāna*).

**The final letting go — even the Buddha lets go.** Before parinirvāṇa, Ānanda was anxious. The Buddha left a final instruction: "**Be your own lamp; make the Dharma your lamp. Do not seek another lamp. Take refuge in yourself; take refuge in the Dharma. Do not take refuge in anything else.**" Take the precepts as your teacher, not the Buddha as your teacher. He let go even of his disciples' dependence on himself. The final verse: "All formations are impermanent—this is the dharma of arising and ceasing. When arising and ceasing themselves cease, quiescent cessation is bliss."—"**This is my final teaching.**"

The whole arc—from longing to releasing, to turning inward, to crossing through terror, to awakening, to forty-five years of embodied living, to letting go even of himself—one passage of the Heart Sūtra, **demonstrated by the Buddha across an entire lifetime**.

### Manas → Equality Wisdom

The mechanism of crossing through terror is stated precisely in Yogācāra: **the manas consciousness is transformed into equality wisdom** (*samatā-jñāna*).

The manas is not annihilated—it is **transformed**. Yogācāra has a wonderfully vivid metaphor: the **charmed snake**. When a mantra has been recited over a snake, the snake is still there, but its venom is controlled. The bodhisattva dwells in saṃsāra; the "snake" of the manas is still present, but it no longer has any venom. **It is not killing the snake (that is the śrāvaka's path); it is letting the snake remain while doing no harm (this is the bodhisattva's path).**

The mechanism of transformation is concrete: the manas has no power of its own to sever afflictions; it depends entirely on the sixth consciousness entering the twofold-emptiness contemplation. The timeline runs like this—equality wisdom first comes to the fore at the path of seeing in the first bhūmi; below the seventh bhūmi it is still intermittent; from the eighth bhūmi onward, self-grasping is permanently subdued; after the tenth bhūmi it is no longer intermittent. This is why the Heart Sūtra pairs "no terror" with the eighth bhūmi: that is the watershed where self-grasping is truly subdued for good.

This also answers a common misunderstanding. "Letting go of the self" sounds like extinguishing the self. It is not. What prajñā does has never been to kill the manas; it is to turn the manas from "clinging to a self" into "treating all things equally." **Terror is not overcome. It dissolves of its own accord in the process of transformation.**

---

### Ultimate Nirvāṇa — Not Leaving, but Being Here Freely

"Ultimate nirvāṇa" here is not the śrāvaka's nirvāṇa. The śrāvaka's nirvāṇa is "body and wisdom completely extinguished, like a lamp going out"—once gone, it does not return. The bodhisattva's nirvāṇa is called **non-abiding nirvāṇa** (*apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa*)—**not abiding in saṃsāra, and not abiding in nirvāṇa either**.

The Mahāyānasaṃgrahabhāṣya states the principle with perfect clarity: "By prajñā one does not abide in saṃsāra; by compassion one does not abide in nirvāṇa. If one discriminates saṃsāra, one abides in saṃsāra; if one discriminates nirvāṇa, one abides in nirvāṇa. The bodhisattva attains non-discriminating wisdom—discriminating nothing, and therefore abiding nowhere." **To discriminate is to abide; not to discriminate is not to abide.**

This is a two-axis structure. The **prajñā axis** sees that saṃsāra has no self-nature, and so one is not trapped in saṃsāra. The **compassion axis** sees that sentient beings are still in suffering, and so one does not enter nirvāṇa alone. Where the two axes meet is non-abiding nirvāṇa.

The Cheng Weishi Lun condenses it into four characters: "**active yet always quiescent**" (用而常寂)—within the "activity" of benefiting beings, the essential nature of tathatā remains "quiescent" throughout. It is not that one is too busy to remember stillness; rather, **stillness is inherent in the activity itself**.

Return to the Buddha's life: forty-five years of begging and teaching are exactly the daily display of these two axes. Entering the city each day to beg is the compassion axis (not abiding in nirvāṇa); washing the feet, arranging the seat, and entering meditation each day is the prajñā axis (not abiding in saṃsāra). **The Buddha himself is what non-abiding nirvāṇa looks like walking, eating, and sleeping.**

---

### Far from Inverted Dream-Thinking

"Dream-thinking" is not two things, "dreams" and "thoughts"—it is a synonym for deluded discrimination. An old master put his finger right on it: "**Not having awakened to true wisdom, one perpetually dwells in a dream; therefore the Buddha speaks of the long night of saṃsāra.**" Ordinary beings are dreaming—not the dream of sleep, but **the dream of waking life**. Believing there is an unchanging "I" (permanence-inversion), believing that what one grasps can bring happiness (pleasure-inversion), believing that body and mind are pure (purity-inversion), believing that the assembled aggregates are a self (self-inversion). Four inversions.

The four inversions are not just one aspect of the imagined nature (*parikalpita*)—**the four inversions are the core operating mechanism of the imagined nature**. How does a person take something of dependent nature and treat it as the imagined? Through inversion.

The two words "far from" (遠離) also carry depth. They do not mean "having none," but **the freedom that comes after the crossing**. It is not that one never dreamed of permanence, pleasure, purity, and self—one dreamed, one woke, one became free. To have dreamed and then awakened—that is what "far from dream-thinking" means.

The last practice the Buddha taught his disciples before parinirvāṇa—the **four foundations of mindfulness**—counters exactly these four inversions: mindfulness of the body breaks the purity-inversion, mindfulness of feeling breaks the pleasure-inversion, mindfulness of mind breaks the permanence-inversion, mindfulness of dharmas breaks the self-inversion. What the Buddha placed in his disciples' hands at the last moment was the very tool for countering the dream.

---

From the longing of Avalokiteśvara (2.2) to the freedom of the mind without obstruction (this chapter), the **awakening loop** of the Heart Sūtra now closes. The causal ground is "Avalokiteśvara"; the fruition is "the mind without obstruction." One is the direction of longing, the other the state of arrival; between them lies the entire prajñā contemplation of the Heart Sūtra.

But the Heart Sūtra is not yet finished.

This road of awakening is not the story of one bodhisattva alone. The very next line of the Heart Sūtra pulls the lens back wide: "**All Buddhas of the three times, relying on prajñāpāramitā, attain anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi**"—all the Buddhas of past, present, and future walk the same one road. Then comes "this is the great spirit mantra, the great illumination mantra, the unsurpassed mantra, the unequalled mantra," and finally "*gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā*"—turning from the doctrinal contemplation of the exoteric path toward the mantra and total retention of the esoteric.

One person's awakening expands into the road for all.
