## 2.7 In Emptiness There Is No Form—The Threefold Deconstruction of Faculty, Object, and Consciousness

> Therefore, in emptiness there is no form, no feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness; no eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind; no form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental-object; no eye-realm up to no consciousness-realm.

The word "therefore" carries a great deal of weight here. It means: precisely because the preceding passage—"the true mark of all dharmas" is unborn and unceasing, undefiled and unpurified, unincreased and undecreased—shows that the nature of mind is originally just so, the long string of "no" that now follows is not the "no" of annihilation. Under the light of tathatā, the self-nature of all dharmas is simply unattainable. The six negations are the cause; this systematic deconstruction is the effect.

But this passage is not just repeating, over and over, the single point that "the five aggregates are empty." Look closely and you will find that the Heart Sūtra is here changing the resolution three times, from coarse to fine, peeling open in three layers the illusion that "I am seeing a world."

---

### Three Deconstructions, Three Levels of "Self"

The three layers each dismantle a different form of self-grasping:

| Sūtra line | What it dismantles | Object of grasping |
|---|---|---|
| In emptiness there is no form, no feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness | five aggregates | "there is a self" (personal self-grasping) |
| No eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind; no form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental-object | twelve sense-fields | "there is a world out there" (coarse dharma-self-grasping) |
| No eye-realm up to no consciousness-realm | eighteen elements | "there is a consciousness cognizing the world" (subtle dharma-self-grasping) |

Each layer is more subtle than the last, and harder to illuminate. The first layer—the five aggregates—was already treated in detail in 2.4 and 2.5; it is the starting point of this deconstruction. This chapter carries the work further down, looking at how the next two layers, the twelve sense-fields and the eighteen elements, peel the illusion away even deeper.

---

### The Twelve Sense-Fields—Not Two Solid Things Colliding

The six faculties (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) together with the six objects (form, sound, smell, taste, touch, mental-object) are together called the twelve sense-fields.

"Sense-field" (*āyatana*) precisely means a **gate of generation**—cognition grows from here. When the eye-faculty meets a form-object, eye-consciousness grows in the very instant of that meeting; when the ear-faculty meets a sound-object, ear-consciousness grows in that moment. Faculty and object are the conditions out of which consciousness grows.

Most people's default picture of the world runs roughly like this: out there is a world that exists independently (the six objects); the senses (the six faculties) open like windows to let the world in; and cognition happens inside the window. This picture sounds entirely natural, but hidden inside it are two attachments that have never once been examined—

**First:** the world exists independently of cognition.
**Second:** the senses are neutral channels, faithfully transmitting what is outside into the inside.

What prajñā illuminates is exactly the opposite: faculty and object are **both dependently-arisen phenomena, arising and ceasing.** There is no independent "external object" waiting out there to be cognized, and no neutral "faculty" doing the faithful transmitting. The very meeting of faculty and object is itself a construction—not two pre-existing things bumping together, but a temporary event within the convergence of conditions.

"No eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind; no form, sound, smell, taste, touch, or mental-object" is not an instruction to shut down the senses—that is the path of the non-Buddhist schools, closing the eyes so as not to see, plugging the ears so as not to hear. What the Heart Sūtra is saying is: see that not one of these six faculties is a fixed "mine," and not one of these six objects is a fixed "out there." The division between window and view does not hold up at the root.

---

### The Eighteen Elements—Even "Cognizing" Itself Is a Construction

Add in the six consciousnesses (eye-consciousness through mind-consciousness), and you have the eighteen elements. The deconstruction of the eighteen elements reaches the deepest layer of all.

At the level of the twelve sense-fields, a person might just barely accept that "external objects are not entirely real." But somewhere inside, there usually remains a hidden consolation: "at least I am cognizing—**at least the act of cognizing is real.**"

The illumination of the eighteen elements takes away even this last consolation.

Even "cognizing" itself is constructed by consciousness.

What the eye "sees" is already an image processed by eye-consciousness, not the original face of the external object. What the ear "hears" is already a sound interpreted by ear-consciousness, not the sound-wave itself. And deeper still—the "self" that mind-consciousness "sees" is also constructed by consciousness, not a truly existing subject.

**You think you are "seeing the world"; in fact you are living inside "the world constructed by consciousness."**

Kuiji captured this mechanism with four characters of great precision: "**cognition resembling the object**." The way consciousness cognizes the world is not "directly seeing the external object"; it is "generating a cognition that resembles the object"—an internal image that resembles the object. You think you have seen the table; what you have actually seen is the image that eye-consciousness has manifested, an image that resembles the table. **What consciousness directly contacts has only ever been the content of consciousness itself.**

This sounds abstract. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra offers a set of strikingly forceful counter-cases: Aniruddha lost his eyes, yet possessed the divine eye and could still "see" with perfect clarity; Mahākāśyapa had "long extinguished his mind-root," yet "knows with perfect clarity, not depending on mental ideation." The doctrinal weight of these two cases is this: if the essence of seeing lay in the eye-faculty, then without the eye-faculty one should not be able to see; if the essence of knowing lay in the mind-faculty, then once the mind-faculty was extinguished one should not be able to know. But in fact both still operate. **This means: the "faculty" within the eighteen elements is not the essence of cognition—only its temporary channel.** The same sūtra goes on to sum up this path of practice in two characters—"**releasing adhesion**": prajñā illumination is not shutting down the senses, but freeing consciousness from its adhesion to faculty and object. The six faculties are the entry-points where the six paths of adhesion take hold; "pull out any one faculty" and all six faculties are freed, because the grasping projected through the six gates is, in essence, one and the same.

---

### The Precise Range of "No"

The moment the line "no eye-realm up to no consciousness-realm" is spoken, any reader of Yogācāra is bound to ask: then what becomes of Yogācāra's eight-consciousness system? If even consciousness is emptied, doesn't the whole framework of "all dharmas are consciousness-only," "the three realms are mind-only," collapse?

This is precisely the question this passage must answer, and the Yogācāra tradition itself debated it for a long time. The answer is extremely clean: **"no" negates the imagined, not the dependent.**

Yogācāra's verdict comes in a single line: "**Realizing that what is grasped is non-existent is called awakening to non-being; realizing that the domain of the noble is existent is called awakening to non-nothingness.**" To awaken to the fact that the thing you grasp at (the imagined) does not exist is "awakening to non-being"; to awaken to the fact that the domain the noble ones illuminate (the perfected nature) does exist is "awakening to non-nothingness." "No" is not a single sweeping negation of everything; it is a precise surgery—**what is cut away is the grasping; what is preserved is reality.**

So after this deconstruction, the reader's eyes still see, the ears still hear, consciousness still operates. What changes is not the content of the world but the relationship to the world: from "this is real, independent, mine" to "this is dependently arisen, without self-nature, a temporary event of conditional convergence."

⚠️ To hear "the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, and the eighteen elements are all empty" as "nothing exists at all" is precisely the greatest misreading this deconstruction in the Heart Sūtra means to cure. If the result of this illumination were nihilism, the Buddha would never have needed forty-five years of teaching—a single sentence, "nothing exists," would have done. The Heart Sūtra's "no" always has a precise range: the imagined is empty (negated), the dependently-arisen function of the dependent is not empty (preserved), the tathatā of the perfected nature is not empty (revealed). Within that one character "no," the complete operation of all three natures is at work.

---

### The Speed of Awareness Must Catch Up to the Speed of Faculty Meeting Object

Beyond the doctrine, this passage holds one very concrete entry-point for practice.

In the Saṃyuktāgama, the Buddha describes the relationship between the eighteen elements and suffering as a causal chain: **element → contact → feeling → craving.** The coming-together of the three—the eighteen elements, that is, faculty, object, and consciousness—is "contact"; contact gives rise to feeling (painful, pleasant, neither-painful-nor-pleasant); feeling gives rise to craving (thirsting desire). The Buddha makes a particular point of it: the direction of this chain is irreversible—it is not that you first decide to crave; rather, the moment faculty, object, and consciousness meet, contact is born, feeling arises, and craving comes on its heels. The whole process moves so fast that an ordinary person can scarcely catch sight of it in time.

This means: the point where suffering can be intervened upon is **not at craving** (by then it is far too late), and **not at feeling either** (also already too late), but **in that single instant of contact**—the very second when faculty, object, and consciousness come together and consciousness has just manifested its "cognition resembling the object." If awareness can be present in that instant, feeling will not automatically turn into craving, craving will not automatically turn into grasping, and the entire production line of suffering will never start up.

So the daily work of practice can be described like this: **gradually bringing the speed of awareness up to the speed at which faculty meets object.** From the after-the-fact reflection of the beginning ("ah, I lost my temper again just now"), to awareness in the midst of the act ("anger is rising"), to the instant the thought arises ("the thought of anger is rising"), to that single instant where faculty meets object ("the eye-faculty has just received that sound")—the further forward awareness is pushed, the harder it becomes for that automatic chain of processing to start up after consciousness manifests its "cognition resembling the object." This is what this passage means when it lands in actual practice.

The reason this work is so hard is that consciousness operates at tremendous speed—one moment all is well, the next you are already angry, and what happened in the instant between, there was simply no time to see. The transformation of the formations-aggregate pointed out in 2.3 finds its testing-ground here: while prajñā still rests at the level of the perception-aggregate, reaction will always run ahead of awareness; only once prajñā has sunk down into the formations-aggregate does awareness get a chance to synchronize with the speed of consciousness. So "practicing deep prajñāpāramitā" is no abstract figure of speech—it is the one and only track in this race of speed.

---

After the threefold deconstruction, there is no "self," no "my world," no "I cognizing a world." The three layers of grasping have been seen through. But **luminous awareness has never disappeared**—that which is able to illuminate in this way is not a part of the eighteen elements; it is the background within which all the cognitive activity of the eighteen elements takes place. This is the very mind already established in 2.6 as unborn and unceasing, undefiled and unpurified, unincreased and undecreased—now at work.

The eighteen elements have deconstructed **how** seeing happens—the structure of cognition has been seen through. But the Heart Sūtra immediately presses on to another question: **why does this structure trap a person?** Why, even knowing it is dependently arisen and without self-nature, does a person still suffer? The answer is one word: **ignorance.**

The next chapter begins from "no ignorance, and no ending of ignorance."
