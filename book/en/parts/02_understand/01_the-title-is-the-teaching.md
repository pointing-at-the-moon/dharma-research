## 2.1 Prajñā · Heart · Sūtra — The Title Is the Teaching

Many people have recited the Heart Sūtra. Few have read its title.

That sounds like a contradiction—isn't the title right there on the first line? Who hasn't read it? But "having read" and "having read it in" are two different things. Most people, glancing at the seven characters 般若波羅蜜多心經, take them to mean something like: "**the essential sūtra of prajñāpāramitā**." A version label. It tells the reader that this is a condensed edition. Read it, turn the page, and on to "Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva."

But stop a moment. If this sūtra of two hundred and sixty characters can hold within it the six hundred fascicles of the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra*, then what about these seven characters of the title? **Could it be that the title itself has already said everything the whole sūtra sets out to say?**

The Tang-dynasty master Kuiji wrote a commentary on the Heart Sūtra, and the explanation of just these seven characters took him an entire fascicle. For him, the title was no ornament. It was the gateway into the teaching—and the way out of it as well. What this chapter does is follow his eyes and look at these seven characters once more.

---

### Re-parsing — "Heart" Is the Subject, Not a Modifier

The first question is how to parse it.

The usual reading: **"Prajñāpāramitā · Heart · Sūtra"** = the essential sūtra of prajñāpāramitā. Here "Heart" modifies "Sūtra"; it means "essence."

This chapter offers another reading: **"Prajñāpāramitā · Heart · Sūtra"** = the sūtra that speaks of "Heart" and of prajñāpāramitā. Here "Heart" is not a modifier; it is the **subject**.

The two readings seem to differ by no more than a single thought, but the difference is enormous. The first treats the sūtra as an abridgement. The second points to something else—that what this sūtra means to speak of is precisely the **mind**—and the relationship between the mind and prajñāpāramitā.

Which one to choose? In fact both are right, and they do not conflict—this is what the chapter will unfold slowly. But to see the second reading, we first have to take the three components of the title apart, one by one.

---

### Prajñā — More Than "Wisdom"

Kuiji expanded "prajñā" from the traditional three meanings into five, and the most crucial line is this:

> The nature of wisdom and the resources of wisdom are both called prajñā.

What is "the nature of wisdom" (*huì-xìng*)? It is the wisdom-essence the mind **already inherently possesses**. What are "the resources of wisdom" (*huì-zī*)? They are the wisdom-capital **accumulated through** the course of practice. Both dimensions are gathered into the single word "prajñā"—the inherent and the cultivated, principle and function, are not two things but two faces of one thing.

And among the five meanings, besides the traditional three—true reality, contemplative insight, and verbal expression—Kuiji added "accompanying practices, namely the myriad practices" and "objects of cognition, namely all dharmas." The point is this: prajñā is not only the illumination within the mind, but takes in the entire activity of that illumination (the myriad practices) and the entire field of its objects (all dharmas). From essence to function, from function to its field, there is no place that is not prajñā. One word gathers four quarters: essence, resources, activity, and the field it faces.

To put it another way: prajñā is not some distant thing, waiting to be obtained only after you have practiced your way to it. It is **the illuminating nature the mind already has within it**, together with a whole apparatus of practice and objects that set that illuminating nature into operation. The entire apparatus is called "prajñā."

This single distinction is the doctrinal foundation of the whole Heart Sūtra. Remember it.

---

### Pāramitā — Not a Far-Off Place, but a Road

"Pāramitā" is often translated as "reaching the other shore," which sounds like the description of a journey—a person on this shore, the destination on the far side, a crossing to be made.

The same expansion is applied to "pāramitā"—from the traditional two meanings, "bodhi and nirvāṇa," to five: **that which is to be known, the teaching, the principle, the practice, and the fruit**. From a person's first coming to know (*jñeya*), to contact with the teaching, to understanding the principle, to actual practice, to realizing the fruit—five stages, five other shores, forming a complete road.

The crux of this analysis is not the five categories themselves, but what they point to: **pāramitā is not a place, it is a process.** Not "arriving over there," but "walking out from here, walking across, walking all the way through."

"Prajñā" is the inherent eye; "pāramitā" is the entire course by which that eye gradually opens.

---

### Heart — Solid, Wondrous, Supreme; and the Mind That Is the Subject

Back to the character "heart."

In this tradition the character "heart" is glossed as "a designation for that which is **solid, wondrous, and supreme**"—what is firm, real, profound, and highest. And immediately after comes this passage:

> Though phenomena manifest in ten thousand forms, they are unified as "form is emptiness"; though the path unfolds through a thousand gates, it is threaded through as "no wisdom and also no attainment."

All things, gathered together, come down to the non-duality of form and emptiness; the hundreds of methods, gathered together, come down to "no wisdom and also no attainment." Take note—these two lines are precisely the final conclusion of the entire Heart Sūtra. In glossing the "heart" of the title, the conclusion of the whole sūtra has already been spoken once in advance.

In other words, the character "heart" already contains the deepest meaning the whole sūtra means to reveal. It is at once the "essence" (solid, wondrous, supreme) and the "subject" the sūtra means to reveal—that one mind on which all dharmas depend, the very place where the transformation of the basis takes place. The two readings do not conflict—**the essence of the sūtra is precisely that it speaks of this mind.**

This is why the chapter says the "heart" of the title is the subject. It is not set there to ornament the word "sūtra." It is the very thing this sūtra means to point out.

---

### Three Natures as Three Modes of Cognition — The Title Is the Transformation of the Basis

Map these three components onto the three natures of Yogācāra, and the structure shows itself in full. In his commentary Kuiji set down an extraordinarily concise equation:

> *Parikalpita-svabhāva* (the imagined nature) is solely deluded cognition; *paratantra-svabhāva* (the dependent nature) is solely conventional cognition; *parinispanna-svabhāva* (the perfected nature) is solely ultimate cognition. **Therefore all dharmas are inseparable from mind.**

The three natures are three modes of cognition: the imagined nature is the deluded grasping; the dependent nature is the operation of dependent arising; the perfected nature is the true nature of suchness—and all three are inseparable from mind.

Read the title back through this equation:

- **Prajñā** = the perfected nature = the mind's inherent ultimate wisdom (the nature of wisdom)
- **Pāramitā** = the course of practice upon the dependent nature = the entire road of the five other shores (the resources of wisdom)
- *(what practice eliminates)* = the imagined nature = the deluded structure grasped as "I" and "my world"
- **Heart** = the mind on which the three natures depend, on which all dharmas depend
- **Sūtra** = the words that point out this road

Seven characters, the three natures complete, and the entire structure of the transformation of the basis already inside them.

Here is an image a modern reader may find easier to catch. The mind is like a computer. The core firmware beneath has been running perfectly all along (the perfected nature); the operating system processes events as they arise and pass according to conditions (the dependent nature); but a great deal of malware has been installed and is running on top—the story of "I," the anxiety over gain and loss, the grasping at the five aggregates (the imagined nature)—dragging the whole machine nearly to a halt. Practice is not swapping in a new computer; it is uninstalling the malware, so that the core firmware can once again show itself at work. ⚠️ This is a heuristic analogy, not a theoretical equivalence: firmware is a manufactured artifact that arises and perishes, whereas the perfected nature is beginningless and inherent suchness. The image serves intuition only; it cannot be pressed into rigorous doctrinal inference.

---

### Transformation of the Basis — Not Forging a New Mirror, but Wiping Away the Dust

What is "the transformation of the basis"? The *Cheng Weishi Lun* defines it:

> One is able to turn away from *parikalpita* superimposed on *paratantra*, and turn toward *parinispanna* within *paratantra*.

The key words are "superimposed **on** *paratantra*" and "**within** *paratantra*." The same dependent nature—the same mind—is the one upon which the ordinary being grasps at the imagined nature and revolves in saṃsāra, and the one upon which the noble one realizes the perfected nature and is liberated. **It is not destroying one mind and making another, but turning away and turning toward upon the very same mind.**

This is why the mirror analogy is used so often in the Yogācāra tradition. The *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* says:

> Just as clean water becomes turbid when impurity is added, and when it later becomes clear, it is only that the impurity has been removed—**the clarity does not come from outside, because its nature is inherently clear.**

Water is clear to begin with; defiled by dust, it turns turbid; and the clarity that returns once it is cleaned is not a new clarity but the original clarity manifesting again. The *Madhyāntavibhāga* gathers it into a single fixed verse: "**because the nature of mind is originally pure, it is defiled only by adventitious defilements.**"

Prajñāpāramitā is not forging a new mirror. It is wiping away the layer of dust that never belonged to the mirror, so that the inherent capacity to illuminate can come forth again.

⚠️ But be careful of a common misreading: if the nature of mind is originally pure and prajñā is inherently possessed, then is practice unnecessary? The *Awakening of Mahāyāna Faith* refuted this idea long ago with the analogy of the *maṇi* jewel—"Like a *maṇi* jewel whose nature is luminous and pure, yet which is encased in ore and impurity—even if someone assiduously contemplates it, **without applying skillful means and exerting effort, the desire for purity will never be fulfilled**." The jewel is luminous and pure to begin with, but while it lies buried in the ore, merely knowing that it is luminous and pure is of no use—you still have to dig, still have to wash. The only difference is that the luminous purity washed clean **is not newly made**. The labor is real, and the original purity is real too; the two do not contradict each other. "Recognizing" does not mean "no need to practice"—this is one very clear middle-path line in the Yogācāra tradition.

---

### Seven Characters, a Map Home

Back to the question we began with: why can two hundred and sixty characters stand on the shoulders of six hundred fascicles?

Because from its title onward, this sūtra is doing one and the same thing—**pointing out the original face of the mind**. Prajñā is the treasure inherently possessed. The mind is where the treasure is kept. The sūtra is the road that leads back there. But "leading back" too is only a concession to convention—the true meaning is that you never once left.

The Diamond Sūtra puts this "never having left" most piercingly in two lines: "**The Tathāgata has nowhere from which he comes, and nowhere to which he goes**"; "**Tathāgata means the suchness of all dharmas.**" The Tathāgata is not a traveler who has walked a long road and at last reached the other shore, but the true, suchness-nature of all dharmas, which has always been right here. "The suchness of all dharmas" means "thus from the beginning"—not something to be achieved, but the original that is to be seen.

Why place this line here? Because the very figure who first steps onto the stage in the Heart Sūtra's opening sentence—Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva—is the embodiment of this "neither coming nor going." Avalokiteśvara's name is itself a closed loop: the one who observes is the one who is observed, and the Avalokiteśvara of the causal stage is the Avalokiteśvara of the fruition stage.

How that loop operates, and why—once you see through it—you find yourself inside it as well, is the business of the next chapter.

The title has already set the ball on the table. The next chapter begins with Avalokiteśvara, and looks at this sūtra's first move.
