## 6.6 The Three Gradual Stages and the Ground of Dry Wisdom: Purification Before Entering the Gate

### Amṛta Will Not Pour into a Poisoned Cup

That last line of the previous chapter—"That which hears has always been there."

If it has always been there, why can we not hear it?

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra's answer is not "because you are not clever enough," nor "because your karmic obstructions are too heavy." Its answer is remarkably concrete: because there is poison in your cup.

> As one removes poisoned honey from a clean vessel, washes it thoroughly with water, ash, and fragrant herbs, and only then stores amṛta within it.

The *amṛta* has always been there. The problem lies not with the amṛta but with the vessel. Residual poisoned honey clings to its walls—you thought you had poured it all out long ago, but what sticks to the sides is invisible to the naked eye and yet enough to contaminate everything you pour in afterward.

The three gradual stages are the process of washing that vessel.

### Why a Whole Fascicle Stands in Between

There is something worth noticing here. After Mañjuśrī selected ear-faculty perfect penetration (at the end of fascicle 6), the Śūraṅgama Sūtra did not move straight to the stages of practice. An entire fascicle stands in between—fascicle 7 is given over wholly to establishing the practice sanctuary and expounding the Śūraṅgama mantra.

Why not teach the stages of practice directly? Because **preparation itself requires preparation.**

The boundary-setting of the sanctuary, the protection of body and mind, the empowerment of the divine mantra—these are the building of an environment before one can even enter the purification procedure. Just as you would not begin a sterilization procedure inside a filthy, cluttered operating room—you first need a clean space, and only then can you do the work of purification inside it. Fascicle 7 is the preparation of the space; fascicle 8's three gradual stages are the preparation of the mind.

From the twenty-five perfect penetrations to the three gradual stages, the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's pedagogical logic runs like this: you now know which door to enter—but before you step over the threshold, first wash yourself clean.

### The First Gradual Stage: Remove the Auxiliary Causes

> Those beings who seek samādhi should sever the five pungent herbs of the world. These five, when cooked, stimulate lust; when eaten raw, increase anger.

The five pungent herbs—onion, garlic, leek, scallion, and asafoetida—seem trivial, but the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's logic is not about the food itself. The point is the effect these stimulating foods have on the practitioner's subtle states of mind: cooked, they feed lust; raw, they increase anger.

The practitioner's mind, as it enters deep meditative stability, becomes extraordinarily sensitive. Any subtle sensory stimulation can become a contributing condition for disturbance. The Buddha goes on to describe a chain of consequences—the one who eats the pungent herbs is shunned by the celestial beings of the ten directions, who recoil from his foul odor and withdraw; hungry ghosts, on the contrary, draw near to lick his lips; good spirits do not come to guard him; and in the end the mighty demon kings find their opening, even manifesting as a Buddha's body to teach him the Dharma.

This is not a moral verdict. It is a description of a causal mechanism—**a subtle auxiliary cause, if not removed, sets off a chain reaction in the deeper reaches of practice.**

### The Second Gradual Stage: Excise the Root Nature

> First maintain the śrāvaka precepts of the four and eight grave prohibitions—holding the body motionless. Then practice the bodhisattva's pure discipline—holding the mind unmoving.

"Excise"—to gouge out, to hollow away—is more thorough than "remove." The first gradual stage removes an external contributing condition (the five pungent herbs); the second must gouge out the inner root—lust, theft, killing, and falsehood. This is a deepening from leaf and branch to the root itself.

The most precise structure in this passage hides in the progression of two phrases: from "holding the body motionless" to "holding the mind unmoving"—from precept-keeping at the level of the body up to precept-keeping at the level of the mind. The śrāvaka precepts govern the conduct of the seven actions of body and speech (doing or not doing); the bodhisattva discipline governs the subtlety of the stirring thought itself (moving or not moving the thought). This upgrade is itself the "gradual stage"—coarse first, then subtle; body first, then mind.

⚠️ Here a word of caution—precept-keeping in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's context is not "following rules" in the social-moral sense. The progression from holding the body motionless to holding the mind unmoving shows that the true purpose of the precepts is the purification of mind, not the policing of behavior. A person whose outward conduct is perfectly within the precepts but whose heart is full of aversion has not completed the second gradual stage. The precepts here are a tool of inner purification, not an external standard of moral judgment.

### The Third Gradual Stage: Reverse the Present Karma

> Because of non-outflowing, one returns to the source. Since objects are no longer engaged, the roots have no counterparts. Reversing the flow into unity, the six functions cease to operate, and the lands of the ten directions are luminously pure—like crystal with a bright moon hung within.

"Reverse"—to go the opposite way. The first two gradual stages are "not doing" (not eating the five pungent herbs, not breaking the precepts); the third gradual stage is an **active reversal.** Not merely stopping the outward chase, but turning the entire habitual direction by which the six faculties chase the six objects clean around.

"Present karma" here is not past karma; it is the habitual pattern operating right now—the autopilot of the six faculties chasing the six objects. This habit does not need you to switch it on; it has been running all along. The work of the third gradual stage is to "reverse" this autopilot: to return to the source, to reverse the flow into unity.

The four characters "reversing the flow into unity" are the core action of the whole thing. **Reverse**—turn the direction around; **flow**—the streaming of faculty toward object; **into unity**—the functions of the six faculties returning to a single whole.

This is the same structure as Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty perfect penetration from the previous chapter, "entering the flow, losing the object"—not suppressing the six faculties, but letting their outward rush reverse into an inward return. The text goes on to say: "This person immediately attains the patience of the non-arising of dharmas"—on completing the third gradual stage, the practitioner gains not merely the ease of meditative stability but the true acceptance, in accordance with reality, that all dharmas neither arise nor cease. This is a major milestone on the bodhisattva path.

### Remove, Excise, Reverse—The Force of Three Verbs

Zǐxuán's eye was exceedingly sharp; he caught that these three verbs are not used carelessly:

- "Remove and discard it"—like clearing clutter off a table. The object is external, visible (the five pungent herbs). The action is lightest, because the auxiliary cause is not itself the fundamental problem.
- "Excise and empty it"—like gouging out a tumor from within the body. The object is internal, deep-rooted (lust, theft, killing, falsehood). The action is heavier, because the root nature is the fundamental cause of birth-and-death. The word "excise" is chosen with particular severity—not pruning, but uprooting entirely.
- "Reverse and turn away from it"—like reversing a river. The object is the most concealed of all: not some specific thing, but a direction, a habit. The action is deepest, because what you must reverse is not a single behavior but the entire default direction of the mind's operation.

This deepening across three layers of purification carries an important pedagogical meaning: **the practitioner must not skip ahead.** You cannot accomplish "reversing the flow into unity" while you are still eating the five pungent herbs—not because eating onion and garlic is morally grave, but because if the stimulation at the bodily level is not cleared away first, the mind lacks the keenness it needs to handle the deeper habits.

### Jiāoguāng's Correspondence

Jiāoguāng saw an even deeper structure—each of the three gradual stages corresponds precisely to one of the three phases of Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty perfect penetration from the previous chapter:

| Three Gradual Stages | Ear-Faculty Perfect Penetration |
|---|---|
| First stage (remove auxiliary causes) | Entering the flow, losing the object—reversing the hearing again and again, sometimes working, sometimes resting, not yet unified |
| Second stage (excise root nature) | Movement and stillness both cease—the moving object already forgotten, the still object gradually forgotten, the practice becoming unified |
| Third stage (reverse present karma) | Hearing and what is heard exhausted—faculty and object both dissolved, the flow reversed into unity |

This correspondence is no arbitrary parallel. It reveals something important: the three gradual stages and the ear-faculty perfect penetration are **two languages describing one and the same process of practice.** The three gradual stages speak from the angle of "purification through the precepts"; the ear-faculty penetration speaks from the angle of "the deepening of contemplative illumination." Mañjuśrī, in fascicle 6, selected the door (the ear-faculty); the Buddha, in fascicle 8, teaches you how to prepare to enter it (the three gradual stages)—and the way of entering is, from the start, the practice of reversing the hearing.

The previous chapter's "reverse the hearing to hear the self-nature" and this chapter's "reverse the flow into unity"—both use the same verb, "reverse."

### The Ground of Dry Wisdom—The Starting Line, Not the Finish Line

After the three gradual stages are complete, the practitioner enters the first of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's fifty-five stages—the ground of dry wisdom.

> Desire-love has dried up, roots and objects no longer paired, the present residual form no longer continuing to arise. The held mind is empty-bright, purely wisdom. The wisdom-nature, bright and complete, illuminates the ten directions. Dry in its wisdom—this is called the ground of dry wisdom.

"Dry in its wisdom"—there is wisdom, but it is dry. Wisdom-observation has been established ("purely wisdom, the wisdom-nature bright and complete"), but it has not yet been joined to the water of the Tathāgata's dharma-flow. The text goes on to say one utterly crucial line:

> The habit of desire only just dried, not yet connected with the water of the Tathāgata's dharma-flow.

The practitioner on the ground of dry wisdom has completed the purification of the three gradual stages, and his wisdom-observation is already bright and complete—**but he has not yet entered the holy stages.** To use a comparison—the seed is ready, the soil has been cleared, but the rain has not yet fallen.

This word "dry" points to a very deep reality of practice: you can wash the cup perfectly clean, but a clean cup is not the same as a cup with water in it. Purification is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

This answers a very common frustration in practice—"I have already worked so hard, why has the breakthrough not come?" The ground of dry wisdom tells you: **finishing the preparation is not the same as arriving at the destination.** Preparation qualifies you to stand at the starting line, but the running itself is another matter. From the ground of dry wisdom to the ten faiths, what is needed is not more effort but a shift in kind—from the contrived state of "I am practicing" into the responsive state in which "the dharma-flow water connects."

### The Name of the Starting Point Appears Again at the End

After the ground of dry wisdom, the Śūraṅgama Sūtra unfolds the full fifty-five stages in fascicle 8—the ten faiths, ten abodes, ten practices, ten dedications, four preparatory stages, ten grounds, equal enlightenment. But the Śūraṅgama Sūtra's most ingenious design appears at the conclusion of all the stages. After the entire course of practice from the ground of dry wisdom to equal enlightenment, the Buddha says one line:

> From the dry-wisdom mind to equal enlightenment—this enlightenment first obtains the initial ground of dry wisdom within the vajra mind. Thus, layer upon layer, single and compound twelve times, one exhausts wondrous enlightenment and achieves the unsurpassed Way.

The "initial ground of dry wisdom within the vajra mind" to which the equal-enlightenment bodhisattva returns—**the name of the starting point appears once more at the end.**

But this is not a simple repetition. The beginner's ground of dry wisdom is the first step, "not yet connected with the dharma-flow water"; the equal-enlightenment bodhisattva's "initial ground of dry wisdom" is the consummate return within the vajra mind. The same name of a place, an entirely different depth.

This is the rounded-teaching character of the Śūraṅgama's view of the stages: **practice is not a straight line but a spiral ring rising upward.** The single line of clarity you saw at the start and the same single line of clarity you face again at the end are the same thing—the difference being that the cultivation and realization in between have carried you from "knowing it is there" to "being it."

### The Śūraṅgama's Four Preparatory Stages

Within this whole system of stages, the four lines the Śūraṅgama Sūtra writes for the four preparatory stages (warmth, summit, patience, supreme worldly dharma) are the most beautiful in the entire passage, and the most worth pausing over—because each line is only a dozen or so characters, yet each compresses a complete experience of practice.

**Warmth: As if emerging yet not emerged, like drilling for fire when the wood is about to ignite.**

Yogācāra's stage of warmth comes with a precise technical description—which kind of meditative stability, what mode of contemplation, what is observed. The Śūraṅgama gives you only an image: fire **about to emerge but not yet emerged.** The one drilling for fire feels the wood beginning to heat, but the flame has not yet broken out.

What this image captures is a threshold experience—you sense some precursor of qualitative change, but you have not yet achieved the certainty of a breakthrough.

**Summit: Below, a subtle obstruction remains.**

Four characters. You have already reached the summit of the mountain, but beneath your feet there is still a thin layer of subtle obstruction. **The word "subtle" is the Śūraṅgama's distinctive sensitivity to the fine grain of practice**—not the gross obstructions (those were already handled in the three gradual stages), but the last residual, almost imperceptible barrier. You think you have passed all the way through, but there is still that little bit to go.

**Patience: Like one enduring something—neither held within nor released.**

Like a person enduring something, the thing in his heart can neither be kept hidden ("not held within") nor spat out ("not released"). "Neither held within nor released" describes an existential tension—you have touched something real, but this real thing is not something you can "possess" (hold within), nor something you can "express" (release). It transcends the cognitive relation of subject to object.

**Supreme Worldly Dharma: In the middle way between delusion and enlightenment, neither can be targeted.**

The summit of the entire four preparatory stages. Almost like a kōan. "Delusion" and "enlightenment" are the practitioner's most basic frame of reference—I am deluded, I want to become enlightened. The breakthrough of supreme worldly dharma is that **even this frame itself disappears.** Not "no longer deluded" (that still leaves enlightenment as a counterpart), not "enlightened" (that still leaves delusion to be left behind), but the middle way between delusion and enlightenment, in which even "targeting"—the cognitive aim, the taking-aim—no longer exists.

Take the four lines together—warmth (the precursor at the threshold), summit (the residual subtle obstruction), patience (the tension of neither held nor released), supreme worldly dharma (the dissolving of the coordinate frame).

These four qualities of experience do not appear only that once, between the ten dedications and the ten grounds. The line "layer upon layer, single and compound twelve times" hints that **this four-beat rhythm recurs at every threshold of practice.** The crossing from the ten faiths into the ten abodes has its own "warmth," "summit," "patience," and "supreme worldly dharma." So does the crossing from the ten abodes into the ten practices. Every passage from one level to the next repeats this same rhythm.

This also explains why the Śūraṅgama Sūtra writes the four preparatory stages so concisely—each line no more than a dozen or so characters. Not because they are unimportant, but precisely because they are so important: each must be condensed enough to be extracted as a pattern, applicable again and again at different depths. "As if emerging yet not emerged" does not describe only the first time drilling for fire; it describes the universal feeling of every moment about to break through. "Neither held within nor released" does not describe only that one moment of bearing at the stage of patience; it describes the universal state of every contact with a reality that transcends language.

### The Sterile Protocol Before Surgery

The logical structure of the three gradual stages can be illuminated by a contemporary comparison (⚠️ a heuristic analogy, not a theoretical equivalence)—

Modern surgery has a strict sterile protocol before any incision. Before the knife, the operating room passes through three layers of purification: the first layer, environmental sterilization (clearing external sources of contamination); the second layer, pre-operative preparation (the team scrubs in, dons sterile gowns; the patient's surgical site is sterilized); the third layer, the "time-out" just before the operation begins—the whole team pauses to confirm the patient's identity, the surgical site, and the procedure, reversing any potential automatic error.

The structure of the three gradual stages runs parallel to this: removing the auxiliary causes (environmental sterilization), excising the root nature (pre-operative preparation), reversing the present karma (the time-out reversal). The insight shared by both is this: **the precondition for a high-precision operation is thorough purification—and purification itself is not the operation.** No surgeon would say "the sterile preparation is the surgery," but no surgeon would skip the sterile preparation and cut straight in either.

⚠️ Here the analogy must be set down. Surgical sterility is at the physical level, contending against microorganisms; the purification of the three gradual stages is at the level of mind, contending against the afflictive habits of beginningless time. Surgery is a single event; the three gradual stages are a transformation at the level of one's whole life—not something that, once done, returns to its former state. This analogy helps you see the structure of "purification as prerequisite," but practice cannot be reduced to a standardized procedure.

### Three Places Easily Misread

**This is not vegetarian advocacy.** Severing the five pungent herbs is not the same as vegetarianism. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra's emphasis is not on the moral properties of food but on food's subtle effect on the practitioner's states of mind. The five pungent herbs are singled out because they directly disturb the practitioner's mental balance. This is not an ethical argument about "what one ought to eat"; it is a technical guideline about "what disturbs meditative stability." To reduce the three gradual stages to vegetarian advocacy is to swap a moral framework in for a practice framework.

**The ground of dry wisdom is not the same as awakening.** "The wisdom-nature bright and complete, illuminating the ten directions" sounds extraordinary, but the ground of dry wisdom is the starting line, not the finish line. "Not yet connected with the water of the Tathāgata's dharma-flow" already says it plainly—the establishing of wisdom-observation is not the same as entering the holy stages. The practitioner on the ground of dry wisdom has not yet entered even the ten faiths, let alone seen the path. To equate the ground of dry wisdom with awakening is to mistake the completion of preparation for the completion of the task.

**The fifty-five stages are not a fixed linear ladder.** Although the Śūraṅgama Sūtra arranges the fifty-five stages in strict order, the circular structure (the starting point appearing at the end) already hints at a view beyond the linear. Different scriptures and treatises use different stage systems—the Śūraṅgama's fifty-five stages, the Avataṃsaka's fifty-two, Yogācāra's five stages—describing the same course of practice through different classificatory frameworks. To absolutize any one stage system is itself a kind of attachment to dharma.

### Are You Ready?

The Śūraṅgama Sūtra uses the three gradual stages to answer a question that looks simple but is in fact profound—once you know which door to enter, **are you ready?**

The work of preparation is threefold: first remove the external interference (remove the auxiliary causes), then gouge out the inner fundamental obstruction (excise the root nature), and finally reverse the default direction of the mind (reverse the present karma). Each layer rests on the one before; none can be skipped.

Once preparation is complete, you stand on the ground of dry wisdom—the starting line. Wisdom-observation is formed, but the dharma-flow water has not yet connected. You have done everything within your power; what comes next requires not more effort but a resonance of a different kind.

Reading this far, perhaps you feel this road is too long. The scriptures speak of "three incalculable great eons"—three immeasurable great eons of practice before one becomes a Buddha. This is not a course you can finish in a ten-day meditation retreat.

But notice another thing—**that you can read this far, and understand it, is itself no accident.** If, when you reached "neither held within nor released," something stirred in your heart—even if only a faint sense that "this line seems to be saying something very important"—that stirring is itself evidence of accumulated provision.

So do not be discouraged, but do not be cavalier either. Every step counts. When the equal-enlightenment bodhisattva walks to the very end, what he returns to is still the "initial ground of dry wisdom"—but that "initial" is no longer the parched first step of long ago; it is consummation within the vajra mind.

The next chapter turns back to handle the four clear and decisive instructions on purity in the latter half of fascicle 6. The order here is worth pausing to consider: the four instructions are in the latter half of fascicle 6, the practice sanctuary and the Śūraṅgama mantra in fascicle 7, the three gradual stages in fascicle 8—the text first erects the protection of the precepts, then the protection of the space, and only last unfolds the purification of mind. So 6.7 is not about "the guardrails after entering the gate"; it is about **the first boundary set before one even enters.**

This chapter has spoken of the innermost layer—the purification of mind; the next chapter speaks of the outermost layer—the protection of the precepts. The three boundaries together are what make up the truly complete preparatory package for the ear-faculty perfect penetration.
