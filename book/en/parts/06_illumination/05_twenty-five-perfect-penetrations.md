## 6.5 The Twenty-Five Methods of Perfect Penetration—The Gate That Suits You

### Twenty-Five Doors Stand Before You

Twenty-five doors stand before you.

Each one leads to the same place. But not every door is one you can open.

By the end of the previous chapter, all dharmas had been gathered, every one of them, into the tathāgatagarbha. The theory had reached its end. Yet at the very moment it ended, Ānanda did something deeply honest—he did not say "I understand." Instead he asked the question that hurts a practitioner most.

### Alternating-Day Malaria

Ānanda said to the Buddha:

> World-Honored One, we in this assembly, the voice-hearers still in training, are like this as well. Since beginningless time, we have been born and have perished along with ignorance. Though we have obtained such roots of good through much learning and bear the name of "renunciants," we are like those afflicted with alternating-day malaria.

"Like those afflicted with alternating-day malaria"—these words are the most poignant confession in the entire Śūraṅgama Sūtra. Malaria flares one day, eases the next, then flares again. What Ānanda is saying is this: World-Honored One, I remember every word of your teaching, but my afflictions are like malaria—better for a while, worse for a while, and the root is never cut.

This is the chasm between knowing and realizing. You may be able to recite from memory every line on the four categories and seven elements—but the next time someone insults you, you will still flare with anger.

Ānanda then asked: "How are body and mind bound today? And from what point do they come untied?"—Today, where exactly has my body-and-mind tied itself into knots? And from where do they loosen?

The Buddha answered with the image of a silk cloth tied into six knots. The knots were not tied all at once, so they cannot be untied all at once—the key to untying lies in **untying in sequence**. Not opening everything in one stroke, but first finding the point of entry. Then he spoke a line that is the very hinge of the entire chapter on the twenty-five methods of perfect penetration:

> The six being untied, the one also vanishes. Select a faculty for perfect penetration; enter the stream and realize true awakening.

The six knots untied, even the concept of "untying" is set down—choose one faculty, enter the stream, and that is the road to true awakening.

But which faculty to choose? The Buddha did not prescribe the answer himself. He had twenty-five sages who had already realized the Way—bodhisattvas, arhats, dharma-princes—each stand up and report which entry-point he had once gone in through. Then he asked Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva to make the final selection.

This is not an exhibition of twenty-five methods. It is an empirical report on faculty-disposition fit.

### Four Ways of Entering the Way

The reports of the twenty-five sages were given a precise classification by the Song-dynasty master Zixuan—four gates.

**The gate of awakening through sense-objects**: entering through the object that is perceived. Ājñāta Kauṇḍinya, foremost of the five bhikṣus the Buddha first liberated after his awakening, entered through the "sense-object of sound"—he heard the Buddha speak the four noble truths, and within that very sound he awakened. He said, "The wondrous sound was secretly complete; through sound I attained arhatship."

**The gate of realization through sense-faculties**: entering through the faculty that perceives. Aniruddha's story is especially dramatic—rebuked by the Buddha as being "like an animal" for his fondness for sleep, he resolved to stay awake for seven days and went blind as a result. It was precisely after losing his physical eyes that he realized a seeing-nature beyond them: "Without relying on eyes, I see the ten directions—purely real and penetrating, like observing a fruit in my palm." This is the most direct empirical confirmation of the ten demonstrations of seeing—the seeing-nature does not depend on the physical eye, nor does it vanish when the physical eye is destroyed.

**The gate of cultivation through consciousness**: entering through the workings of mind-consciousness. Mahāmaudgalyāyana's account is "I reversed the clear, still radiance of my mind"—turning the radiance of mind-consciousness back upon itself, "like a muddy stream gradually becoming clear and pure." Let the turbid water settle slowly, and the clarity that was always there reveals itself.

**The gate of returning to nature through the great elements**: entering through the basic elements that constitute all dharmas. Mahāsthāmaprāpta's perfect penetration through mindfulness of the Buddha is the central scriptural foundation of the Pure Land school—"gathering in all six faculties with pure mindfulness in continuous succession," using the mindfulness of buddha-recollection to gather in the six faculties so that pure mindfulness continues without a break. He used a most beautiful image: "Like a person who has been near incense, whose body carries the fragrance—this is called adornment with fragrance and light." The fragrance on your body is not your own; it comes from your having stayed long in a fragrant environment, permeated by it.

These four represent four utterly different ways of entering the Way: through the sound that is heard, through the seeing-nature that sees, through the clarification of consciousness, through the continuity of buddha-recollection. What they share is not method but result—they all arrived. And where they differ is not in the merit of their methods but in their faculty-dispositions—each found the door that suited him best.

### Avalokiteśvara's Six Steps

When the twenty-five sages had finished reporting, Avalokiteśvara rose last to give his account. His report far surpasses the preceding twenty-four in both length and depth—and that in itself is a structural signal.

Avalokiteśvara first disclosed his causal ground—his own teacher was also named "Avalokiteśvara," master and disciple sharing one name. The teaching he received from that buddha was: "Enter samādhi through hearing, contemplation, and cultivation." These nine characters are the overarching framework of the entire ear-faculty's perfect penetration.

What follows is the most precise sequence of practice in the whole Śūraṅgama Sūtra. Avalokiteśvara describes in six steps how he walked the path:

> At first, within hearing, I entered the stream and the object vanished. The object of entry being still, both movement and stillness became clearly non-arising. Gradually increasing in this way, hearing and what is heard were exhausted. Not abiding even where hearing was exhausted, awareness and what is aware became empty. Emptiness and awareness perfected to the utmost, emptiness and what is emptied were extinguished. Arising and ceasing having ceased, quiescent extinction manifested.

**Step one: entering the stream, the object vanishes.** Within the operation of hearing, reverse its direction. Do not flow outward after the sense-object of sound (do not run out after the sound), but turn inward to contemplate that which is able to hear. "Entering the stream" is reversing the flow; "the object vanishes" is the dying-away of what is heard.

**Step two: movement and stillness do not arise.** Once the heard sound-object has died away, the paired concepts of "sound present" and "sound absent" no longer arise either. People ordinarily think "stillness" means "no sound"—but at this step, even the opposition of "movement / stillness" itself must be transcended.

**Step three: hearing and what is heard are exhausted.** Both the capacity to hear and what is heard are exhausted. This is the first layer of emptiness—emptying the subject-object duality of hearing.

**Step four: awareness and what is aware become empty.** Do not linger in the awareness that "hearing has been exhausted"—empty even "awareness" itself and "the object of awareness."

**Step five: emptiness and what is emptied are extinguished.** Even "emptiness" and "the object that has been emptied" are extinguished. Emptiness pushed to its limit, until even "emptiness" itself cannot be found.

**Step six: quiescent extinction manifests.** The entire process of the first five steps belongs to the realm of arising and ceasing; when all arising and ceasing comes to rest, quiescent extinction—that nature of neither arising nor ceasing—manifests of itself.

These six steps are not a linear staircase but a **layer-by-layer negation**: first empty what is heard, then empty the one who hears, then empty awareness, then empty emptiness itself, and finally even the act of "emptying emptiness" disappears. Each step is the negation of the one before—you think you have arrived, but you have not. Only when even the distinction between "arrived" and "not arrived" disappears does that which was always there manifest.

⚠️ One thing to note: "gradually increasing" is the wording of the sūtra itself, and it means a logical sequence of priority, not a set of rigid stages. The actual experience of practice is often that certain steps alternate, recur, and deepen—not that you arrive once and never look back. Hardening the six steps into fixed stages will breed in you an attachment to "which step am I on now"—whereas the core spirit of Avalokiteśvara's six steps is precisely to let go layer by layer, including letting go of keeping score of "where am I."

### Circular, Penetrating, Constant—Mañjuśrī's Criteria

When all twenty-five perfect penetrations had been recounted, the Buddha commanded Mañjuśrī to select, for Ānanda and for the beings of the Dharma-ending age, the one gate most suited to them. Mañjuśrī answered in verse, and his opening four lines are of the utmost importance:

> Returning to the source, the nature is non-dual; the expedient means are many. The nature of the holy is without obstruction; both the forward and the reverse are expedient methods.

Returning to the original nature, there is only one (non-dual), but the gates of expedient means are many. The nature of the sages is without any obstruction.—These four lines first establish equality. Then:

> Yet for the beginner entering samādhi, the speed is not the same.

For the beginner, the speed of entering samādhi genuinely does differ.

From these two seemingly contradictory statements, you can see Mañjuśrī's stance in selecting: **all gates are equal when seen from the fruit; but seen from the practical efficiency of the beginner's mind, there really is a difference in what suits and what does not.**

Mañjuśrī then used a uniform four-line format to eliminate the other twenty-four perfect penetrations one by one, each elimination ending with "how can this yield perfect penetration?" When the eliminations were complete, he used three "authenticities" to set forth the supremacy of the ear-faculty—

**Circular authenticity**: "Suppose a person sits quietly while drums are struck in all ten directions—all ten places are heard at once." The eye can only see what is in front; nose, mouth, body, and mind each have their limits; only the ear-faculty can hear the sounds of all ten directions at once. This is **encompassing all without remainder**.

**Penetrating authenticity**: "One can hear sounds through walls, whether far or near." Walls do not block sound; far and near can both be heard. This is **unobstructed freedom**.

**Constant authenticity**: "When sound is absent, hearing does not cease; when sound is present, hearing does not arise." When there is sound, the hearing-nature does not arise on that account; when there is no sound, the hearing-nature does not cease on that account. The hearing-nature transcends the presence and absence of sound—and this is exactly the auditory counterpart of the ten demonstrations of seeing, "the seeing-nature does not perish with objects."

Circular, penetrating, and constant, fused into one—this is the precise doctrinal meaning of the two characters "perfect penetration."

### Four Conditions

The master Jiaoguang drew the logic of Mañjuśrī's selection tighter still. He pointed out that Mañjuśrī used four criteria to eliminate the other penetrations one by one—and the reverse of these four criteria is precisely the ear-faculty's four advantages:

**Suited to the locality**—it fits the faculty-disposition of beings in the sahā world. The "teaching-substance" of the sahā world is sound—the Buddha teaches the Dharma through sound, and beings receive it through hearing. "The true teaching-substance of this land is pure and lies in hearing." Other buddha-lands may carry out the Buddha's work through light, fragrance, or silence (as in the Fragrant Accumulation buddha-land, where there are "no words; only various fragrances"), but in this world the Buddha's work is accomplished through sound.

**Convenient for beginners**—a beginner can take it up at once. Unlike Aniruddha, who needed a special karmic circumstance (the extreme situation of blindness), or Mahāmaudgalyāyana, who needed a deep foundation in supernatural powers.

**Requiring no special prerequisites**—it needs no extra conditions. Some methods require specific preliminary practices, initiations, transmissions, or special precept-substances. The ear-faculty needs none of these.

**Viable for sustained cultivation**—it can be practiced steadily over the long term. It is not a one-time triggering of opportunity (as in certain Chan kōans), but a method you can keep working at day by day.

What Mañjuśrī selected was not "the most sublime method" but "**the method the most people can use**"—a pedagogical fit, not a doctrinal ranking.

### Reverse the Hearing to Hear the Self-Nature

Mañjuśrī's verse finally comes to rest on a single core operation:

> Reverse the hearing to hear the self-nature; the nature realizes the unsurpassed Way.

The operative meaning of "reverse hearing" is this: turn the direction of the hearing-nature around. Ordinarily, the hearing-nature flows outward—hearing sounds, discriminating meanings, generating reactions. Reverse hearing is to stop the hearing-nature from chasing the sound-object outward and instead turn it back to contemplate itself—what is it that is able to hear.

This corresponds exactly to the "seeing-nature" of the ten demonstrations of seeing. The ten demonstrations asked: close your eyes, what do you see? You answer "I see nothing"—but you have forgotten that you are seeing. The version in the twenty-five methods of perfect penetration is: in a silent room, what do you hear? You answer "I hear nothing"—but you have forgotten that you are hearing. The very judgment "I hear nothing" proves that the hearing-nature is operating. If the hearing-nature were not present, you would not even know that there was no sound.

Jiaoguang gave a most concrete guide to practice:

> Rising and sitting at the fifth watch, when the night air is clear and the myriad sounds have just begun to stir, with not a single thought arising—perceive this hearing-nature, vast and circular, clear and illuminating.

No special environment or ritual is needed. In the early morning at the fifth watch, when everything around is at its quietest, with not a single thought arising—right there in that moment, recognize the hearing-nature that can hear all things. It is vast (reaching in every direction), clear (unobstructed by anything), and constant (neither arising nor ceasing with the presence or absence of sound).

⚠️ Jiaoguang warned at the same time against two common errors. The first: taking "within hearing" to mean **within the physical ear**—understanding reverse hearing as concentrating attention on the ears. This is a materialistic misunderstanding. The second: taking "within hearing" to mean **within ear-consciousness**—understanding reverse hearing as observing one's own mental process of discriminating sound. This falls into the discursive thinking of the sixth consciousness. The true "within hearing" transcends both the physical ear and ear-consciousness, working directly at the level of the hearing-nature. If, while "reversing the hearing," you are still thinking in your mind "I am reversing the hearing"—then what you are reversing is not the hearing-nature but a concept in the sixth consciousness.

### The Auditory Channel of the Mother Tongue

Why should the default entry-point of the sahā world be, of all faculties, the ear? Here there is a structural observation we can confirm in the present day (⚠️ a heuristic analogy, not a theoretical equivalence)—

Human first-language acquisition begins almost entirely with hearing. Even before birth, the infant has already received the prosodic patterns of its mother's speech through hearing within the womb. After birth, long before it can produce meaningful speech, it is already hearing—distinguishing phonetic boundaries, perceiving the rise and fall of intonation, building categorical representations of speech sounds. Vision plays a supporting role in language acquisition too (lip-reading, expression), but hearing is the overwhelmingly dominant channel. A congenitally deaf child, without a sign-language environment, suffers severely delayed language development—and this fact confirms, by negation, hearing's default status.

The logic of Mañjuśrī's selecting the ear-faculty runs structurally parallel to this observation of a "default channel": under the ordinary conditions of the sahā world, the ear-faculty is the default channel for receiving the Dharma. Change the buddha-land (as with the Fragrant Accumulation buddha-land, which does the Buddha's work through fragrance), and the default channel changes; change the individual whose senses are impaired (as with Aniruddha's blindness), and the default channel can be rewritten too.

⚠️ But the analogy can go only this far. Linguistics deals with a symbol system—language is constituted of phonemes, morphemes, and grammatical rules, and hearing is the channel that receives this system. Reverse hearing deals not with a symbol system but with the hearing-nature itself—not with what you hear, but with what that is which is able to hear. Language acquisition relies on the "content" of hearing (discriminating the differences between sounds); the crux of reverse-hearing practice is precisely to let go of the "content" of hearing (entering the stream, the object vanishes) and return to the "capacity" of hearing itself. The directions are exactly opposite.

### Three Places Easily Misread

**Absolutizing "the ear-faculty is best."** The most common misreading is to understand Mañjuśrī's selection as "the ear-faculty's perfect penetration is best under all conditions." But Mañjuśrī himself opened by saying "Returning to the source, the nature is non-dual; the nature of the holy is without obstruction"—the selection is an optimal recommendation under the three specific conditions of "this land," "the beginner," and "the Dharma-ending age," not an absolute doctrinal ranking that transcends all conditions.

**Devotion to Avalokiteśvara ≠ Avalokiteśvara's method.** Devotion to Avalokiteśvara is extremely widespread in the culture of Chinese Buddhism—reciting the name, chanting sūtras, prostrations, petitionary prayers. These are all meritorious practices. But they are a different level of practice from the ear-faculty's perfect penetration that Avalokiteśvara recounts of himself in the Śūraṅgama Sūtra. Reciting "Namo Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva" is **outward-directed faith**—taking Avalokiteśvara's vow-power as a contributing condition; "entering the stream, the object vanishes / reverse the hearing to hear the self-nature" is **inward-directed cultivation**—taking one's own hearing-nature as the object of practice. The two can complement each other, but they must not be confused. Equating devotion to Avalokiteśvara with Avalokiteśvara's method obscures the precise operational guidance of the ear-faculty's perfect penetration.

**The mindfulness-of-the-Buddha method is not being negated.** Mañjuśrī's verse eliminating Mahāsthāmaprāpta's perfect penetration through mindfulness of the Buddha—"All conditioned activities are impermanent; the nature of mindfulness is originally arising-and-ceasing"—is most easily read as "mindfulness of the Buddha is ineffective." This is a serious misreading. Zixuan clarified it specifically: "The mindfulness-of-the-Buddha approach is the most essential for this land"—the mindfulness-of-the-Buddha method is effective; through the concentration of pure mindfulness combined with the Buddha's vow-power, one can take birth in the Pure Land, and after taking birth there, with further cultivation, realization is assured. Jiaoguang's analysis is more precise: using mindfulness as the cause to seek birth and the sight of the Buddha—cause and fruit correspond; but using this arising-and-ceasing mindfulness as the cause to seek the present-life realization of the non-arising, non-ceasing perfect penetration—there cause and fruit are of different categories. This is only a comparison within the specific criterion of "perfect penetration," not a negation of the overall value of the mindfulness-of-the-Buddha method. The mindfulness-of-the-Buddha method walks another road that arrives just as surely—only the manner of arriving differs—and this is something very important for those who practice mindfulness of the Buddha; it must not be misread.

### That Which Hears Has Always Been There

Twenty-five doors, all leading to the same place. You need only open one.

If, having read this chapter, you feel a deep resonance with the practice sequence of Avalokiteśvara's ear-faculty perfect penetration—that is good. But please remember: understanding is not the endpoint of practice. It is not even its starting point. The starting point of practice is when you actually sit down, in the very quietest moment, stop all thoughts, and listen—not for any sound, but for that which is able to hear.

That which hears has always been there.

You have found the door and entered the practice—and then? How do you avoid going astray? How do you tell an illusory state from a true one?

The next chapter—The Three Gradual Stages and the Ground of Dry Wisdom—is what addresses precisely this question. The four categories and seven elements told you "everything is originally tathāgatagarbha" (the principle); the twenty-five methods of perfect penetration tell you "where to begin" (the door); the three gradual stages tell you "how to walk after entering the door" (the path).
