## 6.9 The Ten Māras of the Feeling Aggregate — The Refinement of the Second Arrow

### The Second Arrow

Twenty-five hundred years ago, the Buddha offered the monks a parable.

> The ignorant, unlearned worldling gives rise to pleasant feeling, painful feeling, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling; the well-instructed noble disciple also gives rise to pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Monks—what is the difference between the worldling and the sage?
>
> It is as if a person were struck in the body by two poisoned arrows, and were wracked with extreme pain. So too is the ignorant, unlearned worldling: he compounds two feelings, bodily feeling and mental feeling, and is wracked with extreme pain.
>
> When the well-instructed noble disciple is touched in the body by a painful feeling, oppressed by great suffering even to the loss of life, he does not give rise to grief, lament, weeping, wailing, or derangement of mind. At that time he gives rise to one feeling only—bodily feeling—and not to mental feeling.

This is Sūtra 470 of the *Saṃyukta-āgama*, known to later tradition as the "Arrow Sūtra" or the parable of the two arrows.

The force of the parable lies in its dissection of "suffering" into two distinct arrows.

**The first arrow:** bodily feeling—the physical sensation of the body, which any sentient being will be struck by.
**The second arrow:** mental feeling—the reaction to bodily feeling, the interpretation, the resistance, the longing. This arrow is the one the worldling fires into himself.

The noble disciple is not free of the first arrow. What he is able to do is sever the second arrow before it follows.

The parable looks simple, yet it is the early canon's most exact dissection of "suffering." It means: **the core of suffering is not feeling itself but the judgment passed upon feeling.** The body may be struck by a poisoned arrow, but what truly drives the worldling into "grief, lament, weeping, wailing, derangement of mind" has never been the first arrow. It is the second.

A question follows. When a practitioner has already crossed the form aggregate and seen "the minds of all the buddhas appearing as images in a clear mirror"—that is, when he has entered a state of absorption no longer disturbed by ordinary bodily feeling—**where has the second arrow gone?**

The answer is: **the second arrow has not vanished. It has become invisible.**

### The Refinement of the Two Arrows in the Depths of Absorption

The central claim of this chapter can be stated in a single sentence: **the ten māras of the feeling aggregate are not a new category of demonic territory invented by the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*; they are the refined return, at the depths of absorption, of the early canon's "second arrow."**

But first we must answer the question left hanging from the last chapter—**why is the feeling aggregate harder than the form aggregate?**

Each of the ten realms of the form aggregate has a clear *object*. The body passes through obstructions, one sees worms within one's own body, one sees buddhas, one sees jeweled colors, one sees objects in a dark room… each of these ten realms has an external, identifiable, refusable object. When you "do not generate sagely conceit," you can say: "this body that passes through walls is not my sagely realization; this buddha-image that appears is not my sagely realization." **The thing to be refused is clear.**

And the ten realms of the feeling aggregate? The first is "boundless pity," the second "boundless courage," the third "fixed brooding without dispersal," the fourth "settling for little gained," the fifth "ceaseless sorrow," the sixth "boundless welling joy," the seventh "the towering of great conceit," the eighth "boundless pacification," the ninth "the verdict of nihilistic emptiness," the tenth "boundless arising love."

**Notice the nature of these things—they are not "objects," they are "states."** They are not what I see; they are what I feel. How do you refuse "boundless welling joy"? How do you refuse "boundless pacification"?—these are the very things the practitioner has been pursuing all along.

This is the root reason the feeling aggregate is harder than the form aggregate: **the form aggregate's danger lies in misidentifying the "object" (mistaking an outer state for sagely realization); the feeling aggregate's danger lies in misidentifying the "act of awareness" (mistaking a state of mind for sagely realization).** The object has shape and form; the act of awareness has neither. The object can be refused; the act of awareness can only be seen through. **Seeing through is far harder than refusing.**

At the worldling's stage the two arrows are distinct—the body's pain is bodily feeling, the story "I am ruined" is mental feeling, and you have time to insert "knowing-as-it-is" between the two. In the depths of absorption the two arrows arise almost together. The first arrow is refined into "the savor of meditative absorption," "subtle joy," "boundless lightness," "the vast openness of luminous clarity"—these are still feelings, only that the coarse sensation of the physical body has become the reception of an inner attainment. The second arrow is refined into the instantaneous, silent, self-endorsing verdict "I have attained sagely realization."

**The two arrows arise almost simultaneously, almost identically in appearance, almost indistinguishably.** This is the foundational difficulty of the ten māras of the feeling aggregate.

### The Region of the Feeling Aggregate — Like a Person in a Nightmare

The definition of the feeling aggregate comes in the ninth fascicle:

> Ānanda, when this good man cultivating *samādhi* has, in *śamatha*, exhausted the form aggregate, he sees the minds of all the buddhas as images appearing in a clear mirror. If he attains something but cannot yet make use of it, he is like a person under the spell of a nightmare—his hands and feet intact, his seeing and hearing unconfused, but his mind touched by intruding deviance and unable to move. This is called the region of the feeling aggregate. When the nightmare lifts and his mind departs from his body, able to look back upon its own face and to come and go freely without obstruction, this is called the exhaustion of the feeling aggregate. Examining its source, **empty-luminous false thinking** is its root.

**"He sees the minds of all the buddhas as images appearing in a clear mirror"**—this is an extraordinarily lofty stage, beyond the worldling's wildest dreams. Yet though he sees the minds of all the buddhas, the practitioner "attains something but cannot yet make use of it"—he can see but cannot use.

Then the sūtra reaches for a vivid simile: **like a person in a nightmare.**

Like one in the grip of a nightmare—his hands and feet are all there, his seeing and hearing are clear, but his mind is touched by intruding deviance and he cannot move. This is the essential nature of the region of the feeling aggregate: **already seeing but not yet able to use; already opened but not yet free of the body.**

The root of the form aggregate is "solid false thinking" (the fixedness of the sense of materiality); the root of the feeling aggregate is **empty-luminous false thinking**—the false-thinking structure of "empty-penetrating reception." From the solid to the empty-luminous is a transition from the formed to the formless. This transition is what makes the feeling aggregate harder to cure—the solid can be broken; the empty-luminous cannot be broken, it can only be seen through.

### The Ten Māras of the Feeling Aggregate

Below we walk through the ten māras one by one. The textual structure of each is the same formula—**occasion of arising → excessive reaction → "this is called X" → "recognized, there is no harm, for it is not sagely realization; taken as sagely realization, the X-māra enters his heart-organ" → he loses correct *samādhi* and falls into sinking destruction.**

**The first māra: the māra of pity.** Attaining great radiance, his inward suppression goes to excess, and suddenly boundless pity arises. Seeing even mosquitoes and gnats as his own infants, he weeps without noticing. This is called "the function of self-reproach gone to excess"—to spur and reproach oneself was a means of breaking the feeling aggregate, but suppression in excess gives rise to a pathological pity.

**The second māra: the māra of frenzy.** When victorious signs appear before him, his gratitude goes to excess, and suddenly boundless courage arises. His will would equal all the buddhas; he holds that the three incalculable kalpas can be transcended in a single thought. This is called "the function of rashness gone to excess."

**The third māra: the māra of recollection.** Nothing new is realized ahead, and the old ground has been left behind. Intellectual force weakens; brooding recollection will not disperse; and he mistakes this for the appearance of diligent effort. This is called "training the mind without wisdom, losing oneself."

**The fourth māra: the māra of premature contentment.** Wisdom-power exceeds concentration; the keen edge is lost. Holding victorious natures within his heart, he privately suspects himself to be Vairocana and settles for the little he has gained. He tells others: "I have attained the supreme, ultimate truth." This is called "the use of mind that has lost the constant deliberation and sunk into knowledge."

**The fifth māra: the māra of ceaseless sorrow.** The new attainment ungained, the old mind already gone, surveying both edges he gives birth to peril, and suddenly boundless sorrow arises. As though seated on a bed of iron, as though drinking poison, **the mind no longer wishes to live, and he constantly seeks others to take his life** so as to obtain liberation early. This is called "cultivation that has lost skillful means." ⚠️ Note that the fifth māra has progressed to the literal level of self-harm and the wish for death—this is not metaphor; it is a meditative pathology the *Śūraṅgama* explicitly warns against.

**The sixth māra: the māra of uncontrolled delight.** When his mind dwells settled and at ease in clear stillness, suddenly boundless joy arises of itself; his heart is glad past all restraint. He sings and dances by the roadside, declaring himself to have attained unhindered liberation. This is called "pacification without the wisdom to restrain."

**The seventh māra: the māra of towering conceit.** Declaring himself sufficient, he suddenly gives rise to boundless conceit without cause. The seven kinds of conceit erupt at once; he holds the tathāgatas of the ten directions in contempt, refuses to bow at shrines, and destroys scriptures and images. He says to donors: "These are metal and clay, or wood; the scriptures are tree-leaves, or cloth; this body of flesh is the truly eternal—yet you do not revere it and instead worship clay and wood; this is upside down." This is called "seeing the victorious without the wisdom to save oneself."

**The eighth māra: the māra of satisfied lightness.** Suddenly he declares himself to have "attained great accord," to have gained great radiance and perfect comprehension throughout the world, and proclaims himself "born into the pure land of the buddhas, leaving no residual coarseness behind." This is called "from wisdom acquiring various kinds of lightness."

**The ninth māra: the māra of emptiness.** Within his luminous understanding he attains the empty-luminous nature, and **denies cause and effect, plunging entirely into emptiness**, declaring this eternal extinction to be liberation, and denying cause and effect. His mind, even in the Avīci hell, feels neither grief nor fear; he comes to eat the wine and meat of his donors and to engage in defiled conduct. This is called "the mind of concentration sunk and submerged, losing its illuminating response."

**The tenth māra: the māra of desire.** **Savoring the empty-luminous, he lets it penetrate to the bone.** Suddenly his heart gives rise to boundless love; **love at its peak becomes derangement, and so becomes lust.** He gathers white-robed lay followers to practice desire as equals, and names the fornicators "true sons of the Dharma." This is called "the meditative state settling smoothly into the heart, entering without the wisdom to hold oneself."

The verdict on all ten māras is the same formula: **recognized, there is no harm, for it is not sagely realization; taken as sagely realization, he loses correct *samādhi* and falls into sinking destruction.**

### Five Paired Reverse-Overextensions

If the ten māras were merely ten parallel items, they would be nothing but a list. But look closely at their inner workings and you find a surprising structure—**five paired reverse-overextensions.** The two māras of each pair are **not parallel but opposed**—they are two reverse-overextensions of one and the same occasion.

| Pair | The Two Māras | Reverse-Overextension | Common Occasion |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Pity (1) ↔ Frenzy (2) | Inward suppression vs. rashness | Reaction to first seeing the minds of the buddhas |
| II | Recollection (3) ↔ Premature Contentment (4) | Backward longing vs. settling for little | Reaction to "no new attainment" |
| III | Sorrow (5) ↔ Delight (6) | Despair vs. ecstasy | Facing the perils of the two edges |
| IV | Conceit (7) ↔ Satisfied Lightness (8) | Aggressive arrogance vs. stagnant complacency | Reaction to "self-attainment" |
| V | Emptiness (9) ↔ Desire (10) | Annihilation vs. savoring | Reaction to "the empty-luminous nature" |

**Pair I** (pity ↔ frenzy): both māras arise on the occasion of "attaining great radiance." The pity-māra collapses inward into self-reproach; the frenzy-māra inflates outward into bravado. One says "sentient beings are too miserable and I have realized too late"; the other says "the three incalculable kalpas can be transcended in a single thought."

**Pair II** (recollection ↔ premature contentment): both māras face the threshold of "no new attainment." The recollection-māra tries to retreat (longing for the lost old ground); the contentment-māra tries to halt ahead (privately suspecting himself to be Vairocana).

**Pair III** (sorrow ↔ delight): both māras stand amid "the perils of the two edges." One wants to die (the fifth māra has reached literal suicide); the other revels madly (the sixth māra has reached singing and dancing in the street).

**Pair IV** (conceit ↔ satisfied lightness): both māras arise on the occasion of "self-attainment." Towering conceit expresses "I am higher than all" through attack; satisfied lightness expresses "I already have enough" through stasis.

**Pair V** (emptiness ↔ desire): both māras arise on the occasion of "savoring the empty-luminous." The emptiness-māra uses annihilation to legitimize indulgence ("denying cause and effect, engaging broadly in defiled conduct"); the desire-māra legitimizes indulgence directly ("savoring the empty-luminous to the bone, love at its peak becoming derangement"). **Two endings that look opposite converge strangely in behavior**—one uses "emptiness" to legitimize desire, the other simply embraces desire.

The insight of this structure is: **the structure of the form aggregate is layered (body → hearing → feeling → emptiness → other); the structure of the feeling aggregate is paired (suppression vs. rashness, retreat vs. settling for little…). The structure of objects is layered; the structure of judgments is paired.**

And neither pole of any pair is the answer—**the Middle Way lies between the two poles of each pair.** Against pity one is not to swing to frenzy; against frenzy one is not to swing to pity; against recollection one is not to switch to contentment; against contentment one is not to revert to recollection. The Middle Way is to find, between each pair's two extremes, that unbiased position of "not generating sagely conceit."

### To Grasp Is to Draw In the Māra

In his concluding verdict on the first māra (the māra of pity), the master Zǐxuán set down a twelve-character maxim that can serve as the axis of the entire ten māras of the feeling aggregate:

> This is not sagely realization; the moment one grasps it, demons are drawn in. The other [nine] all follow this pattern.

Translated, these twelve characters say: these states are not themselves sagely realization; **the moment you grasp them as sagely realization, the māra is drawn in.** The other nine realms are all handled in the same way. Twelve characters say it all.

In the form aggregate section the Buddha's "do not generate sagely conceit" was enough; the feeling aggregate section requires "do not grasp"—not only must one not generate the conceit of sagely realization, one must not even "grasp" at the meditative state at all. "Grasping" is more refined than "generating"—"generating" is conscious contrivance, while "grasping" can be unconscious clinging. From "not generating" to "not grasping" is the refinement from the worldling's stage to the feeling aggregate's stage.

Why does the feeling aggregate section tighten its tone to such urgency? Zǐxuán gives a structural explanation—**the feeling aggregate is "the head of the four mind-aggregates."**

The form aggregate is form-dharma, belonging to the body; the four aggregates of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are mind-dharmas, belonging to the mind. To cross from the form aggregate to the feeling aggregate is to cross from "the practice of the body" to "the practice of the mind." The form aggregate is only the threshold; the feeling aggregate is the doorway—once one enters the feeling aggregate, the practitioner has entered the genuine territory of psychological practice.

The feeling aggregate is the first of the mind-dharmas—the meeting of faculty, object, and consciousness yields contact, contact yields feeling, feeling yields perception, perception yields formation, formation turns into consciousness. Feeling is the source link of the chain of psychological dependent origination. Break the first link and the whole chain is broken. This is why the sūtra says that one who breaks the feeling aggregate "is now able, from this ordinary body, to ascend through the sixty sagely stations of the bodhisattva"—the exhaustion of the feeling aggregate is the doorway into the sagely stations.

This is also why the verdict on every realm of the ten māras of the feeling aggregate is "he loses correct *samādhi* and falls into sinking destruction"—repeated ten times. This is not textual redundancy; it is structural alarm. **The worst outcome of the ten realms of the form aggregate is "harboring the māras" (the verdict on the tenth form-realm); the worst outcome of the ten realms of the feeling aggregate is the tenfold repetition of "losing correct *samādhi*"**—and to lose correct *samādhi* is to lose *samādhi* itself. Why does the tone of the feeling aggregate section suddenly turn urgent? Because from here on, any small slip of judgment can end the whole road.

### The Differential Diagnosis of the Three Feelings and the Three Latent Tendencies

The early canon offers another diagnostic tool. *Saṃyukta-āgama* 468 maps the three feelings onto three root afflictions—pleasant feeling corresponds to the latent tendency to craving, painful feeling to the latent tendency to aversion, neutral feeling to the latent tendency to ignorance. This mapping can become a clinical triage structure for the ten māras:

| Feeling-System | Corresponding Tendency | Māras of the Feeling Aggregate |
|---|---|---|
| Pleasant-feeling system (craving) | Retention and expansion of "self-attainment" | 6 Delight, 7 Conceit, 8 Lightness, 10 Desire |
| Painful-feeling system (aversion) | Reaction to "loss" | 1 Pity, 3 Recollection, 5 Sorrow |
| Neutral-feeling system (ignorance) | Misjudgment of "the present condition" | 2 Frenzy, 4 Contentment, 9 Emptiness |

Placing towering conceit in the pleasant-feeling system may seem surprising. But if you trace conceit to its root, its core is "declaring himself sufficient, holding victorious natures within his heart"—this is a self-satisfied pleasure that merely expresses itself as outward aggression. **The substance of conceit is the distortion of pleasant feeling.**

The "aversion" of the three painful-feeling māras (pity, recollection, ceaseless sorrow) is not aversion toward others but **self-aversion**—aversion at oneself for realizing too late, aversion at oneself for having lost what one had, aversion at oneself for being alive. In the feeling aggregate section the latent tendency to aversion takes the refined form of self-aversion, not aversion toward others.

When you sense that you may have fallen into grasping at some meditative state, you can ask yourself—is this the grasping of pleasant feeling (craving)? the grasping of painful feeling (aversion)? or the misjudgment of a neutral feeling (ignorance)? Each of the three directions has its own antidote: for the pleasant-feeling system, "contemplate pleasant feeling as suffering"; for the painful-feeling system, "contemplate painful feeling as a sword-thrust"; for the neutral-feeling system, "contemplate neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling as impermanent." **The three contemplations of the three feelings are the antidote to the ten māras of the feeling aggregate**—only this antidote, a basic lesson at the worldling's stage, must be applied at the depths of the feeling aggregate with extraordinary precision.

### Three of the Easiest Misreadings

**Not everyday emotions.** The first and most common misreading is to identify the sadness, excitement, complacency, despair, or lust of everyday life as the "māras of the feeling aggregate." This is a serious confusion of levels. The ten māras of the feeling aggregate arise only in the depths of absorption after "the form aggregate is exhausted, the feeling aggregate clarified"—their precondition is that the practitioner has already crossed the form aggregate, suppressed the five hindrances, and entered deep *samādhi*. Everyday emotions belong to the level of the five hindrances, the coarse defilements; their antidote is foundational mindfulness of feeling and basic mindfulness training, not the framework of the fifty skandha-māras. To call everyday sadness "the māra of pity" is to call a common cold "lung cancer"—a misclassification, and so a mistaken treatment.

**"External māras creep in" is not a literal doctrine of spirit-possession.** Jiāoguāng says of the feeling aggregate section that "the external māras have already been summoned, yet they only creep into the body, and the māra has not yet manifested in person"—a line often read literally as "an external spirit-entity has entered the practitioner's body." This reading turns the whole feeling aggregate section into mysticism and forfeits its function as an instrument of psychological discernment. The correct understanding is—**"the external māra creeps in" is a phenomenological description, not an ontological assertion.** It describes the *mode of appearance* of the feeling aggregate's demonic event (the deformation of the practitioner's own act of judgment). Jiāoguāng himself says it plainly: "the māra darkly enters the heart of the practitioner himself and causes him to overturn himself unaware." **"To overturn himself unaware"—the practitioner has overturned himself, but to him this overturning is invisible**, because its content is "self-endorsement" (I have attained sagely realization). When you are in the act of endorsing the "I," how can you simultaneously see the "I" overturning? The act of endorsing is itself the overturning, but there is no second "I" who can step out and see it.

**The tenth māra is not a literal doctrine of anti-sexual asceticism.** The sūtra's description of the desire-māra is exceedingly strong ("he transforms white-robed laity into practitioners of desire as equals, and the fornicators are named true sons of the Dharma"), and is often read as the *Śūraṅgama*'s "anti-sexual creed." This is a superficial misreading. The core of the tenth māra is not "sexual conduct" but the subtle inner transition by which "**the savor of the meditative state becomes the bone of craving and love**." The order of the text is clear—first "**savoring the empty-luminous, penetrating to the bone**" (the aesthetic tasting of the meditative state), then "boundless love arises" (the budding of love), and only then "love at its peak becomes derangement, becoming lust" (the eruption). Lust is the final detonation of the inner transition, not its starting point. **The starting point is "savoring." The object of treatment is "savoring," not "lust."** The true antidote is to see through it in the very moment of "savoring the empty-luminous"—not to treat the meditative state as something that can be tasted. Once you begin to taste, love is born; once love is born, lust is on the way.

### The Antidote Is to See Through, Not to Suppress

There is one more crucial point—the antidote to the māras of the feeling aggregate is not to suppress feeling but to **not generate sagely conceit.**

The antidote to the first māra is not to suppress compassion but to not take that compassion for sagely realization. The antidote to the sixth māra is not to suppress joy but to not take that joy for unhindered liberation. The antidote to the tenth māra is not to reject the meditative state itself but to not generate "savoring" (the possessive tasting) toward that state.

**The object of the antidote is always the judgment, never the phenomenon.** To misread the antidote as suppression results in the practitioner falling into the new grasping "I must hold down my own joy"—and this grasping is itself another form of "losing correct *samādhi*."

Feeling may arise, may exist, may pass through—so long as you do not grasp it as sagely realization. The positive practice of mindfulness of feeling is "feeling X, he knows feeling X"—when feeling X arises, he knows-as-it-is that feeling X is arising, without resistance, without pursuit, without misjudgment. The inverse of the ten māras of the feeling aggregate is "**feeling X, he grasps feeling X**"—when feeling X arises, the practitioner takes it as "my sagely realization," makes it "my possession," makes it "my identity."

From "he knows" to "he grasps"—one word apart, and a whole road apart.

### From Inward Implosion to Outward Pursuit

The feeling aggregate section ends with the "māra of desire" (the inward implosion of savoring the empty-luminous), and the next chapter, the perception aggregate section, opens with "craving for skill in expedient means" (the outward pursuit of an external capacity). **The same craving, but the form has shifted from "implosion into the savor of the meditative state" to "pursuit of capacities to be performed."**

This turn from inward to outward carries a clear structural meaning in Jiāoguāng's threefold division: the feeling aggregate section is "external māras creeping in while the māra has not yet manifested in person"—the māra is within the heart; the perception aggregate section is "the heavenly māra, finding its opportunity, sends forth a spirit to possess another"—the māra is out in the surrounding scene. From feeling to perception, the principal arena of the demonic event shifts from the practitioner's inner heart to the world outside him—concretely, to a bystander who looks like a "good and wise friend."

But this outward shift has an exceedingly subtle inner cause: **only when the feeling aggregate has been exhausted (when the practitioner has severed his attachment to the savor of his own meditative states) is the māra forced to enter from outside.** While the feeling aggregate remains unexhausted, the māra can darkly enter the practitioner's heart and cause him to overturn himself—because there is a gap within the heart for it to enter. Once the feeling aggregate is exhausted, the inner space is closed, and the māra can only manifest from outside, beguiling through the mouth, the form, and the "good and wise friend"-status of a bystander.

This is why the māra of the perception aggregate must "send forth a spirit to possess another"—not because the heavenly māra has grown stronger, but because the practitioner's inner gap has shrunk, and the māra must change its theater.

### From "Not Generating" to "Not Grasping"

"Not generating sagely conceit, this is called a wholesome state"—this is the verdict of the form aggregate section.
"This is not sagely realization; the moment one grasps it, demons are drawn in"—this is the verdict of the feeling aggregate section.

The two sentences share the same structure, but differ in depth. The form aggregate section says "do not generate sagely conceit," and that is enough; the feeling aggregate section requires "do not grasp"—not only must one not generate the mind of sagely realization, one must not even "grasp" at the meditative state. "Grasping" is more refined than "generating," because "generating" is conscious contrivance, while "grasping" can be unconscious clinging.

**From "not generating" to "not grasping"—this is the whole point of this chapter.**

In the next chapter, the māra will change its theater—from your own heart, it walks out onto a bystander who "looks like a good and wise friend."
