## 6.12 The Ten Māras of the Consciousness Aggregate—The Final Grasping Between the Two Pillars

### The Hour When the Day Is About to Dawn

The practitioner has passed through the aggregate of form, the aggregate of sensation, the aggregate of perception, the aggregate of formation. Arriving at this point in the tenth fascicle, the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* offers a passage of extraordinary solemnity:

> Ānanda! When this good son cultivating *samādhi* has exhausted the formation aggregate, the subtle disturbing motion of the worldly nature, the common ground of the life-mechanism, suddenly tears apart; the deep pulse of *pudgala*-karma-recompense, the threading and binding of the mind to the round of rebirth—its responsive linkage is severed. At the heaven of *nirvāṇa* he is about to realize great brightness, like the cock crowing for the second time; gazing eastward, he already sees essential color in the dawn sky. The six sense-faculties are empty and still, no longer galloping outward; inwardly and outwardly lucent, he enters where there is nothing to enter.

Every clause of this passage carries such weight that it must be read slowly.

**"Having exhausted the formation aggregate"**—the "fine waves" of the previous chapter have grown still. The subtle channel by which sentient beings repay their old karma (the "deep pulse of *pudgala*-karma-recompense") has had its responsive linkage severed. The mark of the formation aggregate's exhaustion is that the root of segmentary birth-and-death has been cut: there is no further taking of rebirth.

**"At the heaven of *nirvāṇa* he is about to realize great brightness"**—the heaven of *nirvāṇa*. The five aggregates are like night; *nirvāṇa* is like the sky. The first four aggregates are the night; once the consciousness aggregate is broken, that is the sky. At this point the practitioner stands at the very moment when the day is about to break.

**"Like the cock crowing for the second time"**—this is a traditional image borrowed by the commentator Jiāoguāng. The ancients divided the night into watches, and the rooster crows just before the day grows light. At the rooster's first crowing the sky is still wholly dark; at its second crowing the east already shows essential color. Jiāoguāng writes: "When the first three aggregates are exhausted, it is like the cock's first crowing: a glimmer of dawn, but two aggregates still cloud the sky, and essential color has not yet emerged. When the formation aggregate is exhausted, it is like the cock's second crowing: only one aggregate remains, and so he is *about* to realize great brightness."

The day is about to break—but it has not yet broken.

**"The six sense-faculties are empty and still, no longer galloping outward; inwardly and outwardly lucent, he enters where there is nothing to enter"**—the six faculties no longer run out toward objects. Within and without is a single clear brightness. Those last words—"enters where there is nothing to enter"—are exceedingly hard to grasp: he has entered some place, and yet that place is itself a place of no-entering.

This is an exceedingly beautiful position. Anyone who has read the *Śūraṅgama* falls silent at this passage—"like *vaiḍūrya*, inwardly and outwardly perfectly clear," "the six sense-faculties empty and still, entering where there is nothing to enter"—the words themselves have weight.

But just after this solemn description, the sūtra hands down a verdict that seems almost slight—**"yet the subtle marvel of cessation is not yet rounded out."**

**The subtle marvel is not yet rounded out.**

Subtle, marvelous—these two words are not wrong. The practitioner has indeed arrived at a refined and marvelous realm. But it is *not yet rounded out*. To round out is to complete, the roundness of perfect penetration. It is not yet round.

This is the position the present chapter must address—a position where almost everything important has already happened, but the final step has not yet been walked.

### Between the Two Pillars

When the Ming-dynasty commentator Jiāoguāng Zhēnjiàn reached the consciousness-aggregate section of the tenth fascicle, he faced a structurally thorny problem: each of the ten passages on the graspings of the consciousness aggregate opens with exactly the same formula.

> Ānanda, you should know that this good son, having exhausted the realm of formations as empty, having returned to consciousness at its source, with arising-and-passing already extinguished, yet with the subtle marvel of cessation not yet rounded out…

All ten passages share an identical opening; they diverge only in the second half, branching into ten different paths. This never happened in the first four sections of the fifty skandha-māra (form, sensation, perception, formation). Each of the form aggregate's ten states begins from nothing; each of the sensation aggregate's ten states unfolds independently; each of the perception aggregate's ten begins with "craving for X"; the formation aggregate's ten māras vary slightly in their "generating-reckoning" syntax. Only the consciousness aggregate's ten graspings share a wholly identical premise.

Why this structure?

Jiāoguāng gave it an exceedingly precise name:

> The formation aggregate is the front pillar; the consciousness aggregate is the rear pillar; and the locus where the ten graspings arise is precisely between these two—which is why all ten are placed under this same heading.

**Between the two pillars.**

The pillars are the columns that flank the front and back of an ancient hall. The ritual of entering a formal hall takes "between the two pillars" as its crucial position—neither outside the hall nor inside it, but on the very threshold.

By this name Jiāoguāng points out: **the position where the consciousness aggregate's ten graspings arise is not "inside the consciousness aggregate" but at the transitional position where "the formation aggregate is already broken and the consciousness aggregate is not yet rounded out."** The front pillar (formation aggregate) has already been crossed; the rear pillar (consciousness aggregate) has not yet been crossed—the practitioner stands on the threshold between the two pillars.

"Having exhausted the realm of formations as empty" marks that the front pillar is past; "the subtle marvel not yet rounded out" marks that the rear pillar is not yet reached. All ten graspings share this same premise, differing only in *what* they resolve as final upon this threshold. The ten are not "ten different errors occurring at different positions"; they are **ten branching paths that fork from one and the same threshold.**

The metaphysical situation of this position is exceedingly delicate. On the one hand, the practitioner has completed the entire cultivation of 6.08 through 6.11: he is no longer disturbed by outer demons (form aggregate), no longer misled by inner sensation (sensation aggregate), no longer deceived by a mirroring other (perception aggregate), no longer locked in by his own reflexive judgment (formation aggregate). On the other hand—he has not yet walked the final step. The consciousness aggregate has not yet been crossed; true cessation has not yet been fully rounded out.

**He stands on the threshold. Every difficulty that came before has been resolved, but the last step has not yet been walked.**

The commentator Zǐxuán names it in a single line:

> Although the formation aggregate is exhausted and arising-and-passing already extinguished, returning to consciousness and tracing its source, he has not yet returned to cessation; this is why it says the subtle marvel is not yet rounded out. **Because consciousness itself has not been broken; it is precisely a subtle arising-and-passing.**

"**Precisely a subtle arising-and-passing**"—these few words are the root of the entire consciousness-aggregate problem. The consciousness aggregate is itself "subtle arising-and-passing." It is not "free of arising-and-passing." The formation aggregate is the "coarse streaming"; by the time one reaches the consciousness aggregate it seems already to have stopped moving. But that "seeming not to move" is not yet true non-moving—it is still a more refined kind of motion. Any verdict that takes it for "free of arising-and-passing" is, at this threshold position, "fixing it as final"—and any such verdict manufactures one of the ten graspings.

### Apparently Unified, Apparently Permanent—What the Consciousness Aggregate Looks Like

So what does the consciousness aggregate actually look like? Jiāoguāng gives four words:

> "Essential brightness, lucent and unmoving"—this refers to the eighth consciousness. Pure unity unmixed, with horizontal and vertical penetrating illumination, called "essential brightness"—this is what is meant by *apparently unified*. Floating thoughts already exhausted, and the disturbances all stilled, called "lucent and unmoving"—this is what is meant by *apparently permanent*.

**Apparently unified**—pure and unmixed, illuminating in every direction. It looks like that non-dual true mind.
**Apparently permanent**—floating thoughts exhausted, disturbances all stilled. It looks like that unchanging substantive ground.

Both phrases turn on one fatal word—**apparently.** Not "is," but "appears to be."

Sit slowly with the fineness of this "apparently."

When Mahāyāna Buddhism describes suchness, buddha-nature, the true mind, the two phrases it reaches for most often are "one" (a single, unmixed unity) and "permanent" (an unchanging constancy)—the sūtras and treatises are full of them. And the consciousness aggregate, at this position "between the two pillars," **has fulfilled every surface mark of "one" and "permanent."** Observing his own mind within this realm, the practitioner sees: pure and unmixed (not tangled like the prior seven consciousnesses), lucent and unmoving (not disturbed like the prior four aggregates), illuminating in every direction (pervading the ten directions), floating thoughts all exhausted (no notion rising up). Every item matches the description of the true mind.

The difference lies in one place only—**neither arising nor passing.** The "one" and the "permanent" of the consciousness aggregate are both "apparent," not "true." And the difference between "true" and "apparent"—neither arising nor passing—**is invisible at the level of the senses.**

This is the deepest danger the present chapter must address. The consciousness-aggregate section does not require the practitioner to do anything aberrant in order to go wrong. It does not ask him to deny some obvious fact (as in the sensation-aggregate's misjudgments); it does not ask him to be deceived by some external teacher (as in the perception-aggregate's mirroring other); it does not ask him to make some thoroughgoing conceptual verdict (as in the formation-aggregate's sixty-two views). It asks only that he **take half a step further at a position of extreme resemblance**—and that half-step is "fixing it as final," is "this is the true mind," is taking the consciousness aggregate as ultimate.

### The Swift Stream—This Lucence Is Not True

Zǐxuán offers a precise image to lay the "apparent" wholly bare:

> This lucence is not true: like a swiftly flowing stream, viewed from afar appearing tranquil, the very speed of its flow concealing the flow itself—not at all the absence of flow.

**This lucence is not true.** Four words that are the core diagnostic of the consciousness-aggregate section. This "lucent stillness" is not a true stillness. Why? *Like a swiftly flowing stream.*

Picture a river flowing at tremendous speed. Seen from afar—**it appears tranquil.** No waves on the surface, no spray, no rise and fall. It looks exactly like a still pool. But walk closer and drop a leaf in, and the leaf is swept away in an instant—only then do you realize the water is in fact flowing, only flowing so fast that no surface texture has time to form. **The flow is too swift to see, but it is not the absence of flow.**

The consciousness aggregate is this river. "Lucent and unmoving" is true—seen at the level of the senses it really does not move. But it does not move not because it is not flowing, but because it flows so fast that it looks as though it were not flowing.

Zǐxuán adds one more line of great weight:

> The ordinary man and the Two-Vehicle adept do not perceive this at all; before the tenth bodhisattva-stage one perceives but cannot exhaust—and so it is said "the flow is too swift to see."

**The ordinary man and the Two-Vehicle adept do not perceive this at all.**

Sit with the weight of that line. It does not only say the ordinary man cannot see—the ordinary man cannot even see the coarse arising-and-passing that came before, which is no surprise. It says that **even the arhat of the Two Vehicles cannot see it.** One who has already realized the arhat fruit, who has already crossed the prior four aggregates, is also blind before the "swift flow" of the consciousness aggregate. This is not that the arhat has not practiced enough—he has completed the whole cultivation of personal liberation; it is that **the subtlety of the consciousness aggregate has already surpassed the discriminative power of the arhat fruit itself.**

To see the fine stream of the consciousness aggregate, what is needed is not deeper meditative absorption (the arhat's absorption is already very deep) but **the stages of the Mahāyāna bodhisattva**—the bodhisattva before the tenth stage begins to see it, and only after the tenth stage can cross it completely.

Jiāoguāng links this image to the whole system of water-images across the five aggregates:

> The perception aggregate is like great waves; the formation aggregate is like fine waves; **the consciousness aggregate is like a wave-less flowing water.** The body of true awareness should be like a still and unmoving water.

Five layers of wave. The form aggregate like spray at the shore, the sensation aggregate like a swelling crest, the perception aggregate like great waves, the formation aggregate like fine waves—all four have visible disturbance. And at the consciousness aggregate—**wave-less flowing water.** The surface shows no wave at all.

But the last line is the crux: **the body of true awareness should be like a still and unmoving water.**

The consciousness aggregate is "wave-less flowing water"; true awareness is "unmoving water." What is the surface difference between them?—there is none. Both are lucent, both unmoving, both without any visible wave. The difference lies in one word—**flowing.** Wave-less flowing water is still flowing; unmoving water truly does not flow. And this difference is indistinguishable at the level of the senses.

This is why the consciousness-aggregate section is the final station of the fifty skandha-māra. Not because its content is more complex (in content it is simpler than the formation aggregate's sixty-two views), but because **at this stage the tools of comparison fail completely.** From the mirroring other of 6.10, to the mirror-student of 6.11, to the two pillars of 6.12, the availability of comparison-tools steadily drops:

| Stage | Object mirrored | Comparison-tool |
|---|---|---|
| 6.10 perception | An external teacher | Sūtra teaching, precepts, the verdict of a genuine teacher—fully available |
| 6.11 formation | The practitioner's own act of observation | Awareness itself caught in reflexive tangle—largely fails |
| 6.12 consciousness | The subtle object of the consciousness aggregate | Comparison-tools fail completely—the consciousness aggregate looks exactly like true cessation |

This does not mean the consciousness-aggregate section cannot be crossed. It can. But the way of crossing can no longer rely on "comparing and judging"—because any comparison or judgment, at this position, becomes "fixing it as final," and thereby manufactures one of the ten graspings. The way of crossing must be of another kind—**continuing forward without resolving anything as final.**

### The First Eight Graspings—Taking a Subtle Object as Ultimate

The first eight of the consciousness aggregate's ten graspings share a common structure: **at the position between the two pillars, fixing some subtle object as final, as the true and permanent.** Each grasping points to some object that has been mistaken for "this is the true mind, this is the ultimate."

| # | Name of grasping | What is resolved as final | The kind one falls into |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cause/caused | The round origin one returns to, set up as a true permanent cause | Kapila's *prakṛti* (Sāṃkhya) |
| 2 | Capable/incapable | Reckoning all beings of the ten directions as flowing out of one's own body | *Maheśvara* (the great-self heaven) |
| 3 | Constancy/non-constancy | Reckoning the round origin as a true permanent body | *Īśvara* heaven |
| 4 | Knowing/non-knowing | Reckoning that knowing pervades all, that grass and trees too have feeling | *Vaṭa-Śaṇika* (grasping all as aware) |
| 5 | Generating/non-generating | Taking the gathered dust-elements as the root cause, worshipping fire and water | The Kāśyapa Brahmins |
| 6 | Returning/no-returning | Reckoning the emptiness within round brightness as the support of permanent extinction | The perception-less heaven |
| 7 | Attached-craving/non-craving | Reckoning the round-constant solidified body as permanently abiding | The Asita seekers of long life |
| 8 | Real/non-real | Seeing life as mutually penetrating, and so indulging craving without restraint | The *Ṭakkirāja* heavenly demon |

Each grasping has an Indian non-Buddhist counterpart. This looks as though the *Śūraṅgama* were drawing up "a blacklist of eight non-Buddhists"—but that is the surface reading. The previous chapter, addressing the formation aggregate's ten māras and their correspondence to the *Brahmajāla*'s sixty-two views, already corrected this structural reading with one verdict of Zǐxuán's:

> All of these are practitioners reaching this point, where wrong wisdom suddenly arises, called the unfolding of views—**not that there really exist non-Buddhists from outside.**

This same line applies in the consciousness-aggregate section. **The first eight graspings are not "eight historical non-Buddhists reappearing in meditation," but the structural exhaustion of one single act—"the final grasping at a subtle object"**—and the Indian non-Buddhists happen to be the historical embodiments of these structural positions.

In the consciousness-aggregate section this is even more refined than in the formation aggregate. The sixty-two views the formation aggregate corresponds to can be found in the general literature of Indian philosophy (any metaphysical system makes such verdicts). But the eight non-Buddhists the consciousness aggregate corresponds to—**the Sāṃkhya school (*prakṛti*), the Yoga school, *Maheśvara* worship, the graspers of all-as-aware, the fire-worshipping Brahmins, the perception-less heaven, the cultivators of longevity arts, the *Ṭakkirāja***—these are not just any non-Buddhists. **They are the deepest meditative traditions in the history of Indian religion.** The traditions capable of reaching the position "between the two pillars." Across two and a half millennia of Indian religious history, the traditions that could truly enter this depth can be counted on one hand.

Put another way—**the *Śūraṅgama*'s refutation of the first eight graspings is Mahāyāna's final verdict on the highest stratum of the Indian meditative tradition.** Mahāyāna does not deny that these traditions reached an exceedingly deep meditative absorption. To reach the position "between the two pillars" is already a tremendous attainment. Mahāyāna points out only one thing: **at this position, fixing something as final is wrong; the right act is not to fix anything as final, and to walk the last step.**

Depth of meditation is not the ultimate. "Not fixing anything as final" upon that depth is the ultimate.

### The Last Two Graspings—Where Buddhism's Own Vehicles Are Listed as Māras

Up to here the logic of the first eight graspings is clear—a non-Buddhist fixes something as final at the consciousness-aggregate position and is judged a māra. But at the ninth and tenth graspings, the structure suddenly turns:

> Ninth grasping: **the definite-nature *śrāvaka***—corresponding to "monks of no-learning afflicted by overweening pride."
> Tenth grasping: **the definite-nature *pratyekabuddha***—corresponding to "those of solitary lineage who do not turn back the mind."

*Śrāvaka* means the arhat. *Pratyekabuddha* means the solitary realizer. **Two of Buddhism's own proper vehicles are listed, in the consciousness-aggregate section, as "māras."**

This is a structural embarrassment. The arhat fruit is a holy fruit personally affirmed by the Buddha. The Āgamas are full of arhats—Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Mahā-Kāśyapa, Ānanda, Subhūti—and the Buddha never denied their realization. The *pratyekabuddha* fruit is likewise a real, stage-level holy fruit. **Why would Buddhism's own fruit-positions be judged as māras within Buddhism's own meditation map?**

To this structural question Jiāoguāng gives, in the tenth fascicle of his *Zhèngmài Shū*, the most precise single passage in the whole fifty-skandha-māra discussion:

> Question: *Śrāvaka* and *pratyekabuddha* are the proper vehicles of the inner teaching, named "world-transcending lesser sages"—why are they here listed in the count of māras?
>
> Answer: **"Māra" is the designation "that which destroys the right."** In the great *samādhi* of this sūtra, that which advances perfect penetration and approaches *nirvāṇa* is benefit; that which violates perfect penetration and turns from *nirvāṇa* is loss. **And the Two Vehicles, plainly, violate and turn away—branching off, blunting and stagnating, equally injurious to the right. How should they not be māras?** However, qualified by "definite-nature," the verdict **applies only to those who do not turn back the mind.** Those who can turn back the mind do not fall into this count.

This passage must be seen step by step. Jiāoguāng's argument has three steps.

**First, the word "māra" is given a fresh definition**—"the designation 'that which destroys the right.'" In the context of the *Śūraṅgama*, "māra" is not an external monster or spirit, not red fangs and claws, not a body wrapped in flame. Māra is **the general name for whatever obstructs right practice.** Anything that obstructs perfect penetration and *nirvāṇa* is a māra, no matter what it wears—even if what it wears is the words "holy fruit."

**Second, why the Two Vehicles enter the count of māras**—"branching off, blunting and stagnating, equally injurious to the right." In the context of the *Śūraṅgama*'s great *samādhi*, to go forward along perfect penetration toward *nirvāṇa* is benefit; to turn one's back on perfect penetration and stop is loss. The Two Vehicles' problem is not that "their teaching is wrong" (the teaching itself is spoken by the Buddha) but that "at a certain position they stop and go no further" (branching off and stagnating). The direction of the stopping faces away from perfect penetration (since the holy fruit is not the ultimate of perfect penetration), and so even though the position they stop at is a real holy position (a world-transcending lesser sage), it still constitutes an obstruction to the practitioner's entry into perfect penetration. To call whatever obstructs right practice a "māra"—this is the *Śūraṅgama*'s way of defining the term.

**Third, the restriction of scope**—"qualified by 'definite-nature,' it applies only to those who do not turn back the mind." Note those words "qualified by"—this is a restrictive sieve. Not all *śrāvakas* and *pratyekabuddhas* are listed as māras; **only the portion of "definite-nature" (those who do not turn the mind toward the Great) are so listed.** Those who can turn the mind toward Mahāyāna—even if they once rested at the arhat fruit—are not judged māras by the *Śūraṅgama*.

Zǐxuán's commentary widens this restriction one layer further:

> "Definite-nature" simply means that during one period of seeking quiescence the disposition does not change—provisionally judged as "definite." But there are real *kalpas* still ahead, and they will eventually turn back to the higher vehicle.

"Definite-nature" is not an eternal verdict on someone's nature—it does not say this person will *never* turn the mind. It is only a description of "one period of seeking quiescence, the disposition unchanged"—in this present span of life he temporarily does not turn the mind. But over a long enough time (the span of *kalpas*), he will in the end turn the mind toward the Great.

These three steps together yield Mahāyāna's deepest verdict on the Two Vehicles—**extraordinarily generous and extraordinarily severe at once.** The generous side: the *Śūraṅgama* does not deny the real attainment of the arhat and the *pratyekabuddha*, does not deny that they have realized real stage-fruits; it does not even deny that the "definite-nature" ones will eventually turn the mind. The severe side: within the great context of perfect penetration and *nirvāṇa*, **any posture of "no further advance" is a māra—even when it stands upon a real attainment.**

Jiāoguāng adds an exceedingly precise verdict on the position of the arhat fruit on the Mahāyāna map:

> "Bound by emptiness," it is called the bound-emptiness seed. For the *nirvāṇa*-with-remainder is positioned only at the seventh stage of faith—covered by the consciousness aggregate, not yet reaching even the cause-ground of perfect penetration. How could it reach the fruit-ground of *nirvāṇa*-without-remainder?

"**Positioned only at the seventh stage of faith**"—the arhat's realization, among the fifty-two positions of the Mahāyāna bodhisattva path, corresponds only to the "seventh stage of faith" (the seventh of the ten stages of faith). And to enter perfect penetration, the minimum threshold is the "first abode" (the first of the ten abodes). From the seventh stage of faith to the first abode there still lie the eighth, ninth, and tenth stages of faith brought to fullness, and then the entry into the first abode—the arhat's realization, on the Mahāyāna map of stages, **has not even reached "the cause-ground of perfect penetration,"** let alone its fruit-ground.

This is the position of the last two graspings. Not "no attainment"—the attainment is real; but "a real attainment mistaken for the endpoint"—when in truth this position is still very far from the true endpoint.

### Why the Last Two Are Deeper Than the First Eight

The first eight graspings and the last two both look like "fixing something as final between the two pillars," but their structures differ at the root, and this difference is the deepest insight of the whole consciousness-aggregate section.

**The object of the first eight graspings is "false."** The subtle object of the consciousness aggregate is mistaken for "true permanent cause," "true permanent body," "true permanent knowing." The consciousness aggregate's "essential brightness, lucent and unmoving," its "awareness pervading all classes," its "entering the round origin"—these realm-marks do indeed appear in the practitioner's meditation, but they are only the imaging of the consciousness aggregate. Zǐxuán names it in one line: "This entire experiential field is the shadow of consciousness, not yet cessation." The direction of the antidote for the first eight is **"breaking the grasping"**—recognizing the falsity of the object and no longer taking the shadow of consciousness as the ultimate.

**The object of the last two graspings is "real."** The definite-nature *śrāvaka* really has attained "seeing suffering, severing the cause, realizing cessation, cultivating the path"—this is the real content of the arhat fruit, not an empty claim. The definite-nature *pratyekabuddha* really has attained "round and clear bright awareness"—this is the real content of the *pratyekabuddha* fruit. These are not shadows of consciousness, not "resembling but not real," but holy fruits affirmed by the Buddha's own mouth.

Here is a conclusion that makes one uncomfortable—**a real stage-attainment, if taken as the ultimate, is more dangerous than a false claim.**

A false claim is easily countered. The practitioner need only honestly examine his own mind, and he will discover "I have not really reached that position"—and then the false holy-attainment falls away by itself. Falsehood has an inner weakness: it cannot survive examination.

But a real stage-attainment cannot be countered the same way. The practitioner honestly examines his own mind—and finds that he really has reached the true knowledge of "suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path"; that he really has reached "round and clear bright awareness." These are real. The more honestly he examines, the more he confirms that the attainment is real.

**The antidote cannot be "denying this attainment"—because the attainment is real.**

The antidote can only be—**not taking this attainment as the endpoint.**

This is the deepest structural reason the *Śūraṅgama* judges the last two graspings as māras. It is not that the attainment is false, but that **the act of taking a real stage for the endpoint is wrong.** This is also why the direction of antidote for the first eight and for the last two is fundamentally different:

| Dimension | First eight graspings | Last two graspings |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Subtle object of the consciousness aggregate | Real stage-attainment |
| Nature of the object | False (shadow of consciousness) | Real (holy fruit) |
| The mistaken act | Taking a false object as true and permanent | Taking a real stage as the endpoint |
| Direction of antidote | Break the grasping (recognize the falsity) | Turn the mind (recognize the non-ultimacy) |
| Principle of antidote | Do not fix anything as final | Turn the mind toward the Great |

From this table one can see—the antidote of the consciousness-aggregate section is not one road but two. The antidote for the first eight is "breaking" (breaking the falsity of the object); the antidote for the last two is "turning" (turning the mind toward a more ultimate direction). Only these two antidotal principles together make up the complete antidote of the consciousness-aggregate section.

### Not Fixing Anything as Final + Turning the Mind Toward the Great

**Antidote for the first eight graspings: do not fix anything as final.**

Across the whole of the fifty skandha-māra, one sentence is repeated again and again—"If one regards it as a sagely realization, one falls prey to the host of demons; if one does not take it as a sagely realization, it is called a wholesome state." From the form-aggregate section to the consciousness-aggregate section, this single criterion runs through all of it. But its meaning unfolds completely only in the consciousness-aggregate section.

In the form-aggregate section, "not taking it as a sagely realization" meant "do not take light-appearances as realization."
In the sensation-aggregate section, it meant "do not take changes in feeling as realization."
In the perception-aggregate section, it meant "do not be deceived by a mirroring other into thinking yourself a sage."
In the formation-aggregate section, it meant "do not make a thoroughgoing pronouncement on the intrinsic nature of the realm you perceive."
And in the consciousness-aggregate section—**do not fix the subtle object of the consciousness aggregate as final, as the true and permanent, even when the content of the verdict looks entirely correct.**

This final "not taking it as a sagely realization" does not mean "not knowing the content of the realm." The practitioner knows his realm clearly—"essential brightness, lucent and unmoving" he can feel; "awareness pervading all classes" he can feel; "entering the round origin" he can feel. But he does not fix these realms as "the true permanent cause / true permanent body / true permanent knowing." **He knows the content of the realm, but does not fix it as the ultimate.**

Jiāoguāng's swift-stream image supplies a precise operating principle—**any realm of "essential brightness, lucent and unmoving" should be suspected of being "flowing too swiftly to be seen."** The closer a realm-mark comes to the description of true cessation, the *higher* one's alertness should be, not the lower. This runs against ordinary intuition—ordinary intuition says "the more it looks real, the more I should trust it"—but in the consciousness-aggregate section, **the more it looks real, the more wary one should be.** Because the entire danger of the consciousness aggregate is built upon the word "apparently."

**Antidote for the last two graspings: turn the mind toward the Great.**

This one is the reverse of "breaking the grasping"—there is no "grasping" to break. The real arhat fruit cannot be broken; the real *pratyekabuddha* fruit cannot be broken. The antidote can only be to make the stage-attainment already reached into a "passage" rather than an "endpoint."

Turning the mind toward the Great has three steps:

**First, acknowledge the reality of the stage-attainment.** The first step of the antidote for the last two is *not* to deny the attainment. Denial only breeds greater confusion—the practitioner really has reached a real realization, and if he is told "you have reached nothing at all," he will reasonably doubt whether the whole system can be trusted. Acknowledging "you have reached a real stage" is the starting point of the antidote, not its endpoint.

**Second, acknowledge that the stage-attainment is not the ultimate.** This step is to accept the Mahāyāna map of stages—the arhat fruit, among the fifty-two positions of Mahāyāna, is the seventh stage of faith, still a considerable distance from the ultimate. This acknowledgment is not a negation of the Lesser Vehicle, **but an acceptance of the Mahāyāna map.** The Lesser Vehicle has its own endpoint (the arhat fruit *is* the Lesser Vehicle's endpoint), but within the Mahāyāna map the Lesser Vehicle's endpoint is a way-station of the Great.

**Third, arouse the aspiration of turning the mind toward the Great.** Transform the attainment of personal liberation into the provision for benefiting beings. Zǐxuán's verdict—"there are real *kalpas* still ahead, and they will eventually turn back to the higher vehicle"—states clearly that **this is not a question of whether it will happen, but of when.** Even the "definite-nature *śrāvaka*," over a long enough time, turns the mind in the end. The question of cultivation has never been "whether one will turn the mind" but "whether one turns now or only after *kalpas*."

From 6.09's "non-clinging" to the second arrow, to 6.10's "seek the great teacher" before the mirroring other, to 6.11's "do not take it as a sagely realization" before the mirror-student, to 6.12's "do not fix anything as final + turn the mind toward the Great" between the two pillars—the antidotal principle of the fifty skandha-māra has walked its full lineage of refinement. Each stage is more interior, more subtle, closer to the ultimate principle of "not grasping at any position" than the last. By the consciousness-aggregate section, the antidotal principle reaches parity with the very subtlety of the consciousness aggregate—**"not fixing anything as final" is precisely letting the consciousness aggregate spontaneously disclose its "subtle arising-and-passing" nature, performing no act of freezing upon it.**

The moment the threshold of the ten graspings is crossed, the *Śūraṅgama* offers a description of great solemnity:

> If, within the assembled bonds already attained as one, the six gates are smelted clear and their union and separation perfected, hearing, seeing, and the rest of the senses pierce through to one another; the ten directions of the world together with body and mind are **like *vaiḍūrya*, inwardly and outwardly perfectly clear**—this is named the exhaustion of the consciousness aggregate.

From the realm where "essential color has not yet sunk," to the exhaustion-mark "like *vaiḍūrya*, inwardly and outwardly perfectly clear." The threshold is crossed. The passage between the two pillars is walked. The east of the rooster's second crowing has at last grown fully bright.

### From the Last Demon-Revealing Mirror to the Exhaustion of the Five Aggregates

The moment the consciousness aggregate is exhausted, the whole map of the fifty skandha-māra is walked to its end.

Looking back at the intrinsic nature of each of the five aggregates—the *Śūraṅgama* gives five precise names:

- The root of the form aggregate: **solid deluded thinking**—the congealing of the sense of materiality
- The root of the sensation aggregate: **empty-bright deluded thinking**—the receiving structure of feeling
- The root of the perception aggregate: **fusing-and-penetrating deluded thinking**—the imagination that can transform reality
- The root of the formation aggregate: **hidden deluded thinking**—the subtle, unbroken streaming
- The root of the consciousness aggregate: **the inverted deluded thinking of phantom forms and emptiness**—seeming real yet empty, seeming present yet absent, seeming true yet inverted

From solid to empty-bright, from empty-bright to fusing-and-penetrating, from fusing-and-penetrating to hidden, from hidden to phantom-form—**this is a single road of refinement in deluded thinking.** Each layer is more interior, more subtle, harder to see through than the last. The form aggregate is "the visible sense of materiality"; the consciousness aggregate is "the invisible image that is almost the true mind itself."

The dismantling of the five aggregates, layer by layer, is the road of cessation. How these five kinds of deluded thinking, added together, form a single net—and how that net gathers and resolves as a whole—is the subject of the next chapter, which is also the closing chapter of this part.

This chapter ends here. The threshold is crossed. The last demon-revealing mirror has been held up. What remains is to see the road we have walked *as* a road—to see its shape, to see how it gathers and resolves, to see where it leads.
