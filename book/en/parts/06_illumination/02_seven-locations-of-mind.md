## 6.2 The Seven Locations of Mind — The Mind You Think You Have Is Not the Mind

### Hunting for Your Glasses

Have you ever hunted for your glasses?

You rummage across the desk, the bookshelf, down the gap in the sofa, then double back to the bedroom—growing more frantic by the minute—until someone beside you says, "They're on your head."

You put them there the moment you put them on, but every second you spend looking at the world through them, they sit outside your field of view. You see with them, and so you cannot see them. You look for them with them, and so you can never find them.

This is not a joke. It is what the venerable Ānanda was doing twenty-five centuries ago in the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*—except that what he was hunting for was not his glasses, but his own mind.

### The King and the Bandits

Before the Buddha asked Ānanda "where is your mind," he set up a sharp comparison:

> It is like a king besieged by bandits, who dispatches troops to put them down—the troops must first know where the bandits are.

A king sends out his soldiers to suppress the bandits, but the soldiers have to know where the bandits are; without a location, no number of troops can strike. The Buddha went on: the reason you drift through birth and death is that mind and eyes are the culprits. Now I ask you—where exactly are your mind and your eyes?

The comparison states the urgency of the question with perfect clarity. Practice is not a matter of slogans. You say you want to cut off afflictions—where are the afflictions? You say you want to know the mind and see its nature—where is that "mind"? If you cannot even point to a location, the troops you send out only spin in place.

Ānanda answered in earnest, seven times. Seven times the Buddha gave back the same single line:

> There is no such place.

The place you named—mind is not there.

### Seven Wrong Answers

**The first location—mind is inside the body.** Ānanda's first response was the most instinctive: every being in the world assumes mind is inside the body. The Buddha did not argue common sense with him; he simply ran a piece of plain logic. If mind really were inside the body, it ought to see the heart, liver, spleen, and stomach first, and only then the things outside. Yet you have never once seen your own internal organs. If it cannot know what is inside, how could it know what is outside?

**The second location—mind is outside the body.** Ānanda flipped at once: since it is not inside, it must be outside. The Buddha broke this with a comparison from daily life—when one person eats a meal, it does not leave another person full. If body and mind really were two unrelated things, then whatever your eyes saw, your mind ought not to be able to make it out at the very same moment. But in fact, the instant your eyes see my hand, your mind knows it is a hand. Inside and outside do not come apart like that; the answer does not hold.

**The third location—mind hides inside the eye-faculty.** This time Ānanda got creative and built his own comparison: mind hides behind the eye, looking out as if through a layer of clear crystal. The Buddha turned the comparison back on him: you say it is like crystal covering the eye—then why, when you look at mountains and rivers, do you not see the crystal? If you can see it, the crystal becomes part of the outside world—and then it is no longer the container of mind.

**The fourth location—closing the eyes and seeing darkness is seeing inward.** Ānanda changed tactics, shifting from space to function: the inside of the body is dark, so to close the eyes and see darkness is to see one's own interior. The Buddha pressed all the way down: for someone sitting in a dark room, is the whole room's darkness his five organs? If closing the eyes can see inward, then why, when you open them, can you not see your own face? This road does not go through either.

**The fifth location—mind comes to be wherever it makes contact.** Ānanda gave up fixed location altogether, citing the Buddha's own words—"because mind arises, all the various dharmas arise"—for support: mind has no settled home; wherever it meets something, there it is. The Buddha questioned him on two fronts. If mind has no substance of its own, where does the act of "joining" even happen? A verb with no subject does not stand. And if it does have substance, is it one or many? Does it fill the whole body, or only part? If it fills the whole body, then when you touch your head, your foot ought to feel it too.

**The sixth location—mind is in the middle, between faculty and object.** The eye-faculty meets the form-object, and consciousness arises in between—here Ānanda came closest to the language of Yogācāra. But the Buddha pointed out: this "middle," if it carries the properties of both faculty and object, mixes matter and mind together—how is that to be counted? And if it carries neither property, then it is neither matter nor mind, and even the mark of a "middle" cannot stand up.

**The seventh location—not clinging to anything at all is mind.** Ānanda fell back to his last line of defense: inside, outside, and the middle are all wrong, so I will grasp at nothing—and this very "not grasping" is mind itself. The Buddha sealed off even "nothing": this "not clinging" you speak of—is there something that does the not-clinging, or is there not? If there is not, then it is like a horn on a rabbit's head, a thing that does not exist at all—so what is there to "not cling" about? And if there is, then there is something with a mark; what has a mark has a presence; what has a presence has a location—and you have fallen straight back onto the old road.

Seven locations, seven times "there is no such place." Ānanda pointed to every place he could think of, and every one of them was broken.

### The Mistake Almost Everyone Who Practices Makes

The seventh location is worth pausing on by itself—because the trap here is one the Song-dynasty commentator Zixuan named in the gravest terms:

> Practitioners throughout the world mostly make this very mistake.

The error people who practice fall into most often is to take "clinging to nothing at all" as the ultimate. Sitting in the meditation hall, letting every thought that comes go ungrasped, they assume this is seeing the Way. But "not grasping" is itself an act; "non-clinging" is itself a posture—there is still a faint standpoint of "I am the one who is not clinging" hidden behind it.

This standpoint runs the deepest precisely because it looks the most like the ultimate. The errors in the first six locations are easy enough to spot—after all, you have put mind in some place, and the Buddha only has to point out that the place is faulty. The seventh is different: it has done away with "place" altogether, and so it appears to have already risen above all clinging.

But there is one last step missing: that "not clinging"—who is doing the not-clinging? There is still a subject standing there, sustaining the state of "not clinging." So long as there is still a subject, it still has a location.

A misreading easily slips in here. Reaching this point, some will think: "Since all seven locations are broken, does that mean mind simply does not exist? Is having nothing at all the most ultimate thing of all?" This drops into another pit—the view of annihilation. What the Buddha broke is the false mind's **foothold**, not the mind's **existence**. As the next chapter will show, once the seven locations are broken through, the Buddha does not say "and so there is no mind." He says something else entirely.

### "Location" Was Only Ever a Borrowed Idea

With the seven locations behind us, we can step back and look at the structure all seven errors share underneath.

Every one of them assumed the same thing: **that mind has a location.**

Inside the body, outside the body, behind the faculty, in the darkness, the point of contact, the middle between faculty and object—even the seventh location's "non-clinging"—each is some version of "location." Non-clinging is a "location with no location," still occupying a spot in the space of concepts.

But what is "location" itself?

The precision with which Yogācāra handles this question is astonishing. Among the dharmas dissociated from mind in the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* (treated in detail back in 3.12), there is one item called "direction"—orientation, the points of the compass. The treatises say it plainly: direction is a borrowed designation, set up in dependence on the cause-and-effect distinctions among material things. East, south, west, and north have meaning only within the material world. Mind is not a material thing; it simply has no capacity to "occupy a direction" at all.

Which is to say—the very question "where is mind" was the wrong question from the start.

This is the structural paradox of the seven locations: to use mind to look for the location of mind is to use a concept mind manufactures—direction—to catch the manufacturer of concepts. You cannot find it, not because mind does not exist, but because the tool in your hand and the thing you are looking for are one and the same.

Back to the glasses we began with—you walk around hunting for your glasses with your glasses on, and it is exactly because you are looking *through* them that you cannot see them.

### Where Descartes Stopped

Readers at home in Western philosophy may, at this point, think of Descartes—he too used a method of elimination. He doubted the senses, doubted the body, doubted the entire external world, until one thing was left that could not be doubted: the fact of "I am thinking" itself. "I think, therefore I am"—and so the starting point of Western philosophy was set.

The Buddha's elimination and Descartes' have a structural resemblance—both close in on the core by stripping away layer after layer. But there is one decisive difference:

⚠️ Descartes stopped at "I think." The Buddha did not stop.

Had Ānanda, at the seventh location, changed his answer to "I am thinking, and that which thinks is mind," the Buddha's refutation would be exactly the same as his refutation of "non-clinging": this thinking you speak of—does it have substance or not? If it has substance, it has a mark and a presence, and that is location again; if it has no substance, it is a rabbit's horn—so what thinking is there to speak of? Descartes found one unmoving fulcrum, stopped there, and on it rebuilt his whole edifice of knowledge. The Buddha pointed out that every fulcrum is something mind sets up, and went on inviting you to look directly at the one who does the setting-up.

This comparison is only suggestive; it is not a claim of theoretical equivalence. The two men cared about fundamentally different questions—Descartes wanted a certain foundation for knowledge, the Buddha wanted liberation. But the comparison helps make the precision of the seven locations clear: this is not some primitive religious back-and-forth. Its logical rigor stands level with Descartes' methodical doubt—it simply pushes further.

### Mistaking the Thief for Your Son

After the seven locations were broken, the Buddha did not say "and so mind does not exist." He revealed something fundamental—

Every person who practices carries two roots:

> The first is the beginningless root of birth and death—namely the grasping mind that you and all beings now take to be your own nature.
> The second is the beginningless essence of bodhi and nirvāṇa, originally pure—namely the bright essence of consciousness in you now, which can give rise to all conditions yet is overlooked by those very conditions.

The first—the grasping mind. This is the mind Ānanda pointed to seven times. Its nature is to cling and attach: it grabs whatever it meets, inside, outside, at the faculty, at the object, and every time it is like a guest in an inn, lodging for a night and then moving on, never the host.

The second—the bright essence of consciousness. This is the true mind, the source of bodhi and nirvāṇa. The sūtra describes its nature with great precision: it "can give rise to all conditions yet is overlooked by those very conditions"—it lets every cause and condition arise, but the moment you chase after those causes and conditions, you forget it. It has been there all along, and you cannot see it, because your eyes are forever chasing outward.

The Buddha's final verdict on the whole process of the seven locations was four characters:

> Mistaking the thief for your son.

Taking the burglar who broke into your home for your own child. Every "mind" Ānanda pointed to across the seven locations was that thief—it wears the clothing of mind, but its nature is restless, drifting, grasping. To take it for your own true mind is the very starting point of all cyclic existence.

One thing to watch here: the four-aspect analysis is a tool you can use to examine the seven locations (Ānanda kept circling at the level of mind's projections and functions, never touching mind's self-knowing essence), but do not read the four aspects as "a map of the mind." If, having finished the seven locations, your thought is "then I should go and find that self-witnessing aspect"—you have gone wrong again; you are still looking for a location. The *Cheng weishi lun* says it clearly: the four aspects can be gathered into one. They are not four rooms inside the mind; they are four analytical facets of one and the same mind.

### The One Thing That Was Never Broken

The seven locations were all broken. Every "location" was torn down.

But one thing was never broken, start to finish—the thing that, after being knocked back by the Buddha each time in the seven locations, could stand up again and point one more time. It was there the whole time. Every time Ānanda failed, it was there. Every time the Buddha refuted him, it was there. Yet Ānanda never once saw it.

Having finished this chapter, you may think: "Good, I understand now—mind is not in any of the seven locations."

But the next time someone makes you angry, your first reaction will still be—"I feel awful inside."

That "inside"—is the first location.

Knowing and seeing are two different things. This is exactly the measuring stick set up by the kōan mirror in Part Five (5.2): you can recount the logic of the seven locations, you can explain the reason behind each of the Buddha's refutations—this is "knowing." But in every reflexive flash of feeling in your daily life, you are still placing mind at some position in the body—and that shows the "realization" has not yet happened.

The seven locations is not a thing you read once and are done with. It is a mirror. Every time you say "I feel, in my heart…" is one more chance to look into that mirror. That "in my heart"—where is it? Inside the body? In the brain? In the middle of some sensation?

You do not need to get the answer right. What you need to do is notice that you have, without realizing it, placed your mind somewhere—and then see the act of "placing" itself.

The seven locations is the breaking. The next chapter is the revealing—the breaking of the seven locations is what makes the revealing of the ten demonstrations possible. If the false answers are not all cleared away first, then when you meet "the nature of seeing" in the next chapter you will instantly file it into some location—and you are back on the old road of the first location all over again.

In the next chapter, we watch the Buddha reveal it head-on—what that thing which was never broken, start to finish, actually looks like.
