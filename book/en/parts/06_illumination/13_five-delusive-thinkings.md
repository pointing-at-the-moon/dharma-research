## 6.13 The Five Delusive Thinkings and the Road to Cessation

### Ānanda Asks Again

The fifty skandha-māras have been taught in full.

From the latter half of Fascicle Nine through the bulk of Fascicle Ten, the Buddha unfolds, one by one, the fifty typical traps of the form aggregate, the feeling aggregate, the perception aggregate, the formations aggregate, and the consciousness aggregate—ten per aggregate, fifty in all. By any ordinary logic, the teaching ought to end here.

But the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* does not end.

After the fifty māras have all been expounded, Ānanda "rose from his seat, and having heard the Buddha's instruction, he made obeisance and respectfully received it, retaining it without loss," and then addressed the Buddha once more. Rising, he asked three questions:

> Just as the Tathāgata has said, "within the marks of the five aggregates, the root mind of thinking is fivefold falsity"—we ordinarily have not received the Tathāgata's subtle exposition of this. Moreover, are these five aggregates extinguished simultaneously, or exhausted in sequence? And of these five gradations, what constitutes the boundary of each?

First, what is the root cause of the five aggregates? Second, are the five aggregates extinguished all at once, or exhausted one after another? Third, where do the boundaries between one aggregate and the next lie?

The structure of these three questions deserves a careful look. Ānanda is not asking for new content—he has just heard the phenomena of the five aggregates in full. He is asking about **structure**. Why are the fifty phenomena divided into five sections? Why does each section contain ten? How are the aggregates demarcated from one another? Are these fifty phenomena fifty independent warnings, or a single map with a structure?

Ānanda himself adds a note explaining why he asks: "May the Tathāgata, out of great compassion, illuminate the minds and eyes of this assembly, and **serve as a future eye for all sentient beings of the latter age!**"

"A future eye"—this is Ānanda's own designation of who this teaching is for. He knows he is not the only questioner. He is asking on behalf of everyone who will, in time to come, read this passage.

The Buddha's way of answering must also be read slowly. He does not take the path of "explaining the remedial details of fifty phenomena one by one." He gives three layers of answer, each of an entirely different nature: **the root cause of the five aggregates is the same—delusive thinking** (the level of principle), **the five delusive thinkings are each distinct** (the level of structure), and **sudden in principle, gradual in phenomena, with their boundaries** (the level of cultivation and realization). These three layers are not supplementary footnotes to the fifty phenomena. They **gather** all fifty phenomena up into a deeper level.

This chapter is about that gathering.

### All the Same Delusive Thinking

The Buddha's first reply to Ānanda runs like this:

> The pure, true, wondrously bright, originally enlightened nature is perfectly pure, retaining neither birth-and-death nor any defilement. **Even space itself arises from delusive thinking.** This original enlightenment, wondrous, bright, true, and pure, deludedly gives rise to the receptacle worlds.

The very first sentence pulls the category of "delusive thinking" to an unprecedented breadth. It is not only that the five aggregates are delusive thinking. **Space itself arises from delusive thinking.**

Then the Buddha continues:

> Delusion is fundamentally without cause; within delusive thinking, causal nature is posited. Those who lose track of causality call it spontaneity. The nature of empty space is itself an illusory production; causality and spontaneity are both calculations of the deluded mind of sentient beings.

**Delusion is fundamentally without cause**—this is the most piercing verdict in the whole passage.

Delusive thinking has no first cause. Why? Because the very act of "asking for a first cause" has already fallen inside delusive thinking. If delusion had a first cause, that cause would be either real (in which case it would not be delusion) or a still-earlier delusion (which would then need a cause earlier still). The first alternative is a contradiction; the second is an infinite regress. So delusion **must** be causeless. "Positing causal nature," "calling it spontaneity"—these are just smaller stages erected inside delusive thinking, after which one takes the rules of the small stage for the rules of the world.

Then comes the axis sentence of the whole passage:

> Therefore the Tathāgata shows you that **the root causes of the five aggregates are all delusive thinking.**

**All the same delusive thinking**—this single sentence gathers the entire catalogue of fifty skandha-māras, from the latter half of Fascicle Nine through the front of Fascicle Ten, under one principle.

Feel the weight of this gathering. The reader who has walked through the last four chapters (6.9 to 6.12) has seen the list of traps: the ten feeling-aggregate māras (sorrow, mania, recollection, false sufficiency, anxiety, joy, conceit, frivolity, emptiness, craving) + the ten perception-aggregate māras (the ten cravings → the ten mirror-others) + the ten formations-aggregate māras (the sixty-two views → the ten deranged interpretations of *dhyāna*) + the ten consciousness-aggregate graspings (the first eight establishing supreme understanding of subtle objects, the last two capping their attainment as the summit). Add the ten form-aggregate states of 6.8, read but not separately tabulated in the book—fifty phenomena, each with its own specific form, its own specific remedy, its own specific verdict. Not one of these fifty can be taken lightly in actual practice.

And yet the Buddha, in a single sentence—**all the same delusive thinking**—pulls them all to the same level.

This does not abolish the specificity of the fifty phenomena. It tells the reader: these fifty are not fifty isolated warnings. They are fifty cross-sections of one structure.

The gathering can be seen in three layers.

The first layer: **from fifty to five.** The fifty phenomena belong to five sections—form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness. The ten phenomena within each section share the nature of that one aggregate. Fifty is not fifty; it is 5 × 10—the "5" is the structure, the "10" is the typical way that structure unfolds.

The second layer: **from five to one.** What do these five sections share? The same delusive thinking. The form aggregate is delusive thinking; the feeling aggregate is delusive thinking; perception, formations, and consciousness are all delusive thinking. The five sections have different degrees of subtlety and different typical traps, but their nature is one.

The third layer: **this principle is itself without cause.** Where does delusive thinking come from? Delusion is fundamentally without cause. The asking has no end, because the asking is itself inside delusive thinking.

These three layers of gathering carry several direct implications for the reader. For reading: the fifty māras need not be memorized item by item—grasp the fivefold structure and the one principle, and every phenomenon can be understood under that principle. For practice: the fifty māras need not be met with fifty separate methods—each of the five structures has one typical remedial principle, and these five in turn gather into the single overarching principle, "do not form a sagely realization." For doctrinal classification: the *Śūraṅgama*'s classification of meditative practice does not enumerate errors, it reveals the principle—any meditative phenomenon not listed in the catalogue of fifty, so long as its structure falls within one of the five aggregate-sections, can be handled by the corresponding remedial principle.

The catalogue is finite, but the principle is exhaustive.

### The Five Delusive Thinkings—One Delusion, Refined Through Five Layers

If the root cause of the five aggregates is the same delusive thinking, then where does the difference between the aggregates lie?

The Buddha's answer is: the difference lies in **subtlety**. Jiāoguāng describes this difference in a single line:

> In sum, when one traces it to the root, there is only one delusive thinking; but the coarse and manifest culminates in the form aggregate, while the fine and subtle culminates in the consciousness aggregate.

The five aggregates are one delusive thinking refined across five levels. The form aggregate is the coarsest form of this delusion; the consciousness aggregate is the finest. From coarse to fine is a continuous spectrum—not five isolated categories.

Let us look at each aggregate in turn.

**Form aggregate = solid delusive thinking.** The Buddha says to Ānanda: "Your body originally arose from the thinking of your parents; if your mind were not itself thinking, it could not come into the thinking that transmits life. As I said before: 'Thinking of a sour taste, saliva arises in the mouth; thinking of climbing a high place, the soles of the feet ache.' The cliff is not really there, the sour fruit has not yet arrived—if your body were not of the same kind as falsity, how could the saliva flow from merely speaking of sourness?"

The defiled thinking of one's parents condenses into a bodily embryo; thinking of sour plums makes saliva flow in the mouth; thinking of a cliff makes the knees go weak—these phenomena share a common structure: **the unreal can produce the real.** The thinking that is the cause is unreal; the bodily reaction that is the effect is real. The physical body is a solid structure "condensed out of thinking." It is called **solid** because the effect-end is real, condensed, set fast and not easily undone.

**Feeling aggregate = illusively bright delusive thinking.** The Buddha says: "Just as I said, the thinking of climbing a high place can really cause your body to feel a stinging ache. From cause arises feeling, which can move the physical body. Your present condition of being driven by the dual phenomena of benefit-conformity and harm-violation is named the second delusive thinking, called illusively bright."

The feeling aggregate has no substance of its own. It is only the continuous driving of two phenomena—the favorable (benefit) and the adverse (harm). The two words "illusively bright": *illusive*, because it has no substance; *bright*, because it can receive. Half unreal, half real.

**Perception aggregate = fluid delusive thinking.** The Buddha says: "Your reflective thoughts drive your physical body; the body is not of the same kind as thinking—why then does your body, driven by thinking, take various forms? When mind arises, form is grasped; the two correspond. While awake it is the thinking mind; while asleep it becomes various dreams. Thus your thinking and reflection, agitating delusive feeling, are named the third delusive thinking, called fluid."

What is special about the perception aggregate is that it can **fuse the two ends, unreal and real.** Reflective thought (unreal) can drive the physical body (real)—and this relation of "unreal-driving-real" needs a mediating structure to carry it, which is the perception aggregate. Perception lies neither at the unreal end nor at the real end, but in the position of "the fluid fusion between unreal and real."

**Formations aggregate = hidden delusive thinking.** The Buddha says: "The principle of transformation does not stay still; it shifts secretly moment by moment. Nails grow, hair sprouts, vital energy decays, the face wrinkles—day and night they alternate without your awareness. Ānanda! If this were not you, how could your body change? If it were truly you, how is it that you do not perceive it?"

This is a double interrogation. If the formations aggregate is not you, why does your body undergo change? If the formations aggregate is you, why are you unaware of that change? Neither side stands—enough to show that the formations aggregate is delusive. But it is subtler than the first three aggregates: **it has no "real" end-point at all.** Any end-point, the moment it is pointed at, has already flowed away. The word "hidden" describes this unlocatability of pure flow.

**Consciousness aggregate = vacuous, inverted, fine-and-subtle delusive thinking.** The Buddha says: "Furthermore, the place where your refined brightness, settled and unmoving, is called permanent … why is it that, having seen some marvelous object years ago, after years and years with neither memory nor forgetting, when you suddenly see that very thing again, your memory of it is intact and not lost? Within this refined and unmoving brightness, moment after moment receives perfuming—what calculation can encompass this? … this stillness is not real, like a swift current that appears tranquil to the eye—its swiftness is not seen, but it is not the absence of flow."

The consciousness aggregate appears settled and unmoving—but the Buddha uses an exceedingly precise refutation: if the consciousness aggregate were truly permanent and unchanging, it should have no capacity to be "perfumed." Yet you see some marvelous object years ago, and when you see it again after many years, you recognize it at once—this proves that the consciousness aggregate is **perfumed moment by moment.** It does flow, it does change; it only flows so fast it looks like no flow. "A swift current that appears tranquil to the eye."

Set these five layers of the aggregates side by side, and a clear spectrum of refinement appears:

| Aggregate | Delusive thinking | Unreal-real structure of the object | Core image |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form | Solid | Unreal cause, real effect | Thinking condensed into substance (saliva from speaking of sourness) |
| Feeling | Illusively bright | Half unreal, half real | No substance but able to receive (driven by favor and adversity) |
| Perception | Fluid | Unreal and real fused | The mediator between the two ends (thought driving the body) |
| Formations | Hidden | Pure flow | No resting-point (secret shift moment by moment) |
| Consciousness | Vacuous, inverted | Seeming-real but unreal | The false appearance of settled stillness (the swift current) |

Two features of this spectrum must be seen clearly.

**First, the object grows ever more detached from the "real" end.** From the form aggregate's "real effect" all the way to the consciousness aggregate's "seeming-real but unreal," the whole process is one in which the real component gradually evaporates. By the consciousness aggregate, all that is left of the "real" is a single word, "seeming"—it looks real, but it is not real.

**Second, the object grows ever more detached from "concrete phenomena."** From the form aggregate's concrete body, to the feeling aggregate's two phenomena of favor and adversity, to the perception aggregate's unreal-real relation, to the formations aggregate's pure flow, to the consciousness aggregate's false appearance of settled stillness—the object grows ever more abstract, ever closer to "the form of phenomena" rather than "phenomena themselves."

This spectrum explains why the five sections of the fifty māras grow ever subtler and ever harder to discern. It is not that the meditator grows more confused as he advances, but that **the object itself grows more refined.** The traps of the form-aggregate section (light-displays, paranormal powers) are easy to identify, because the object is coarse; the traps of the consciousness-aggregate section (the settled stillness that seems unified, seems permanent) are extremely hard to identify, because the object is already "seeming-real but unreal." The whole internal difficulty-gradient of the fifty māras is just the outward expression of this spectrum of unreal-and-real.

This spectrum also explains why the principle "do not form a sagely realization" takes different forms across the five aggregates. For a coarse object, the form is "do not grasp at substance" (do not take a light-display for attainment); for a fine object, the form is "do not establish supreme understanding" (do not take the seeming-real, unreal settled stillness for cessation). The remedy refines because the object refines.

### The Boundaries of the Five Aggregates—Each Aggregate Has Two Ends

Ānanda's third question was: "Of these five gradations, what constitutes the boundary of each?" Where do the boundaries between one aggregate and the next lie?

The Buddha's answer:

> Ānanda! These five received aggregates are constituted by five delusive thinkings. If you wish to know the depth and shallowness of their boundaries: **only form and space are the boundary of form; only contact and separation are the boundary of feeling; only retention and forgetting are the boundary of perception; only ceasing and arising are the boundary of formations; immersed-stillness and conjoined-stillness—these belong to the boundary of consciousness.**

Each aggregate has a pair of categories, and each pair is two ends:

- The boundary of form: **form—space**
- The boundary of feeling: **contact—separation**
- The boundary of perception: **retention—forgetting**
- The boundary of formations: **ceasing—arising**
- The boundary of consciousness: **immersed-stillness—conjoined-stillness**

This passage looks like a dry list of categories, but inside it lies an important structural insight—**each aggregate is not a single category but a pair of mutually defining ends.**

The form aggregate is not just "form." It is the entire range of the pair "form—space." **To break "form" but cling to "space" is still not to have left the boundary of form**—because "space" is only the other end of the form aggregate. The feeling aggregate is not just "contact." To break "contact" but rest in "separation" is still not to have left the feeling aggregate—"separation" is the other pole of feeling. And so on: break "retention" but sink into "forgetting," break "ceasing" but rest in "arising," break "immersed-stillness" but cling to "conjoined-stillness"—each of these is to break **only one end.** The other end remains, and the boundary has not yet been crossed.

Jiāoguāng delivers an extremely heavy verdict on this point: "**All clear and bright dharma-body remains short of the ultimate.**" Even at the height of "clear and bright dharma-body"—a height that in ordinary Buddhist parlance is nearly synonymous with awakening—one has still not crossed the far end of the consciousness aggregate's boundary. Why? Because "clear and bright" is one end, the "immersed-stillness" pole, and "dharma-body" is the other end, the "conjoined-stillness" pole. To abide only in "clear and bright" without passing through the "dharma-body" end, or only in "dharma-body" without passing through the "clear and bright" end, is still to be inside the consciousness aggregate.

This "two-end structure" is the core insight of the aggregate boundaries. It tells the meditator something very easy to overlook: **leaving an aggregate requires letting go of both ends at once**—breaking one end is not enough.

This structure has a direct counterpart in Avalokiteśvara's chapter on the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty. Describing his own cultivation, Avalokiteśvara says: "At first, in hearing, I entered the stream and lost the place where I entered. **The place of entry being still, the two characteristics of motion and rest were both clearly not produced** …"—motion and rest are two ends, and both must be let go. "Thus gradually advancing, **the hearing and the heard were both exhausted**; not abiding in exhausted hearing, **the awareness and the aware became empty**; emptiness and awareness becoming perfectly complete, **the empty and the emptied were dissolved**"—faculty and object, two ends both exhausted; awareness and the aware, two ends both emptied; emptiness and the emptied, two ends both dissolved. The undoing of each knot is "letting go of both ends," not "breaking one end."

This correspondence is no accident—the section below will unfold it directly.

### Sudden in Principle, Gradual in Phenomena

Ānanda's second question was: are the five aggregates extinguished simultaneously, or exhausted in sequence? The Buddha's answer arranges the relation between "sudden" and "gradual" with exquisite precision:

> These five aggregates fundamentally arise in overlapping fashion: **their birth occurs through consciousness, their extinction follows from form.** Principle is awakened all at once, and with that awakening all is dissolved together; phenomena cannot be removed all at once, but are exhausted in sequence. I have already shown you the knots of the kalpa-cloth—what is still unclear, that you ask again?

This passage is extremely dense, and must be read layer by layer.

**Overlapping arising, born through consciousness, extinguished from form down**—this describes the direction of the five aggregates' "arising" and "extinction." In arising, the five aggregates arise in overlapping fashion, and the direction is **from consciousness to form**—the innermost and subtlest consciousness aggregate arises first, then formations, perception, feeling, and last of all the outermost and coarsest form aggregate. In extinction, the direction reverses—**from form to consciousness**. The coarsest form aggregate is exhausted first, then feeling, perception, formations, and only last the consciousness aggregate.

Zǐxuán uses an everyday image to make this asymmetry plain: **putting on clothes** and **taking them off.** Putting on, you go from inner to outer—undergarments first, then the outer coat; taking off, you go from outer to inner—the coat first, then the undergarments. This is a natural order, not an arbitrary rule.

**Principle is awakened all at once, and with that awakening all is dissolved together**—this describes the level of *view*. At the level of view, awakening to the principle that "the five aggregates are one delusive thinking" can be the work of a single moment. Because the principle itself has no magnitude—it is the comprehension of a structure, and the instant comprehension dawns, the structure is seen through. Here Zǐxuán cites the *Sūtra of Perfect Enlightenment* in support of the "sudden principle" side: "Knowing illusion is itself separation from illusion; separation from illusion is itself awakening—there is no further gradation." The inverted thinking of countless eons can be dissolved at once along with this single moment of awakening—what is dissolved is "thinking," the confusion of view.

**Phenomena cannot be removed all at once, but are exhausted in sequence**—this describes the level of *seeds*. At the level of phenomena, the actual severing of the five aggregates cannot be accomplished all at once. Why? Because seeds are a structure accumulated in the *ālaya-vijñāna*—they have magnitude, they have layering. The seeds of the form aggregate, the seeds of the feeling aggregate, the seeds of the perception aggregate—these must be exhausted in order, from coarse to fine, from outer to inner. Zǐxuán uses two very ordinary images: **sunrise** and **a child's growth.** At sunrise the light gradually broadens; it does not leap from total darkness to high noon. A child grows gradually; he does not become an adult the instant he is born. These are "natural gradations," not human-made stages.

"Sudden in principle, gradual in phenomena" is one of the *Śūraṅgama*'s fundamental contributions to the classification of meditative practice. It differs from gradualism (phenomena gradual, and principle gradual too—each stage with a new view to learn) and from certain readings of subitism (principle sudden, and phenomena sudden too—awaken, and all is finished). The *Śūraṅgama*'s position is a third path: **sudden awakening, gradual cultivation**—the view can open all at once, but the severing of seeds must proceed in sequence.

This structure carries three implications for the reader.

**First**, it forestalls a misunderstanding of "sudden awakening." Sudden awakening is not "once you awaken, everything is finished." Sudden awakening is only "the view opens in a single moment." After the view opens, the actual exhaustion of the five aggregates still requires sequence—and this sequence is not a denial of sudden awakening but the **natural unfolding, at the level of phenomena, of what follows sudden awakening.**

**Second**, it strictly distinguishes "the opening of view" from "the exhaustion of seeds." View can be sudden, because the principle has no magnitude; seeds must be gradual, because latent dispositions have magnitude and layering. To confuse the two is one of the most common errors in meditative practice—mistaking "the sudden opening of view" for "the sudden exhaustion of seeds" (a refinement of "taking the unfinished as finished"), or mistaking "the gradual exhaustion of seeds" for "the gradual opening of view" (a denial of the possibility of sudden awakening).

**Third**—this layer is the most important—it gives the fifty māras a structural location. **All fifty phenomena of the fifty māras belong to the "gradual phenomena" layer.** They are the typical wrong turns that may appear once the view has opened and the practitioner has entered the sequential advance of phenomena. The appearance of any one of the māras is not "a problem with view," but "a typical error in the sequential advance at the level of phenomena."

This distinction is enormously important. It means a meditator may have entirely correct view (principle already opened all at once) and yet still meet the māras at the level of phenomena—this is not a contradiction; it is the necessary result of the "sudden principle, gradual phenomena" structure. More sharply put: **the appearance of māras is a sign of meditative progress**, not a sign of meditative failure. Only the meditator who has already entered the sequential advance of phenomena can meet the māras. The meditator with no progress meets no māras at all—because he has not even begun to stir the form aggregate.

The key to handling the māras is never "do not enter this road," but "having entered, do not stop at any position on the road"—which is exactly how the overarching principle "do not form a sagely realization" unfolds at the level of gradual phenomena.

### The Knots of the Kalpa-Cloth—Avalokiteśvara's Perfect Penetration and the Fifty Māras Share One Structure

Right after "sudden in principle, gradual in phenomena," the Buddha adds a single line: "**I have already shown you the knots of the kalpa-cloth—what is still unclear, that you ask again?**"

This line connects the discussion of the aggregates' exhaustion in Fascicle Ten directly to the parable of the kalpa-cloth knots in Fascicle Five. In Fascicle Five, the Buddha took the flower-cloth offered by the deities of the Kalpa heaven, tied it before the assembly into six knots, and asked Ānanda each time: "What is this called?" Each time, Ānanda answered: "This is called a knot." The Buddha then asked: this cloth was originally one—why, after six tyings, do you call each a "knot"? Ānanda could not answer. The Buddha then taught: one cloth, six knots—the flower-cloth is originally one substance, and through six tyings becomes six names. The six knots are the six faculties. The six faculties issue from one nature, and become six through discrimination.

Then the Buddha asked a further question—how does one undo a knot? Ānanda said one could pull from the left, or pull from the right. The Buddha said no. **"You must work at the heart of the knot; release it and it scatters."** One must work from the center of the knot. One cannot pull hard from the two sides—pulling hard only draws the knot tighter.

And there is a still more important line: "**These knots arose in sequence by tying; today they must be released in sequence.**" The knots were tied in sequence, and must be released in sequence too. The first knot must be undone before the second; the second before the third.

In Fascicle Five this parable looks like a self-contained teaching, but the moment the Buddha says in Fascicle Ten, "I have already shown you the knots of the kalpa-cloth," the entire cultivational architecture of the *Śūraṅgama* is drawn into a single line. **The kalpa-cloth knots of Fascicle Five are the aggregate-exhaustion of Fascicle Ten.** The cloth is one, the knots are six; awareness is one, the aggregates are five. The knots must be undone in sequence; the aggregates must be exhausted in sequence. The knots cannot be pulled from the two sides; the aggregates cannot be broken at only one end. The knots must be undone at the heart of the knot; so must the aggregates—work from the center of the knot, not from the two sides.

And in Fascicle Six, when Avalokiteśvara describes the sequence of his own cultivation of the perfect penetration of the ear-faculty, he gives the complete cultivational form of "undoing the knots in sequence":

> At first, in hearing, I entered the stream and lost the place where I entered. The place of entry being still, the two characteristics of motion and rest were both clearly not produced. Thus gradually advancing, the hearing and the heard were both exhausted; not abiding in exhausted hearing, the awareness and the aware became empty; emptiness and awareness becoming perfectly complete, the empty and the emptied were dissolved. Birth and ceasing being already ceased, quiescence appeared. Suddenly I transcended the worldly and the supramundane, and the ten directions became perfectly bright.

This passage lays out the sequential undoing of six knots: the knot of motion, the knot of rest, the knot of faculty, the knot of awareness, the knot of emptiness, the knot of cessation. And then a seventh—"suddenly transcending the worldly and the supramundane."

Now look at an extremely important verdict from Jiāoguāng Zhēnjiàn, in the latter part of Fascicle Ten of his *Commentary on the True Lineage*:

> Investigating the principle, one should know clearly that **Avalokiteśvara's previous releasing of the six knots and entering perfect penetration is completely identical to the present breaking of the five aggregates and entering the first abiding**—only the dharma-numbers, six previously and five here, differ in their grouping and division, making detailed correspondence difficult.

**Completely identical.** Two words.

Here Jiāoguāng plainly judges that the ear-faculty perfect penetration of Fascicle Six (the undoing of six knots) and the fifty skandha-māras of Fascicles Nine and Ten (the sequential exhaustion of five aggregates) are **two unfoldings of one and the same structure.** The former uses six knots, the latter uses five aggregates; the only difference is that "the dharma-numbers, six previously and five here, differ in their grouping"—the two numberings do not align perfectly, but in essence they are "completely identical."

Jiāoguāng then gives the correspondence: the "form-space, contact-separation, retention-forgetting, ceasing-arising, immersed-stillness" of the five aggregates correspond to the first three knots of Avalokiteśvara's penetration (motion, rest, faculty), and the final "conjoined-stillness" of the five aggregates corresponds to the latter three knots (awareness, emptiness, cessation).

The weight of this correspondence is immense.

It means, first—**the ground of cause and the ground of fruit are one road.** Avalokiteśvara's penetration in Fascicle Six is "cultivation at the ground of cause"—it tells the meditator how he should cultivate. The fifty māras of Fascicles Nine and Ten are "meditation at the ground of fruit"—they tell the meditator what he will meet while cultivating. Traditionally these two passages are seen as two independent units: Fascicle Six gives methodology, Fascicles Nine and Ten give a catalogue of traps. But Jiāoguāng's "completely identical" verdict says: **these two structures are two faces of one road.** The seven-stage cultivation of Avalokiteśvara's penetration is the very road walked through the aggregate-exhaustion of the fifty māras; the fifty traps of the fifty māras are the very wrong turns that may be met along Avalokiteśvara's penetration.

It means, second—**the ear-faculty penetration is the "typical form," not a "special option."** Among the twenty-five perfect penetrations, the ear-faculty penetration is often regarded as "the most expedient gateway for the beings of this Sahā world"—and the root of this expedience is usually explained as "a property of the ear-faculty." Jiāoguāng's "completely identical" offers a further explanation: **the ear-faculty penetration is typical because its cultivational sequence is perfectly isomorphic with the form of aggregate-exhaustion.** Whichever faculty one enters by, the road walked is the same—the sequential exhaustion of the five aggregates; only the entry-point differs.

It means, third—and this layer is especially crucial for the reader who has finished the previous chapters—**the fifty māras are not a "list of warnings"; they are a "map of cultivation."**

This is an important repositioning. The previous four chapters (6.9 to 6.12) presented the fifty māras as a set of traps—ten traps per aggregate, fifty in all, looking full of danger. It is easy for the reader to feel: **this road is too dangerous; one wrong step is a pit.** But Jiāoguāng's "completely identical" verdict tells the reader: the very appearance of these fifty traps **proves that this road is real, structured, and directional.** Where there is no road, there are no wrong turns. Only a real road can have wrong turns. The fifty wrong turns are marked on the map precisely because there truly is a road running there.

Reading the fifty māras is not learning "fifty forbidden things." Reading the fifty māras is **seeing clearly the shape of this road**—its starting point (the form aggregate), its destination (the exhaustion of the consciousness aggregate), its five great stations along the way (the five aggregates), and the directions in which it may fork at each station (the ten typical wrong turns). Once this map has entered your mind, it is there. From then on, whatever meditative phenomenon arises, you can set it back into its corresponding place on the map.

This is the inner closure of the S6 series from 6.8 to 6.13—**fifty phenomena drawn together into a single map.**

### Where the Map Leads—Mutual Functioning of the Faculties, Vajra-Dry Wisdom, Wonderful Enlightenment

So where does the map lead?

Fascicle Ten of the *Śūraṅgama* gives a very clear description of the destination of cultivation once the aggregates are exhausted:

> **When the consciousness aggregate is exhausted, the present six faculties function in mutual interchange. From mutual interchange, one is able to enter the bodhisattva's vajra-dry wisdom**, and the perfectly bright, refined mind transforms within it.

Three nodes—mutual functioning of the faculties, vajra-dry wisdom, wonderful enlightenment.

**Mutual functioning of the faculties**—the direct consequence of the consciousness aggregate's exhaustion. The eye can hear sound; the ear can see form; each faculty contains the use of all five. This is not the display of some paranormal power; it is the realized proof of "the faculties' partitions opening and merging." When the consciousness aggregate—the very root of discrimination—is crossed, the partitions among the six faculties, originally built by discrimination, naturally fall away, and the six faculties are restored to one whole. Jiāoguāng judges plainly: exhaustion of the consciousness aggregate = mutual functioning of the faculties = entering the first abiding = witnessing perfect penetration.

**Vajra-dry wisdom**—beginning from the first abiding. Jiāoguāng delivers an extremely important verdict:

> In the perfect school there are only two positions: one cuts the prior pervasive delusions, leaping in one bound from full contemplative practice straight into the first abiding, **with no taking-of-attestation in between**; the other cuts the later distinct delusions, leaping in one bound from entering the first abiding straight to equal enlightenment, **again with no taking-of-attestation in between.**

"No taking-of-attestation in between." From contemplative practice to the first abiding, from the first abiding to equal enlightenment, none of the intermediate positions is treated as a place where one "should stop and attest." **No taking-of-attestation**—this is exactly how the overarching principle "do not form a sagely realization" unfolds across the fifty-five positions. From the first rousing of the mind to the final mind of equal enlightenment, not one of the fifty-five positions is the final ultimate.

**Wonderful enlightenment**—the one and only ultimate. This is the destination of the map, and also the one position that needs no remedy by "do not form a sagely realization"—because it is no longer a "position" at all, but "the thorough transcendence of the very category of position." Any thought that takes "wonderful enlightenment" as one more position to be transcended pulls it back inside the category of "position," and so misses its very nature.

But this three-node map has its trap as well. In his commentary on the aggregate boundaries, Jiāoguāng leaves a verdict heavy enough to bring out a sweat:

> **All clear and bright dharma-body remains short of the ultimate.**

Even an attainment such as "clear and bright dharma-body"—in ordinary Buddhist parlance very nearly a synonym for awakening—**still has not reached the far end of the consciousness aggregate's boundary.** This is not meant to crush the meditator's confidence. It is meant to pull the category of "the ultimate" thoroughly free of every particular attainment. **The ultimate is not any position of "I have already arrived."** The ultimate is the positionless position that lies beyond the thorough transcendence of the category "position" itself.

All clear and bright dharma-body remains short of the ultimate—this line is perhaps the last warning the whole map of the fifty māras gives the reader.

### Transmitting It to the Latter Age—To You Who Have Read This Far

After the section on the five delusive thinkings, Fascicle Ten of the *Śūraṅgama* records an exceptionally important word the Buddha speaks to Ānanda:

> You should take this root of delusive thinking, having gained understanding of it, and **transmit it to the practitioners of the latter dharma age**, so that they may recognize delusion, develop deep aversion, know there is *nirvāṇa*, and not cling to the three realms.

This sentence was written more than thirteen hundred years ago.

The Buddha designates with his own mouth that the recipients of this teaching are "**the practitioners of the latter dharma age**"—which is to say, this teaching was never prepared for those present at the time. It was prepared for those who, more than two thousand years later, would read this passage.

And you are this sentence's counterpart today.

You, reading this far, are at this moment doing something rare.

In all of history, the overwhelming majority of people—even the ordained, even those who devote themselves to practice—seldom finish, in a single lifetime, the full exposition of the fifty māras, let alone the structural gathering that draws those fifty phenomena back into five delusive thinkings and then back into one principle. This is not because the teaching was deliberately hidden—it is right there in the Canon, open to anyone—but because the subtlety and the length of this teaching cause the overwhelming majority of readers to stop partway.

Whoever can read this chapter has already done something exceedingly rare in history: **read through the entire structural map of the fifty māras.**

To read through this map is, in itself, an auspicious thing. Why? Because once this map enters your mind-continuum, **it cannot be deleted.**

What does this mean? A single thought of good, one contact with the true Dharma, one flash of correct view—each plants a seed in the *ālaya-vijñāna*. This seed does not sprout at once. It may take many lifetimes of conditions to ripen. But once planted, **it does not disappear.** Having read through the whole map of the fifty māras, that map already exists, in the form of a seed, in the field of your consciousness.

What does that imply?

It implies that you need not now feel anxious about "whether I can finish walking this road in this lifetime." The overwhelming majority of people will not finish in this lifetime—all fifty positions of the fifty māras are not a journey an ordinary meditator can complete in a few years, or even a few decades. But you do not need to finish it. You need only **let this map become one thread of conditions within your mind-continuum.**

Any future meditative experience—whether it occurs in this life or a life to come—will be recognized, located, and transformed along this thread of conditions. A meditator without this thread, meeting the ten states of the form aggregate, may take a light-display for an attainment; a meditator with this thread, meeting a light-display, will automatically recall—the form-aggregate section; do not grasp at light. This is the seed in the field of consciousness sprouting when its conditions ripen.

**To read is the first step of practice. And it is an irreversible step.**

Let me use an image to convey the weight of this.

Imagine you are handed the syllabus of a twelve-year doctoral program. The syllabus lays out, clearly, what courses you take each year, what difficulties you will meet each year, and what problem your final thesis must solve. You spend five minutes reading the whole syllabus through—and afterward you have a complete grasp of the program's structure. You know what the first year is, what the twelfth year is, and the place and meaning of each year in between.

But **"finishing the syllabus" and "actually completing the program" are two entirely different things.**

Reading the syllabus takes five minutes. Completing the program takes twelve years. And every year you will find something new to overcome: in the third year you may be stuck on some problem for months; in the seventh you may suddenly find that a foundation from your first six years was never laid properly and must be made up; in the tenth you may come to doubt the whole direction of the program and want to give up. These are real, necessary stages that the act of "reading the syllabus" cannot resolve in advance. Reading the syllabus can only let you "know the road is like this"; it cannot let you "have already walked this road."

The program of the fifty māras and the doctoral program share two likenesses and one crucial difference.

**Likeness one**: both have the two-tier structure of "syllabus first, then actual practice." The one who has read the syllabus first has a structural advantage in actual practice over the one who has not—because he knows what position he is now in, what the next position is, and where the road's direction lies. The meditator without a syllabus must grope afresh at every phenomenon; the meditator with one can set each phenomenon directly back into its corresponding place on the map.

**Likeness two**: both have the stagewise character of "every year you find something new to overcome." Reading the syllabus is not completing the program. Reading the map of the fifty māras is not walking the road of aggregate-exhaustion. Each aggregate, each position, will reappear in actual practice in the form of "something to be personally overcome." This is not a failure of the syllabus; it is the inherent relation between syllabus and practice—**the syllabus is a map, practice is walking, and however accurate the map, it will not walk the road for you.**

**The crucial difference**: but the program of the fifty māras differs fundamentally from the doctoral program in one respect—**the doctoral program is completed within one lifetime; the program of the fifty māras is completed across many lifetimes.** The reader who has finished this map need feel no anxiety about "whether I can finish in this life," because this road was never a matter of one life. The bodhisattva path recorded in the sūtras is measured in *kalpas*, not in years. From the first rousing of his mind to Buddhahood, Śākyamuni Buddha passed through three immeasurable *kalpas*—a span far beyond the range of any one lifetime. What you need to do is not "finish in this life," but **from today, give yourself a clear position on this map.**

Even if it is only "I am still in the form-aggregate section, still practicing not grasping at light"—this very sense of position is already the real beginning of the road. The sense of position is the starting point of practice, not its end. A meditator who clearly knows his position is in an entirely different state from a meditator lost and bewildered among fifty phenomena.

Reading the whole map of the fifty māras, one emotion easily arises—"this road is too long; I cannot possibly finish it in this life; so why walk it at all?"

This emotion rests on a misunderstanding: **it assumes that "finishing" is the purpose of practice.**

The purpose of practice is not to finish. The purpose of practice is to **be on the road.** To set yourself on a correct road, to make every present moment a position on that road, to let your mind-continuum refine itself step by step along it. Finishing is the direction, not the condition. **The one who, in the right direction, advances one kilometer a day is closer to the destination than the one who, in the wrong direction, advances a hundred kilometers a day.** For the former, "when will I reach the end" is not an object of anxiety; for the latter, the faster he runs, the farther from the end he goes.

What the whole map of the fifty māras gives you is not the anxiety of "how long this road is," but the clarity of "the direction of this road is thus." Direction matters more than speed; the path matters more than the distance; the sense of position matters more than the sense of arrival. The one who has read this map has already gained those three rarest things—direction, path, and sense of position. What remains is only **the next step, beginning today.**

The *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*'s final word to Ānanda is also its final word to you:

> **So that they may recognize delusion, develop deep aversion, know there is *nirvāṇa*, and not cling to the three realms.**

Rendered in today's language—recognize the nature of delusion, and the mind of aversion rises of itself; know there is the exit called *nirvāṇa*, and you no longer cling to the various positions within the three realms.

Having read the whole map of the fifty māras, if there arises in you a clear sense that "so this is how the road is walked," if there arises toward certain positions among the five aggregates (light-displays, intense emotion, outward projection, mistaken verdicts of view, the final grasping) a loosening that says "these I no longer need to cling to," if you feel toward words like "mutual functioning of the faculties," "vajra-dry wisdom," "wonderful enlightenment" a distant pull—then those eight words, "**having gained understanding of the root of delusive thinking**," have already taken place, in the latter dharma age, in you.

This is not my verdict. It is the verdict of the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* itself. The Buddha's last word in Fascicle Ten is spoken to you.

### From "Establish No Ultimate Position" to "There Is Only One Ultimate Position"

This part—illumination—comes to its close here.

Across the five chapters just walked, the principle "do not form a sagely realization" has appeared again and again. In the form-aggregate section, it was "do not take a light-display for attainment." In the feeling-aggregate section, "do not take a change of feeling for attainment." In the perception-aggregate section, "do not be deceived by the mirror-other into thinking yourself a sage." In the formations-aggregate section, "do not pronounce a thorough verdict on the nature of the states you perceive." In the consciousness-aggregate section, "do not establish supreme understanding of the subtle objects of consciousness as the truly permanent, and do not crown a stagewise attainment as the summit of awakening."

Five stages, one principle. The direction of refinement points, in every case, to a single form—**establish no ultimate at any intermediate position.**

But this very principle gives rise to a natural question: **where, then, is the ultimate?**

If no intermediate position can be established as ultimate, then where, in the end, is the ultimate? Should even "wonderful enlightenment," the destination itself, be met with "do not form a sagely realization"? And if there is no ultimate either, is not the whole of practice a road without an end?

This question the *Śūraṅgama* does not fully answer head-on. Its answer stops at "no taking-of-attestation in between"—from the first rousing of the mind to the final mind of equal enlightenment, none of the fifty-five positions is the final ultimate, and the ultimate lies only at the last stage. What it leaves is a negative form: **no intermediate position is the ultimate.**

What that "one and only ultimate position" looks like, how it is confirmed, how one moves toward it—these the *Śūraṅgama* does not unfold head-on. It leaves these questions to the next sūtra.

Among the Mahāyāna scriptures, the one that answers these questions head-on is the *Lotus Sūtra*.

The center of the *Lotus* is not "enumerating errors" but "pointing out the one correct direction." It establishes, positively, that "there is only one ultimate position," through two narrative structures. The first is **opening the provisional to reveal the real**—the three vehicles taught in the past (*śrāvaka*, *pratyekabuddha*, bodhisattva) are all expedient; in reality there is only one vehicle (the Buddha vehicle). The three vehicles are provisional; the one vehicle is real. All expedient gateways point to the same ultimate, and that ultimate is one. The second is **the conjured city and the treasure-place**—to keep the weary travelers from turning back, the guide conjures a treasure-city midway for them to rest in; the travelers think they have arrived at their destination, and once they have rested enough, the guide says: this is only a conjured city; the real treasure-place is still ahead. The conjured city vanishes, and the travelers walk on.

The *Śūraṅgama*'s "establish no ultimate position" and the *Lotus*'s "there is only one ultimate position" seem, on the surface, to contradict each other—one negates the intermediate, the other affirms the terminal. But in fact they are two faces of the same structure:

**The conjured city is precisely the fifty māras**—each māra is an intermediate conjured city. It truly appears in the course of meditation, and it looks just like the destination. The light-display of the form aggregate is a conjured city; the pliancy of the feeling aggregate is a conjured city; the skillfulness of the perception aggregate is a conjured city; the formations aggregate's "I have seen through the nature of the world" is a conjured city; the consciousness aggregate's "clear and bright dharma-body" is a conjured city. Each truly exists; none is the treasure-place.

**"The real treasure-place is still ahead" is precisely "do not form a sagely realization"**—the guide's reminder to the travelers and the Buddha's reminder to the meditator are the same act. Do not take the conjured city for the treasure-place; the real treasure-place is still ahead.

**"There is only one treasure-place" is precisely "wonderful enlightenment"**—the *Lotus*'s treasure-place and the *Śūraṅgama*'s wonderful enlightenment are both "the thorough transcendence of the category of position," both the ultimate position that "cannot be further negated."

**"The conjured city vanishes" is precisely "the aggregate is exhausted"**—when the meditator truly crosses an aggregate, all the phenomena of that aggregate (its traps included) lose the character of "position" and become pure expedient. No longer "the thing that once deceived me," but "a stretch of road already walked."

The *Śūraṅgama* uses meditative phenomenology to say "no intermediate position is the ultimate"; the *Lotus* uses narrative structure to say "there is only one ultimate position." The two sūtras together are the perfect teaching—**without Part VI, the reader stops at a midway conjured city; without Part VII, the reader cannot find the direction of the treasure-place.** The two are one set. The former breaks attachment, the latter reveals the real; the former negates the intermediate, the latter affirms the terminal; the former is the negative form, the latter the positive form.

The task of this part—illumination—is complete. It has given you the whole structural map of the fifty māras, shown you what each midway conjured city looks like, told you why you must not stop at any conjured city.

The next part—gathering-in—will place the **direction** of this long road in your hands: telling you what treasure-place truly waits ahead, telling you that this seemingly endless road has a real destination, telling you why the three vehicles are expedient and why the one vehicle is ultimate.

Part VI ends; Part VII begins.

The demon-revealing mirror has finished its work. The next mirror to be raised reflects no longer the shape of the wrong turns, but the direction of the road home.
