## 6.7 The Four Clear and Decisive Instructions on Purity—The First Protective Boundary Before Entering

### The Sequence Is Itself the Teaching

At the close of the previous chapter we said that this chapter would treat "the guardrails for after you have entered the door." That statement needs correcting.

The sequence of the sūtra runs like this—the four clear and decisive instructions on purity appear in the latter half of fascicle six, the place of practice and the Śūraṅgama *dhāraṇī* appear in fascicle seven, and the three gradual stages appear in fascicle eight. In other words, the order in which the Buddha taught Ānanda was: **first establish the precepts, then establish the spatial place of practice, and only last unfold the purification of the mind.**

This means that the three gradual stages of the previous chapter—the purification of the mind—are not the starting point of the whole preparatory package. They are the innermost layer. The true first step lies further out still: the four clear and decisive instructions on purity in the latter half of fascicle six.

The three-layered structure of protective boundaries is therefore:

- **Outermost layer**: the guardrail of precepts (the four instructions)—the first protective boundary before entering the door.
- **Middle layer**: the guardrail of space (the place of practice plus the Śūraṅgama *dhāraṇī*)—the purifying and the empowering of the physical space.
- **Innermost layer**: the purification of the mind (the three gradual stages plus the ground of dry wisdom)—the purification procedure that formally unfolds inside the boundary.

The order cannot be exchanged. The precepts must be established first—if they are not, there is no meaning even in building a place of practice; the place of practice must be established before the purification—if the physical space has no clean boundary, the purification of the three gradual stages will be continuously disturbed by incoming conditions from outside.

So the four instructions are not "protection while you are walking on the road." They are **the first protective boundary you must raise before you have even set foot inside the place of practice**. This distinction is enormously important, because it determines how we read every word that follows—these words are not behavioral regulations for someone already in the midst of practice. They are **conditions of entry**.

### Mind-Containment as Precept

After the twenty-five avenues of perfect penetration and Mañjuśrī's selection, Ānanda did not immediately enter practice. He first raised a very practical question:

> World-Honored One, these sentient beings are gradually far from the Buddha; false teachers proclaiming the Dharma are as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. For those who would gather their minds and enter *samādhi*, how should they establish a place of practice, and distance themselves from the deeds of Māra?

This is a request born of the consciousness of the age of declining Dharma. Ānanda is not asking for himself; he is asking on behalf of the beings of that age—how does one build a place where practice can actually occur? How does one stay far from the deeds of Māra? How does one keep the mind of awakening from retreating?

The first layer of the Buddha's answer is the general framework of the three superior trainings:

> Mind-containment constitutes precept; precept gives rise to *samādhi*; *samādhi* gives rise to wisdom.

Pay special attention to the words the Buddha uses—"**mind-containment** constitutes precept," not "ceasing evil constitutes precept." The commentator Zǐxuán saw the weight of these four characters—it is exactly here that the Mahāyāna and Hīnayāna understandings of the precepts part ways.

**The Hīnayāna precepts are "violated through the enacted deed, with severity corresponding to the act":** one holds the precepts according to the gravity of the behavioral act itself—the degrees of killing, stealing, and lust differ, and the violation or non-violation, the lightness or severity of a precept, has a clear scale.

**The Mahāyāna precepts are "guarding each individual mental movement inwardly, with all offenses held equally":** the standard is the movement of the mind—the moment a thought arises it is already a violation, guarded against without gradation of severity.

Jiāoguāng pushes this point to a sharper edge: "We take 'no single thought arising' as the precept"—the operative definition of mind-containment as precept is that the precept is already enacted before a thought has even begun to arise.

This definition sets the coordinates for reading all four of the instructions that follow. When the Buddha says "their mind does not lust," "their mind does not kill," "their mind does not steal," "their mind does not deceive," what He speaks of is not "not doing" but **the thought not arising**. The position of those two words "their mind" matters more than the words that come after them—they tell you that this is not a precept of body and speech but a precept of mind.

### The First Instruction: Their Mind Does Not Lust

The first instruction the Buddha unfolds is the cutting-off of lust:

> If any sentient being of the six realms, throughout all worlds, has a mind that does not lust, then they will not follow the continuity of birth-and-death. Your cultivation of *samādhi* aims at escape from defilement, but if the lustful mind is not eliminated, there is no escape from defilement.

The structure of this passage will recur later—six fixed components: the positive statement (their mind does not X → they will not follow birth-and-death), the inverse statement (the X-mind not eliminated → no escape from defilement), the karmic consequence in three grades, the prophecy of the declining age, the sealing phrase that names the instruction, and the parable together with the Buddha-versus-Māra criterion.

Why does the lust-precept come first? This is a striking difference between the Śūraṅgama Sūtra and other scriptures. Most scriptures place the killing-precept first; the Śūraṅgama places the lust-precept first. Jiāoguāng cites Wēnlíng's explanation: "In other sūtras the precept against killing comes first, because establishing teaching takes compassion as its primary principle. In this sūtra the lust-precept comes first, because authentic cultivation takes renunciation of desire as the root."—Other scriptures face the work of universally delivering sentient beings, and so take compassion as foremost; the Śūraṅgama faces the practitioner who is formally entering *samādhi*, and for such a person, **renunciation of desire is the foundation**. Jiāoguāng continues: "The energy of desire is coarse and turbid, defiling the wondrous clarity.... In perpetuating birth-and-death and destroying the true permanence, nothing exceeds this."

The parable of this instruction is "steaming sand to make rice":

> If you cultivate *samādhi* without cutting off lust, it is like steaming sand and gravel hoping to produce rice. Even after a hundred thousand *kalpas*, it will only be called hot sand. Why? Because rice was never its substance—it is made of stone.

No matter how long you steam sand, it will never become rice, because it was never the material of rice to begin with. To cultivate meditation without cutting off the lustful mind is like cooking rice from sand—you can steam it a very long time, very earnestly, but the result will only be hot sand, never grains of rice.

And then comes a line of perfect-sudden extremity: "**You must make both body and mind cut off lust completely, and then cut off even the nature of cutting-off itself; only then can the enlightenment of the Buddha be hoped for.**"

The five characters "even the nature of cutting-off is not" are the summit of the whole instruction. Zǐxuán's commentary: "One who truly observes precepts does not even have the concept of 'observing'—how could one then, in body or mind, commit a grave violation?" The ultimate of precept-observance is not merely non-violation—it is to lay down even the thought "I am observing precepts," "I have already cut this off." Bodily cutting → mental cutting → the dissolution of the very concept of cutting. These are the three depths of precept-observance, each penetrating further than the last.

### The Second Instruction: Their Mind Does Not Kill

The structure of the second instruction is exactly parallel to the first. Within this instruction comes an important clarification—concerning the "five pure meats."

> Ānanda, when I allowed bhikṣus to eat the five pure meats, these meats were all produced by my spiritual power—they had no life-root. Your Brahmanical land is hot and damp, with stones and sand, where vegetation cannot grow; through my great compassionate spiritual power, I provided what was nominally "meat" so you could sustain yourselves. How then, after the Tathāgata's *parinirvāṇa*, can those who eat the flesh of sentient beings still be called disciples of Śākyamuni?

During the Buddha's lifetime certain bhikṣus were permitted to eat the "five pure meats" (meat from an animal one did not see killed, did not hear killed, was not killed for one's own sake, and so on), but this was an expedient under specific geographical conditions—in the Brahmanical land vegetation would not grow, and only meat produced by spiritual power could sustain the life of the saṃgha. Jiāoguāng's judgment is decisive: "Any instance in which the Buddha permitted the eating of meat was a skillful expedient in his lifetime.... After his *parinirvāṇa*, it is the flesh of beings whose lives were actually taken—can one still eat it?" During the Buddha's lifetime it was an expedient; the deep sūtras after his *parinirvāṇa* "abrogate the expedient and reveal the real"—and this is something to take special note of in the age of declining Dharma.

Jiāoguāng adds another weighty line: "**Although one does not slaughter directly, one causes the killing through payment—the karma is identical.** Therefore eating meat is killing." You did not kill with your own hand, but you pay with money, and the money drives another to kill—the karma is the same.

The parable of this instruction is "stopping one's ears and shouting":

> If one cultivates *samādhi* without cutting off killing, it is like a person who stops their own ears and shouts loudly, seeking not to be heard. This is what is called "trying to hide but becoming more exposed."

To stop up your own ears and then shout aloud, hoping others will not hear—the more you conceal, the more obvious it becomes. To cultivate meditation without cutting off the karma of killing looks exactly like this.

### The Third Instruction: Their Mind Does Not Steal

The third instruction cuts off stealing. Jiāoguāng's definition of "stealing" extends remarkably wide: "This kind of stealing is not only the taking of another's physical property—it includes every instance of deceptive speech and conduct, every act of intimidation, extending even to **a single thought of hoping for offerings**." The moment a thought arises in the mind hoping to obtain offerings, it is already theft.

The most egregious form is "borrowing the Tathāgata's robes to peddle the Tathāgata"—wearing Buddhist garb to sell the Dharma for profit. This is the gravest theft of the age of decline.

The parable of this instruction is "filling a leaking vessel with water":

> If one cultivates *samādhi* without cutting off stealing, it is like a person who pours water into a leaking vessel hoping to fill it—even after *kalpas* as numerous as dust, it will never be level.

The vessel here is a container with a hole in it. You keep pouring water in, hoping to fill it—but the vessel itself is broken, the water leaks away, and no matter how you pour it will never fill. Zǐxuán's commentary is exquisite: "Pouring the water of *samādhi* into a broken precept-vessel, hoping to fulfill the fruit—through dust-*kalpas* it will not fill. Whose fault is this? How can one not be careful! Inner virtue is empty, outer appearance deceives others; the precept-vessel is pierced, and wholesome dharmas leak away."

This parable is worth pausing on—it forms a pair with the cup of poisoned honey from the previous chapter. The previous chapter said: there is poison in the cup (the three gradual stages are needed to wash it out). This chapter says: the cup itself has a crack (the four instructions are needed to mend it). The two parables are two sides of one structure. **The three gradual stages wash the cup; the four instructions guarantee that the cup itself has no crack.** You cannot wash without mending—no matter how clean you wash it, a leaking cup still cannot hold the sweet dew.

### The Fourth Instruction: Their Mind Does Not Deceive

The literary form of the fourth instruction has a striking rupture from the first three—and the rupture is itself the teaching.

The first three all run "their mind does not X, then they do not follow the continuity of birth-and-death." The fourth becomes:

> Ānanda! Even if sentient beings of the six realms have bodies and minds free from killing, stealing, and lust—even if these three acts are already perfected—**if they commit gross false speech, their *samādhi* cannot attain purity**. They become love-view Māras, losing the seed of the Tathāgata.

Why does the literary form change? Jiāoguāng gives an exact explanation: "There are two kinds of false speech: minor false speech consists in saying things untruly; **gross false speech consists in falsely claiming spiritual realization**. What is cut off here is gross false speech. This must be **spoken aloud**—it is unlike the mind-based violations." The first three are violated at the level of the arising thought; the fourth must be "spoken aloud"—one must declare to another person "I have realized it"—before it is constituted.

The root of this difference lies in the special structure of gross false speech: **it is the only affliction that necessarily involves another**. A lustful thought can stay within the mind; killing can be done by one person alone; stealing can be done by one person alone. But gross false speech cannot—you must declare to another person "I have realized it" for it to be constituted. Without a listener, there is no gross false speech.

This is also why the fourth instruction directly foretells the fifty skandha-māras that come later—the seven characters "they become love-view Māras, losing the seed of the Tathāgata" are the ticket of entry to the chapters on the fifty skandha-māras in fascicles nine and ten. The trap-point in every single state among the fifty skandha-māras is never the state itself (the states are bound to arise in the course of practice); it is the practitioner's **outward claim** about the state—"each claiming to have attained the unsurpassed path." The state is not the problem; only at the moment you take it to be realization and begin telling others about it does the skandha-māra come into being.

In this instruction the Buddha also gives an extremely important iron rule. He says that in the age of declining Dharma, bodhisattvas and arhats will take birth to deliver sentient beings, manifesting in all kinds of forms (*śramaṇas*, lay Buddhists, rulers, officials, children, and even prostitutes, widows, butchers and merchants), dwelling alongside beings to guide them into *samādhi*. But—

> They will never say "I am truly a bodhisattva" or "I am truly an arhat," never revealing the Buddha's secret causation, never casually speaking to the unlearned—except at the moment of their own death, when they may leave a hidden indication.

A true bodhisattva, when manifesting an identity in the age of decline, must depart that very moment; if they reveal it and yet remain, they are not the genuine article. The three sages Fēnggān, Hánshān, and Shídé are the classic examples of this rule. Jiāoguāng condenses the iron rule into eight characters:

> If they remain, they do not reveal; if they reveal, they do not remain.

**A true sage never declares it of themselves; one who declares it is certainly not a true sage.** This is the most direct demon-revealing mirror to hold up to anyone in the age of decline who "declares their own realization."

The parable of the fourth instruction is "carving excrement into sandalwood shape," and at its close there is a positive line: "If bhikṣus have minds as straight as a lute-string, entirely truthful, entering *samādhi* they will permanently have no demonic affair." At the very end of precept-observance, the precept is no longer a precept—it is the intrinsic condition of the mind.

### "If I Speak This Way, This Is the Speech of the Buddha"

Each of the four instructions closes with the same formula, appearing four times in succession:

> If I speak this way, this is the speech of the Buddha; if one does not speak this way, this is the speech of Pāpīyas.

Pāpīyas is the Māra king. The meaning of this formula is that the Buddha personally sets down a criterion—and the authority of this criterion needs no external endorsement whatsoever. You simply check it against the words of the sūtra.

These four repetitions are not emphasis—they are **recursion**. Each instruction is in itself one complete act of Buddha-versus-Māra discrimination; the four discriminations together constitute, in turn, one larger Buddha-versus-Māra discrimination of the four instructions as a whole. You may keep three and break one—then on the fourth you have crossed over to the side of Pāpīyas; you may keep all four, but keep them at the level of the act rather than the movement of the mind—then on the whole you have again crossed over to the side of Pāpīyas. Every layer carries its Buddha-versus-Māra discernment, and no layer admits of vagueness.

This is why the four instructions must be a "checklist" and not an "operating manual"—the defining feature of a checklist is that **not one item may have a gap**. You cannot say "this one item I only did halfway, but on the whole it still counts." A protective boundary is not an operating manual (which can be partially executed); a protective boundary is a checklist (every item must be complete).

### The Boundary of the Ordination Altar

This "checklist" structure finds its most fitting echo within Buddhism's own ritual tradition—**the establishment of the ordination altar** (⚠️ a heuristic structural analogy, not an operational equivalence).

The establishment of a traditional ordination altar is a rigorous procedure. Before the ordination ceremony can begin, these steps must be completed in order: purification (cleansing the ground with pure water) → bounding (drawing the line that may not be crossed) → invoking the holy ones (welcoming the Buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions as witnesses) → raising the banners → laying out the altar → reciting the *dhāraṇī* → the formal reception of the precept-substance. The steps cannot be skipped, each step has the character of a Buddha-versus-Māra criterion, and the ordination altar is not itself the goal—it exists to provide a valid field for the ordination ceremony that follows.

The latter half of fascicle six through fascicle eight of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra is teaching you to build precisely such a **ordination altar of the mind**—

- The four instructions (latter half of fascicle six) = the boundary of the mind's ordination altar—drawing the limits clearly, four items with no gap.
- The place of practice and the Śūraṅgama *dhāraṇī* (fascicle seven) = the purifying and empowering of the mind's ordination altar—cleansing the physical space as well.
- The three gradual stages (fascicle eight) = the formal reception ceremony conducted inside this ordination altar.

⚠️ The boundary of this correspondence: the ordination altar is a structure of the physical and the ritual; the mind's ordination altar is a structure of mental movement and behavior—the two are structurally isomorphic, but they are not events of the same order. The ordination altar's purification uses water; the mind's ordination altar's purification is awareness, thought by thought. The ordination altar's boundary uses banners; the mind's ordination altar's boundary is the four lines of the four instructions. This analogy is structural, not operational—you cannot use the steps of building an ordination altar to "operate" the ordination altar of the mind.

But this internal analogy is more accurate than any external one, because Buddhism's own ritual tradition has already made physical and visible the principle that "a protective boundary must be established before practice." More than two thousand years of monastic life have demonstrated this principle every day: any formal practice, any formal ordination, any formal communal cultivation begins with the establishing of a boundary. The four instructions are not a new requirement—they are the internalizing of this ancient ritual, from the external physical altar into the mind of every practitioner.

### The Weight of the Two Words "Their Mind"

Here two corrections of the middle path must be made.

**The four instructions are not premodern moralizing.** The most common misreading is to read the four instructions as traditional ethics—"the Buddha is telling us not to do bad things." This reading misses the point entirely. The four clear and decisive instructions on purity are not ethics; they are **technical specifications for cultivation and realization**. What they address is not "how a good person ought to behave" but "what technical conditions one who wishes to cultivate *samādhi* must satisfy." Not "do not steal because stealing is wrong," but "the laboratory cannot have stray microbes because stray microbes contaminate the experiment." The former is an ethical judgment; the latter is a technical prerequisite—the two are wholly different in nature.

This distinction matters especially to the modern reader, because modern culture has an instinctive resistance to concepts like "precept," "taboo," and "regulation," sensing in them the repressive legacy of an ancient religion. But the four instructions are not that kind of thing. They are more like an athlete's training discipline, a scientist's experimental protocol, a pilot's pre-takeoff checklist—technical prerequisites set up so that one high-precision thing can happen, not shackles that repress human nature.

**Nor are the four instructions a matter of "just don't do the act."** The second misreading is to read "their mind does not X" as a precept of behavior—as long as I do not actually do it, I have not violated it. This reading thoroughly misunderstands the weight of the two words "**their mind**." Zǐxuán says "guarding each individual mental movement inwardly, with all offenses held equally"; Jiāoguāng says "no single thought arising is taken as the precept"—these are not exaggerated demands but precise standards matched to the scene of cultivating *samādhi*. The mind-consciousness in *samādhi* is extremely sensitive; a single arising of a lustful thought triggers a full-body physiological reaction. If you console yourself with the standard of "I did not do the act," you will be unable to recognize that what truly disturbs the meditation is the movement of the mind itself.

### The Three Layers Viewed Together

The three layers of protective boundaries, taken together, are the truly complete preparatory package for the ear-faculty perfect penetration. With any one layer missing, the later work of the ear-faculty perfect penetration does not, even in theory, hold up. And the order of the three layers cannot be exchanged—precept must precede space, and space must precede the purification of the mind.

Outermost layer (precepts): cutting off lust, cutting off killing, cutting off stealing, cutting off gross false speech. A checklist—four items, independent, not to be crossed.

Middle layer (space): the place of practice and the Śūraṅgama *dhāraṇī*. The purifying and empowering of the physical space.

Innermost layer (purification): removing the contributing causes, scooping out the very nature, going against the present karma. The poisoned-honey parable, the ground of dry wisdom, the recursive rhythm of the four preparatory practices.

Once these three layers are built, the true practice formally begins. But once it has formally begun, is one then safe? Not at all.

The fifty skandha-māras—the demon-revealing mirror of fascicles nine and ten—are the fifty kinds of trap one still encounters even inside this safe protective boundary. The boundary keeps out the Māra outside, but it cannot keep out the illusions the mind itself generates in deep *samādhi*. The three characters "love-view Māra" foretold in the fourth instruction are the first gate of the entire structure of the fifty skandha-māras.

Beginning with the next chapter, we step into the first of these fifty mirrors—the ten states of the form-skandha.
