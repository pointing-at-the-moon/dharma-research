## 6.3 The Ten Demonstrations of Seeing—The Seeing-Nature Does Not Perish with Its Objects

### Close Your Eyes

Close your eyes.

What do you see?

Most people answer "nothing." But that answer has already skipped over a fact—**you are seeing**. The very reason you can answer "nothing" is that you looked first, and then judged the content of what you saw to be "nothing at all."

If you are a little more honest, you will say "I see black." But this skips over the same fact—you saw your own eyelids, and then named that visual experience "black." The fabrication of names runs so fast that you never catch it happening: **you see first, then name, then forget that you saw.**

This is the problem the ten demonstrations of seeing set out to address.

### From Demolition to Revelation

In the previous chapter, the Buddha used the seven locations of mind to strip away every disguise Ānanda had thrown over the question "where is the mind?" After all seven were dismantled, the false mind had no disguises left.

But what comes after the demolition? Ānanda now knew the mind was not in any of the seven locations, yet he still did not know what the seeing-nature is. He knew "what it is not"; he had not yet seen "what it is."

The ten demonstrations step into precisely this gap. The Buddha no longer tells you "what it is not." Instead he displays, from ten angles, the very character of the seeing-nature—not by defining it with concepts (that would only become another name), but through ten live demonstrations, so that you yourself can see: **the thing you have been using all along, and forgetting all along, is it.**

The Ming-dynasty master Jiaokuang Zhenjian gave a precise verdict: the seven locations "completely demolish the delusion at the level of consciousness," while the ten demonstrations "mostly reveal the truth, with minor demolition, at the level of the root." The seven locations break things thoroughly at the level of the sixth consciousness; the ten demonstrations, at the level of the "root"—the capacity of seeing, hearing, awareness, and knowing—reveal the truth while still bearing the delusion. The Buddha does not wait until you have become a Buddha before showing you; he lets you see the pointing finger while you are still an ordinary being.

Those four characters—**revealing truth while bearing delusion**—are the method of this whole chapter.

### What Sees Is Not the Eye

The first of the ten demonstrations is extremely brief, yet it sets up the entire architecture—

> The eye can manifest color; thus the seeing-nature is mind, not eye.

A lamp can illuminate colors, but you would never say "the lamp is seeing." The eye can manifest colors, but the act of "seeing" does not belong to the eye—it belongs to mind. The eye-root is only a condition; what is truly "seeing" lies a layer deeper than the eye-root.

This line sets the keynote for all ten demonstrations: **seeing is not a function of the sense organ; it is a function of mind.**

### The Unmoving and the Imperishable

In the second demonstration, the Buddha bends and stretches his five fingers and asks Ānanda what he sees. Ānanda says: I see the Tathāgata's hand opening and closing. The Buddha presses further: is it the hand that opens and closes, or your seeing-nature that opens and closes? Ānanda answers:

> The Buddha's hand does not rest, yet my seeing-nature does not even possess stillness—who could be without rest?

The hand has motion and stillness, but the seeing-nature does not fall within the category of "motion and stillness" at all. The Buddha then shines light to the left and right; Ānanda's head turns left and right to follow it. The Buddha asks again: is it your head that moves, or your seeing-nature that moves? Ānanda answers: the head moves, the seeing-nature does not.

The Buddha then delivers his first cutting verdict:

> From beginning to end, thought after thought arising and perishing, you have lost your true nature. Acting in inversion, you take objects for yourself. Cycling within this, you seize your own transmigration.

You take what moves as yourself, and what changes as what is real—taking objects for yourself—and this is the very mechanism of saṃsāra.

The third demonstration turns to time. The Buddha is in dialogue with King Prasenajit. The king recounts: at twenty I had already declined from ten, at thirty from twenty, and now at sixty-two my body changes in every instant and will finally perish. The Buddha asks: is there anything in your body that does not perish? The king says he does not know. The Buddha uses the water of the Ganges as an analogy—when you saw the Ganges at three, at thirteen, at sixty-two, was the water the same? It was the same. Your face has wrinkled, but does the capacity by which you "see" the Ganges differ between childhood and old age?

> Your face may wrinkle, but this seeing-essence has never wrinkled.

The face wrinkles; the seeing-essence does not. What changes will perish; what does not change has never arisen or perished. This is not an abstract philosophical proposition—it is a pointer you can verify at once. Recall a scene from your childhood right now. The capacity that is "recalling" it, and the capacity by which you were "seeing" in childhood—is there any essential difference between them? Not the content (the content of a memory is of course different), but the act of "being able to see" itself—has it changed?

Here we must give an early inoculation. Reading that the seeing-essence "does not wrinkle, does not move, does not change," the thought that most easily rises is—"Isn't that just the soul? Isn't that just the divine self that the Indian non-Buddhist traditions speak of?"

It is not. The reason appears later, in the seventh demonstration—the seeing-nature is "neither cause nor condition, neither natural nor non-natural." The divine self is a "naturally self-existent" entity—an eternal, independent self. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra explicitly denies this. The seeing-nature is not some eternal thing sitting there unmoving—it is a something to which the very category of "thing" cannot be applied. Hold on to this distinction; we will return to it below.

### The Eight Returns and the Pointing Finger

The fifth demonstration is the most finely structured of the ten, and the sharpest piece of reasoning in the entire Śūraṅgama Sūtra.

The Buddha first offers an analogy—the pointing finger and the moon. This analogy is itself the name of this whole project:

> When a man uses his finger to point at the moon for another, that person should look at the moon by way of the finger; if instead he gazes at the finger and takes it for the moon, he loses not only the moon but the finger as well.

Someone uses a finger to point out the moon to you, and you should follow the finger to the moon. But you fix your eyes on the finger and take the finger for the moon—then not only do you fail to see the moon, you have even mistaken the finger. The Buddha then lists eight phenomenal qualities—brightness, darkness, openness, obstruction, discrimination, blankness, murkiness, clarity—and each has a source it can be "returned" to: brightness is returned to the sun, darkness to the moonless night, openness to doors and windows. Whatever can be returned is not you.

And then comes the sharpest line in the whole sequence of returns:

> Whatever can be returned is naturally not you; that which cannot be returned—if not you, then who?

Whatever can be returned to its source is not your own self. That which cannot be returned, which has nowhere to go—if it is not your true nature, then what is it?

Notice that the Buddha never directly says "that is the seeing-nature." He points through negation—because the moment he says "that is it," you would immediately turn it into an object, into a new "thing," and you would be back at the first location. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra's teaching method throughout is **apophatic** (pointing through negation), never **cataphatic** (defining through affirmation).

Readers familiar with Western philosophy will think here of Husserl's "epoché"—bracketing every presupposition about the external world, one by one, to see what is finally left. The structure of the eight returns is indeed similar. ⚠️ But the two do not arrive at the same place. What Husserl is left with—"pure consciousness"—still points toward some object (consciousness is always "consciousness *of* something"). What the eight returns leave behind—the seeing-nature—does not even retain the "pointing" itself. Husserl stops inside the structure; the Buddha removes the structure too. This comparison can help you see how rigorous the method of the eight returns is, but it does not mean the two are interchangeable.

### When Seeing Sees Seeing

The fourth demonstration contains the hardest line in all ten—and the most pivotal:

> When seeing sees seeing, that seeing is not seeing. Seeing still transcends seeing; seeing cannot reach it.

When you try to use seeing to look at "seeing" itself, what you see is already no longer the seeing-nature. Your seeing cannot catch up with the seeing-nature—because the seeing-nature is the very thing that is doing the chasing.

**You cannot use your eye to look at your own eye. You cannot use a blade to cut its own edge. You cannot use your fingertip to touch the tip of that same finger.**

This is why Ānanda, hearing this line, finds his "confusion only deepening"—the more he hears, the more lost he becomes. Not because the Buddha is unclear, but because Ānanda is trying to use "understanding" to "grasp" the seeing-nature, and "understanding" is itself a name-making operation. You cannot "understand" the seeing-nature; you can only **be** the seeing-nature.

You have been it all along. You only forgot.

### Transcending All Characteristics, Identical with All Dharmas

The seventh and eighth demonstrations are linked together, and they are the doctrinal heart of the entire Śūraṅgama Sūtra. The Buddha presses through the four frameworks of cause, condition, and so on, and arrives at eighteen characters:

> Neither cause nor condition, neither natural nor non-natural; neither non-non nor not-non; neither is nor is-not. It transcends all characteristics, and is identical with all dharmas.

The seeing-nature is neither produced by causes and conditions (or it would perish along with them), nor a self-existent natural entity (or it could manifest only one fixed appearance, and could not behold a thousand differences)—and then, going further, even the negations "not caused" and "not natural" must be set down. Leaving every characteristic that could describe it, it is at the same time within the very nature of all dharmas.

Master Zixuan gave an exquisitely precise gloss—both medicine and illness forgotten. Cause-and-condition, naturalness, is, is-not: these four are the illness (the false frameworks of ordinary beings and non-Buddhist traditions). Not-caused, not-natural, not-is, not-is-not: these four are the medicine (the negations that treat the illness). But both medicine and illness must be forgotten—**even the medicine must be set down**. To keep the medicine is to keep another layer of clinging.

A different misreading easily rises here: "transcending all characteristics"—doesn't that mean there is nothing at all? That falls into nihilism. But the scripture does not stop at "transcends all characteristics"—it immediately adds "and is identical with all dharmas." To leave the characteristics is not to be left with nothing; it is to be **within all dharmas**. Zixuan points precisely: "The refined awakening, wondrous and luminous, does not have a separate substance—it is simply that, among all dharmas, when the previous false pervading attachment is abandoned, this is itself the perfectly accomplished nature of wondrous awakened luminosity." The seeing-nature is not a separate thing standing outside all dharmas; it is right within them—it appears the moment the dharmas shed their false projection.

Try another image—you wear tinted glasses to look at the world, and everything takes on that tint. "Transcending all characteristics" = taking off the glasses. After you take them off, what you see is not "nothing"; it is the world as it has always been. The world has not changed; only your filter is gone.

### Turn Objects, and You Are the Same as the Tathāgata

In the tenth demonstration, the Buddha begins from the most everyday opposition of all—large and small. Ānanda asks: when I gaze up at the open sky, the seeing-nature fills the whole world; when I return to my quarters, I see only the walls of the room—has this seeing-nature shrunk from large to small, or has it been cut off by the walls?

The Buddha uses the analogy of space inside a square vessel and a round vessel—space is neither square nor round; it is the squareness and roundness of the vessels that make you suppose space has a shape. And then:

> All sentient beings from beginningless time have mistaken themselves for objects, lost their original mind, and are turned by objects. Hence they perceive large and small. If you could turn objects rather than being turned by them, you would be the same as the Tathāgata—body and mind perfectly luminous, the unmoving sanctuary, able to contain the ten directions of lands upon a single hair-tip.

Large and small are not properties of the seeing-nature; they are the result of your being turned by objects. If you can turn objects rather than be turned by them—a single hair-tip can contain the lands of the ten directions.

### The Sixth Patriarch's Five Lines of Corroboration

There is a beautiful resonance between the ten demonstrations of the Śūraṅgama and the Chan school. The Sixth Patriarch, Huineng, heard the line from the Diamond Sūtra—"generate the mind that abides nowhere"—and awakened greatly, uttering five lines:

> How unexpected—the self-nature is originally pure of itself; how unexpected—the self-nature is originally without arising or perishing; how unexpected—the self-nature is originally self-sufficient; how unexpected—the self-nature is originally without wavering; how unexpected—the self-nature can generate the myriad dharmas.

"How unexpected" means "I never imagined"—so it turns out the self-nature has always been just this. These five lines line up exactly against the ten demonstrations: originally pure of itself (the sixth demonstration, non-mixture), originally without arising or perishing (the third, imperishability), originally self-sufficient (the fifth, nothing to return), originally without wavering (the second, immovability), able to generate the myriad dharmas (the eighth, neither caused nor natural).

The Buddha unfolds it through the logic of ten demonstrations; the Sixth Patriarch compresses it into the direct intuition of five lines—they point at the same one thing. The Śūraṅgama is the teaching; the Platform Sūtra is the corroboration.

### Revealing Truth While Bearing Delusion

Return to Jiaokuang's four characters—revealing truth while bearing delusion.

The ten demonstrations of seeing are not describing the pure realm of one who has already awakened. They are displayed in front of Ānanda—an ordinary being still in the midst of confusion. The Buddha knows Ānanda still has delusion not yet purged, but he does not withhold the display on that account. **Bearing the delusion, he reveals the truth.**

This carries one crucial implication: **you do not need to become a sage first in order to see the pointing finger toward the seeing-nature.** Close your eyes and ask yourself "what do I see?"—you can do this right now, with no prerequisite of practice. You forgot that you are seeing—this fact, you can become aware of right now.

But at the same time—becoming aware is not the same as realizing. Right now you can understand, at the level of words, that "the face wrinkles, the seeing-essence does not"; but this understanding is not the same as having personally realized the seeing-nature's freedom from arising and perishing. The "bearing delusion" in "revealing truth while bearing delusion" is not a figure of speech; it is the actual situation—while you look, your delusory grasping is still at work. You can see the direction, but you are still on the road.

### Why Ānanda Wept

After the ten demonstrations ended, Ānanda did something unexpected. He did not say "now I understand." He wept—"weeping in grief, he prostrated."

He had heard every word. He had followed the logic of each of the Buddha's demonstrations. But his "mind was still not opened"—he knew that he had understood, and he also knew that he had not truly understood. The Buddha then said something very weighty:

> Though you have powerful memory, you merely benefit your extensive learning. Regarding the subtle, intimate observation of śamatha, your mind is still not clear.

Your memory is excellent, you have heard a great deal, but toward true stillness-and-insight your mind has still not opened.

This is exactly the thread that recurs throughout this book—knowing and realizing are two different things. You finish this chapter, you grasp the logic of "when seeing sees seeing, seeing is not seeing," and you feel "that makes sense"—this is knowing. But the next time you look in the mirror and see a wrinkle, do you truly become aware: the one that is "seeing the wrinkle" did not change along with the wrinkle? That is the starting point of realizing.

What this book can offer is the path of knowing. The path of realizing lies in your own daily life. The Śūraṅgama Sūtra is a practitioner's diagnostic manual, not a substitute for practice.

So, one last time—

Close your eyes.

What do you see?

If your answer this time is different from before you read this chapter—then this chapter has done its work.

The next chapter enters the four categories and seven elements. The Buddha will no longer display only the unchanging nature of "seeing"; he will gather the five aggregates, the twelve sense-bases, the eighteen elements, and the seven great elements all into the tathāgatagarbha, the wondrous true nature of suchness. From the single point of the seeing-nature, expanding to the totality of all dharmas.
