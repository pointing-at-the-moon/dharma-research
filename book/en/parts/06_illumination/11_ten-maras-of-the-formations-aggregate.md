## 6.11 The Ten Māras of the Formations Aggregate — The Mirror-Student and the Net of the *Brahmajāla*

### The Fisherman's Fine-Meshed Net

On some evening in the fifth century BCE, in a bamboo grove in the land of Magadha, the Buddha said a few words to the assembly of monks.

These words were preserved as the twenty-first sūtra of the *Long Āgama*, and the title eventually fixed for it was *Brahmajāla* — meaning that when the Buddha spoke this sūtra, he shook the whole doctrine of a creator-Brahmā, shook the entire metaphysical apparatus of every Indian school of his day.

In the sūtra, the Buddha laid out in detail the sixty-two views on the nature of "self" and "world" then circulating among the wandering ascetics and the brahmins. Eighteen of them were inferences about the past (views concerning the prior aeon); forty-four were inferences about the future (views concerning the latter aeon). Each had its own meditative background, its own form of inference, its own final conclusion. Sixty-two positions in all.

Then the Buddha gave a simile:

> Whatever views any ascetics or brahmins hold concerning the prior aeon and the latter aeon — innumerable, of every kind, asserted as they please — all enter into these sixty-two views… Just as a skilled fisherman covers a small pond with a fine-meshed net, you should know that all the water-creatures of the pond enter into the net, with no place to escape, and not one passes beyond.

**A fine-meshed net covering a small pond** — six words.

The sharpness of this simile lies not in how vivid it is but in its *exhaustiveness*. The Buddha did not say "beyond the sixty-two views, more views may yet be possible." He said "all enter into the net, with no place to escape." The mesh is fine enough that not one fish can slip through. That is: any effort to make a thoroughgoing verdict on "the nature of self and world" will, in the end, fall into one of these sixty-two types. Not because human imagination is poor, but because **the task itself has only sixty-two structural possibilities.**

At the close of the sūtra, Ānanda asked the Buddha: this Dharma is so profound — by what name should we call it? The Buddha gave five names — **the Moving of Meaning, the Moving of Dharma, the Moving of Views, the Moving of Māra, the Moving of Brahmā.** It shakes every doctrinal meaning, every dharma-mark, every standpoint, every māric event, every doctrine of a creator-Brahmā.

This is the opening scene from the Āgama. Hold this net in mind. Seven centuries later it will appear again in fascicle 10 of the Śūraṅgama Sūtra — not one word changed, not one position missing.

### Seven Centuries Later, the Same Scene Replayed

This is how fascicle 10 of the Śūraṅgama opens its account of the formations-aggregate stage:

> Ānanda, you should know that, in this *śamatha* of right knowing, the noble men, with mind concentrated, luminous, and upright, **are not given their opportunity by the ten classes of celestial māra**. They proceed to the depths of investigation, exhausting the original source of all classes of beings; within the original source of their own class, when the source-marker is laid bare, they observe that obscure-pure perfectly-disturbing source — and from within this perfect source they begin to make calculative reckonings, and such a person falls into the two no-cause doctrines…

Notice the staircase in the first half — right knowing, *śamatha*, mind concentrated and luminous and upright, **the ten classes of celestial māra are not given their opportunity**, deepening investigation, exhausting the source of all classes of beings, observing that obscure-pure perfectly-disturbing source. By this point the practitioner has entered a region that no external māra of the prior three aggregates can reach. The luminous visions of the form-aggregate stage have left the stage; the misjudged sensations of the receptive-aggregate stage have left; the dispatched essence possessing a person from the perception-aggregate stage has left too. **The ten classes of celestial māra are all denied their opportunity.**

In the previous chapter we said that at the moment the perception aggregate is exhausted, the māra has no form left. This line of sūtra is the portrait of that very moment — the celestial māra can find no opening.

But it is precisely at this position that three characters appear: "**begins to make calculative reckonings**."

And the very next line is: "such a person falls into the two no-cause doctrines."

Between "the ten classes of celestial māra are not given their opportunity" and "falls into the two no-cause doctrines," there is no external intermediary. No dispatched essence, no possessed person, no mirror-other ascending the seat to expound the dharma. There is only one act of the practitioner's own — **calculative reckoning.**

This is the structural pivot between 6.10 and 6.11. The māra no longer comes from outside. From where, then? From the practitioner's own act of observation.

The Śūraṅgama then unfolds the ten māras in sequence: first, the two no-cause doctrines; second, the four pervasive-permanence doctrines; third, the four partial-permanence doctrines; fourth, the four finite-and-infinite doctrines; fifth, the four perverted, immortality-equivocating, totally-fabricated doctrines; sixth, the sixteen having-form doctrines; seventh, the eight no-form doctrines; eighth, the eight neither doctrines; ninth, the seven annihilationist doctrines; tenth, the five present-nirvāṇa doctrines.

Set this list beside the sixty-two views of the *Brahmajāla Sūtra*:

| Śūraṅgama: ten formations-aggregate māras | *Brahmajāla*: parallel | Sub-count |
|---|---|---|
| Two no-cause doctrines | Two no-cause doctrines | 2 |
| Four pervasive-permanence doctrines | Four pervasive-permanence doctrines | 4 |
| Four partial-permanence doctrines | Four partial-permanence doctrines | 4 |
| Four finite/infinite doctrines | Four finite-and-infinite doctrines | 4 |
| Four equivocation doctrines | Four immortality-equivocating doctrines | 4 |
| Sixteen having-form doctrines | Sixteen having-perception doctrines | 16 |
| Eight no-form doctrines | Eight no-perception doctrines | 8 |
| Eight neither doctrines | Eight neither-perception-nor-no-perception doctrines | 8 |
| Seven annihilationist doctrines | Seven annihilationist doctrines | 7 |
| Five present-nirvāṇa doctrines | Five present-life nirvāṇa doctrines | 5 |

Add the sub-counts: 2+4+4+4+4+16+8+8+7+5 = **62**.

Order, structure, and manner of naming run almost completely in parallel.

This correspondence cannot be coincidence. The only explanation is this: **these two sūtras are not describing two different things but a single thing — when a meditator tries to make a thoroughgoing verdict on the nature of the formations aggregate, the positions structurally available to him are exhaustive, and there are only sixty-two.** The Buddha in the *Brahmajāla* described these sixty-two positions in the idiom of the early canon; the Śūraṅgama in fascicle 10 unfolded the very same sixty-two positions in the idiom of Mahāyāna meditation, and adjudicated them as "ten dhyāna mad-interpretations of the formations aggregate."

This is the central thesis the present chapter will unfold: **the ten formations-aggregate māras are the dhyāna repetition of the sixty-two views. They are not ten independent errors but a single net long since woven — cast by the Buddha in a bamboo grove seven hundred years before, and dropped again in fascicle 10 seven hundred years later.**

### The Mirror-Other Exits, the Mirror-Student Enters

The core structure of the previous chapter was the **mirror-other**: the māra appearing under the form of an external spiritual friend, putting on a display of the craved object through "dispatching an essence to possess a person." The māra borrowed an ordinary person's body to expound the dharma, and the practitioner, drawn in by the possessed person ascending the seat to teach, walked into errant view. The direction of the cure was "discern the mirror" — to use the sūtra-teaching, the precepts, and true spiritual friends as a standard of comparison and see through the counterfeit.

At the formations-aggregate stage, this structure no longer applies. Why? Because **the object of the formations-aggregate stage is no longer "an external capacity" but "the reading of one's own formations-flow."** No external person can perform this reading on the practitioner's behalf — only the practitioner himself can. So the mirror-other exits the stage.

But this does not mean the "mirror" disappears. The mirror persists in an entirely different form — **the practitioner himself becomes that mirror.**

Concretely: when the practitioner observes his own formations-flow and then renders some verdict on that flow ("this is permanent," "this is annihilated," "this is finite," "this is infinite"…), the verdict has an extraordinarily subtle structure — **the "I" who renders the verdict and the "formations aggregate" that is verdicted-upon are, phenomenologically, the same.** Why? Because the "I" who renders the verdict is constituted by a thought-after-thought-flowing act of awareness, and that thought-after-thought-flowing act of awareness is exactly what the formations aggregate is. **The subject of the verdict is the object of the verdict** — only, in the moment the verdict is rendered, the practitioner is unaware of this identity.

This is the precise structure of the "mirror-student." The practitioner believes he is "watching the formations aggregate" (just as the perception-aggregate practitioner believed he was "watching an external spiritual friend"), but in fact what he is watching is himself. He has objectified his own act of awareness *as* "the formations aggregate," rendered some verdict on that objectified result, and then taken the verdict for "having seen." The whole process is completed entirely within his own interior — no external person needs to take part. **He is at once the teacher and the student; at once the observer and the observed; at once the one who expounds the dharma and the one who hears it.** Every "mad interpretation" is something he says to himself — but because he cannot recognize this identity, he believes that what he hears is "the truth."

Set the two structures side by side and it becomes clear:

| Dimension | 6.10 Perception · Mirror-Other | 6.11 Formations · Mirror-Student |
|---|---|---|
| Mirror object | External spiritual friend (strange ghosts, sovereign heavenly māra, etc.) | The practitioner's own act of observation |
| Mirror form | Dispatched essence possessing a person; concrete form | The mind's reading of its perceived objects; no form |
| Mediation needed | Must have a "possessed person" | No intermediary; the practitioner is the entirety |
| Mode of detection | Compare against sūtra-teaching, precepts, true spiritual friends | The comparison-tool no longer functions |
| Source of danger | Being deceived by another person | Being deceived by oneself |
| Direction of cure | Find the true spiritual friend (seek the great teacher) | Render no thoroughgoing verdict (non-grasping is essential) |

The last row is the most crucial. The previous chapter's cure was still within the forward Āgama structure of "for the sake of severing the three cravings, seek a great teacher" — one only had to detect the "mirror-master" as counterfeit. But this chapter's cure cannot be "find a better teacher." **The root of the problem is not "having found the wrong teacher" but the very act of "rendering a thoroughgoing verdict on the perceived object."** Even if the practitioner found the very best spiritual friend, so long as he kept rendering thoroughgoing verdicts on his own meditative state he would still fall into the ten māras — only the specific content of the ten māras would take a different shape.

In his commentary on fascicle 10, Zǐxuán breaks open this transition in a single line:

> While the perception aggregate is not yet exhausted, external māras are still drawn in. Now that the perception aggregate is exhausted and the formations aggregate is laid bare, [the practitioner] generates errant views regarding his perceived objects, grasping these as right — **and so we speak of mind-māra.**

"**And so we speak of mind-māra**" — five words. From here on, the referent of the word "māra" has changed. It is no longer a disturber of external objects but the mind's very reading of its perceived objects. Jiāoguāng Zhēnjiàn renders the same distinction from another angle:

> Earlier the text spoke of *dhyāna manifesting objects* — these are cases where the celestial māra watches for his chance. Here it speaks of *dhyāna mad-interpretations* — these are cases where **the mind-māra arises of itself, generating its own deep calamity.**

"Manifesting objects" versus "mad interpretations." The former is the surfacing of an object (the common form of the form, receptive, and perception aggregates); the latter is the act of interpretation (the distinctive form of the formations aggregate). **What is mad is not the "object," what is mad is the "interpretation"** — what is mad is the very act of "rendering a thoroughgoing verdict on the object."

### Obscure Concealment — What the Formations Aggregate Is

If the formations aggregate is "the ceaselessly flowing disturbance at the threshold of observation," then what exactly does this thing look like? The Śūraṅgama offers a simile:

> They see the common ground of generation, like wild horses [a mirage], shimmering and pure-disturbing, the ultimate hinge-and-socket of the floating sense-faculties and sense-objects.

**Wild horses** — not the animal here. "Wild horses" is the *marīci*, the shimmering mirage of heated air seen in the dust at a distance. It has two features: first, it is there — you can see the shimmer far off; second, when you walk over to grasp it, it disappears.

The formations aggregate has exactly the same double character: **it is there** (the practitioner can sense a subtle disturbing-flow), **but the moment you try to objectify it, your very act of objectification has already turned it into something else** — turned it into a "thought," a "sensation," a "form," that is, into one of the objects of the prior three aggregates, no longer the formations aggregate itself.

This is the definition the Śūraṅgama gives the formations aggregate — **the false thought of obscure concealment.**

The root of the form aggregate is the "false thought of solidity" (the congealed sense of matter); the root of the receptive aggregate is the "false thought of vacuous brightness" (the receiving structure of sensation); the root of the perception aggregate is the "false thought of fusion" (the mind's power to fuse and transform reality); and the root of the formations aggregate is **the false thought of obscure concealment.**

The two characters "obscure concealment" deserve to be seen carefully. They do not mean "darkness," do not mean "non-existence," do not mean "invisible." Jiāoguāng gives two exquisitely subtle glosses:

> The formations aggregate moves stealthily, never noticed; hence we say *obscure concealment*.

**Moves stealthily** — an extremely subtle displacement. **Never noticed** — not captured by any ordinary act of awareness. The two characters "obscure concealment" mean: **it is right there, always in motion, but its manner of motion is such that it cannot be objectified by "seeing" in the ordinary sense.**

Jiāoguāng also gives a water simile for the five aggregates that makes this position still clearer:

> The form aggregate is like the foam at the shore, the receptive aggregate like the swelling of the wave, the perception aggregate like the great wave, **the formations aggregate like a fine ripple**, the consciousness aggregate like flowing water without waves. The body of true awareness should be like still and motionless water.

Different levels of waves. Form, receptive, and perception are all "visible waves." The formations aggregate is the "fine ripple" — the wave is still there, but its amplitude has shrunk almost to the boundary of the observation threshold. The consciousness aggregate is "flowing water without waves" — the water still flows, but its surface no longer shows a visible wave. True awareness is "still and motionless water."

**The signature of the formations aggregate is that it operates near the threshold of observation.** It sits exactly on the boundary between "can be seen" and "cannot be seen." Any verdict-rendering act that tries to nail it down founders on its ceaseless flow — but because the foundering happens near the threshold, the practitioner all too readily misreads the foundering as "seeing."

This is the phenomenological mechanism of the "mad interpretation."

And this directly determines the form of every māric event at the formations-aggregate stage. In the prior three aggregates the māra can appear as an "object" (luminous visions, misjudged sensations, the possessed mirror-other) — because the prior three aggregates are themselves the level of objectification. At the formations-aggregate stage the māra cannot appear as an object, because the formations aggregate itself cannot be objectified. **What, then, does the māra become?**

The māra becomes "the manner in which the act of observation reads the formations aggregate."

Every "mad interpretation" is one specific manner of reading — pinning this non-objectifiable flowing thing with some thoroughgoing conceptual framework, then taking the pinned result for "having seen." Permanence, annihilation, having-form, no-form, neither, annihilation, present-nirvāṇa — each is one specific way the act of observation tries to pin the formations aggregate down, and the result of each pinning is not the formations aggregate itself but **the shape pinned.**

### Errant Wisdom Suddenly Arises — Not Contagion, but Structural Necessity

Return to a seemingly simple question: **why do the specific positions of the ten formations-aggregate māras correspond, almost one to one, with the names of the Indian non-Buddhist schools — primal-*prakṛti*, naturalism, eternalism, annihilationism?**

A common misreading goes like this: the Śūraṅgama is "describing the appearance of Indian non-Buddhist views in meditation"; it is a work of refutation aimed specifically at the brahmin and ascetic systems surrounding the Buddha's age. This misreading turns fascicle 10 into a "blacklist of non-Buddhist schools" — beware the primal-*prakṛti* school, beware the naturalists, beware the annihilationists.

But Zǐxuán, in his commentary on fascicle 10, overturns this reading completely in a single line:

> The doctrines of the non-Buddhist schools come into being on this account: **all of these arise when, the practitioner having reached this point, errant wisdom suddenly arises — this is named the bursting forth of view; it is not that there are originally separate non-Buddhist schools.**

"**It is not that there are originally separate non-Buddhist schools**" — seven words.

Zǐxuán states it plainly: these views are not "a crowd of non-Buddhist schools out there, which the meditator then goes to study"; they are "the practitioner, at a particular stage, having *errant wisdom suddenly arise*, and the form of that errant wisdom happens to be the form of the non-Buddhist schools." The word "non-Buddhist school" here does not point to a group of historical figures; **it points to a set of structural view-positions** — and the meditator at the formations-aggregate stage, when errant wisdom suddenly arises, falls naturally into one of these structural positions.

This adjudication reverses the traditional cause and effect. It is not "the non-Buddhist schools come first, and the meditator catches the infection"; it is "**there are sixty-two structures by which the nature of the formations aggregate can be misjudged**; the Indian non-Buddhist schools happen to be the historical concretions of those sixty-two structures; the meditator's entry into one of them is *errant wisdom suddenly arising*, not *being infected*."

This explains why the Śūraṅgama and the *Brahmajāla* — across seven centuries, across two entirely different teaching idioms — can nevertheless correspond almost completely in structure. It is not that the Śūraṅgama plagiarized the *Brahmajāla*; **it is that both sūtras describe the same structural trap, only unfolded again in the language of a different era.**

So why exactly sixty-two? Is there a structural necessity to the number?

There is. Jiāoguāng gives an exquisitely precise summary:

> Discussed in general, the ten errant interpretations **do not exit the four characters: annihilation, permanence, empty, existent.** The first five belong to annihilation-permanence; the latter five belong to empty-existent.

"**Annihilation, permanence, empty, existent**" — four characters. Any effort to render a thoroughgoing verdict on the formations aggregate, that thought-after-thought-flowing thing, will in the end land on some combination of these four conceptual positions:

- To say outright that it is "permanent" — the second māra, four pervasive-permanence doctrines.
- To say outright that it is "annihilated" — the first māra, two no-cause doctrines; the ninth māra, seven annihilationist doctrines.
- To say it is partly permanent and partly impermanent — the third māra, four partial-permanence doctrines.
- To say it has a boundary — the fourth māra, four finite-and-infinite doctrines.
- To evade the act of verdict-rendering itself — the fifth māra, four perverted immortality-equivocating doctrines.
- To say there is some "existent" state after death — the sixth māra, sixteen having-form doctrines.
- To say there is "nothing" after death — the seventh māra, eight no-form doctrines.
- To say that after death it is "neither existent nor non-existent" — the eighth māra, eight neither doctrines (and note the Śūraṅgama's adjudication of this māra: "the latter limit is dim and confused, with nothing that can be said" — **not the lucid double-negation of the Middle Way, but a confused, fogged double-negation**).
- To say the present moment is already ultimate — the tenth māra, five present-nirvāṇa doctrines.

The first five māras handle "temporality" — whether the flowing thing persists in time. The latter five handle "real-existence" — whether the flowing thing still exists once time has run out. Within each group the positions are arranged symmetrically around the axis of "both / neither." Multiply each conceptual position by its internal variables (form, receptive, perception, formations; having-form, no-form, having-perception, no-perception; finite, infinite; bliss, suffering; and so on) and you obtain the specific sub-counts 2, 4, 4, 4, 4, 16, 8, 8, 7, 5.

**Sixty-two is not a historical accident; it is a structural exhaustion.** Anyone who goes deep enough in meditation to reach the formations-aggregate position and there tries to render a thoroughgoing verdict on what he sees — whatever his tradition, whatever his language, whatever his historical era — will fall into this net. When the *Brahmajāla*'s fisherman simile says "all enter into the net, with no place to escape," it is not exaggerating; it is stating a structure.

The meaning of this for actual practice is: **the warning "do not study such-and-such non-Buddhist school" is superficial; the real warning is "do not render any thoroughgoing verdict on the nature of the formations aggregate."** For the moment you render the verdict — whatever conceptual framework, whatever teaching idiom, whatever era's terminology you use — your verdict will land in one of these sixty-two positions. Unless you do not render it at all.

### The Tenth Māra — Taking the Present for Nirvāṇa

Of the ten māras, the tenth must be treated on its own.

The errors of the first nine māras share one feature — they all render thoroughgoing verdicts on "something not present." The two no-cause doctrines infer about the past, the four pervasive-permanence doctrines infer about persistence through time, the seven annihilationist doctrines infer about future destruction. The objects of these verdicts are not directly present here and now, so the root of the error is "the leap of inference" — leaping from some present meditative phenomenon to a thoroughgoing assertion about an absent thing.

The tenth māra is not like this. The Śūraṅgama's description:

> Such a person falls into the five present-nirvāṇa doctrines: some take the desire realm as the right transference of basis, observing its luminous fullness and giving rise to attached longing; some take the first dhyāna, because its nature is without sorrow; some take the second dhyāna, because its mind is without suffering; some take the third dhyāna, because of its supreme delight; some take the fourth dhyāna, because suffering and pleasure both depart and one no longer receives the nature of *saṃsāric* arising-and-ceasing. **He confuses a contaminated heaven with the unconditioned**, taking these five places of safety as the supreme pure refuge.

The eight characters "**confuses a contaminated heaven with the unconditioned**" name the structure of the tenth māra precisely. "Contaminated heaven" refers to the desire realm and the four dhyānas of the form realm — all still contaminated states within *saṃsāra*. "Unconditioned" refers to true nirvāṇa — that which transcends *saṃsāra*, uncompounded by causes and conditions. The error of the tenth māra is not a faulty inference about something "not present"; it is **taking the present, still-*saṃsāric*, genuinely-real meditative state and directly identifying it as ultimate liberation.**

Why is the tenth māra especially dangerous? Because **it is the closest to genuine realization.**

The errors of the first nine māras are relatively easy to detect. They involve inferences about "what is not present," which can be checked by other means — comparison with the sūtras, examination by logic, subsequent verification in practice. To say "after death there is nothing at all" can at least be questioned logically; to say "the creator-Brahmā is undying" can at least be refuted by dependent arising.

But the error of the tenth māra lies in the fact that the practitioner is *genuinely* in some real meditative state. The joy of the first dhyāna, the bliss of the second, the marvelous bliss of the third, the equanimous-thought purity of the fourth — none of these are hallucinations; all are real meditative states. The error is not in the state itself. **The error is in mistaking a real, profound, but not-yet-ultimate state for the ultimate.**

This is the deepest form of the "mirror-student" structure. Here the mirror is no spirit-creature, no non-Buddhist view; **it is your own present, genuine meditative state — its identity misjudged by yourself.** You have not yet arrived home, but you feel you have already arrived. And this "feeling that you have already arrived" is itself supported by a real meditative state — so you will not doubt yourself.

This is why the tenth māra is placed last. It is the position among the ten that is only one step from genuine realization. And the missing step is not the depth of the state but **the reading of the state** — whether it is "taken as ultimate" or "known to be not-yet-ultimate."

The tenth māra is also the bridge from the formations-aggregate stage to the consciousness-aggregate stage. The ten māras of the consciousness-aggregate stage in the next chapter (6.12) — grasping the cause as a non-cause, grasping the agent as non-agent, grasping the permanent as non-permanent, grasping the knowing as non-knowing, grasping the arising as non-arising, grasping the returning as non-returning, grasping the craving as non-craving, grasping the true as non-true, fixed-nature *śrāvaka*, fixed-nature *pratyekabuddha* — share a structure deeply isomorphic with the tenth māra: each is "generating a verdict of full attainment at a not-yet-complete position" (Jiāoguāng's phrase). The shift is only from "taking the meditative state for nirvāṇa" to a finer "taking the subtle object of the consciousness aggregate for the ultimate." The "mirror-student" structure has one last layer of refinement waiting at the consciousness-aggregate stage.

### Why It Is Harder Than the Mirror-Master — Three Structural Reasons

Return to the question planted at the opening of this chapter: if the māra no longer comes from outside, why is the danger nevertheless greater?

The positive answer has three layers.

**The first reason: every comparison-tool fails.**

To detect the "mirror-master," one must have some comparison-tool to serve as a standard. Compare against the sūtra-teaching (does what this person says match the Buddha's word?), compare against the precepts (does this person's conduct conform to the precepts?), compare against true spiritual friends (do other true friends accept this person?). Comparison is possible because "the object to be detected" and "the tool used to detect it" are two different things — the mirror-master is outside, the comparison-tool is in your hand.

But for detecting the "mirror-student," no such comparison is available. Why? Because **anything one tries to deploy as a comparison-tool is itself still operating within the formations aggregate.**

When the practitioner tries to compare against the sūtra-teaching, his memory of the sūtra-teaching, his understanding, his interpretation — all are part of the formations-flow. When he tries to compare against the precepts, his judgment in applying the precepts is part of the formations-flow. When he tries to compare against true spiritual friends, his recognition of "who is a true spiritual friend" is still part of the formations-flow. **No comparison-tool can step outside the formations aggregate.** Every tool is inside it. Comparison fails.

**The second reason: the reflexive entanglement of the act of observation.**

To detect anything, one must first observe it. But the special character of the formations-aggregate stage is this: **the act of observing the formations aggregate is itself the formations aggregate.** When the practitioner tries to "see" his own awareness-flow, the "eye" he sees with is itself part of the awareness-flow. This is a structural deadlock — **the observer cannot observe the totality of his own act of observation**, because the act of observation cannot be objectified by itself.

The Śūraṅgama uses a special phrase to describe this deadlock — "**the mind-use intertwining of the formations aggregate**." "Mind-use" is the act of observation; "intertwining" is the reflexive entanglement between the act of observation and the observed object. **It is precisely this entanglement that produces the "mad interpretation."** The practitioner believes he has "seen the formations aggregate clearly," but in fact what he has seen is the specific shaping his act of observation has imposed upon the formations aggregate. Each "mad interpretation" is a specific shape of the act of observation — permanence, annihilation, having-form, no-form, neither, annihilation, present-nirvāṇa — **each is a particular way the act of observation tries to pin the formations aggregate down, and the result of each pinning is not the formations aggregate itself but the shape pinned.**

**The third reason: the irreversibility of the error.**

The error of the "mirror-master" can be corrected — you discover you have followed the wrong teacher, you can leave, you can find another teacher, you can begin again. The error is "directional"; it can be turned around.

The error of the "mirror-student" is "structural" — **once you have rendered some thoroughgoing verdict on your own meditative state and taken it for true, that verdict becomes the implicit framework of all your subsequent observation.** Every later observation is conducted under this implicit framework, and the implicit framework itself is never questioned by those observations — because no observation questions the framework on which it itself depends.

The annihilationist view is not overturned by further observation, because once the practitioner holds the annihilationist view, all his subsequent observations automatically confirm it; the eternalist view, the "five present-nirvāṇa" view, and the rest are the same. **Once the error has occurred, it becomes the formal condition of observation itself, and there is no room left for "beginning again."**

This is why the Śūraṅgama's warning about the ten formations-aggregate māras is so grave in its wording — "completing the great false speech, falling into the Avīci hell." Not because the Śūraṅgama is threatening the practitioner (the Śūraṅgama never uses threats as instruction), but because it is describing a structural fact. Once the "mad interpretation" of the formations-aggregate stage is taken for true, the practitioner has entered a closed loop that admits no self-correction. "**Avīci hell**" is not an external punishment but the structural name for that closed loop itself — a structure with no gap, no exit, no margin within which correction might be possible.

In other words: the danger of the perception-aggregate stage is "finding the wrong teacher," and you can change teachers; the danger of the formations-aggregate stage is "becoming the wrong teacher yourself" — and you cannot change out of yourself.

### Form No Saintly Interpretation — The Only Direction of the Cure

If the structural cause of the ten formations-aggregate māras is the very act of "rendering a thoroughgoing verdict on the nature of the formations aggregate," then the direction of the cure can only be one — **stop this act.**

But the word "stop" needs to be unfolded with great care. It is all too easily misread as "do not think," "enter a state of non-discrimination," "empty out the mind." None of these is what the Śūraṅgama means. None of these is the cure for the ten formations-aggregate māras — **these are themselves just new thoroughgoing verdicts, only pointed in a different direction.**

The positive unfolding must begin from the closing line of the *Brahmajāla Sūtra*. After the Buddha had laid out the sixty-two views one by one and spread the fisherman's net, he said:

> If a *bhikṣu* with respect to the six contacts **knows, as it actually is, their arising, ceasing, savor, peril, and emancipation**, this is supreme, and goes beyond all those views.

Look closely at the form of this line. It is not giving a sixty-third correct view. It does not say "you should hold the *neither* of the Middle Way," nor "you should hold the eternalist view," nor "you should hold the annihilationist view." It does something entirely different — **it replaces the object of observation.**

No longer observe "the nature of the formations aggregate" (that flow which cannot be objectified), but observe "the actual flow of the six contacts" (the concrete events of sense-faculty, object, and consciousness meeting). The six contacts can be known as they actually are, because they have concrete boundaries (this faculty meets this object, this contact arises). The formations aggregate cannot be known as it actually is, because it has no boundary. **The strategy of the cure is to shift observation from the non-objectifiable position to the objectifiable position.**

And what is asked is only **five verb-like kinds of observation** — arising (how it arises), ceasing (how it subsides), savor (its present quality), peril (the dangers it brings), and emancipation (the way out of it). All five describe the flow; **not one is an assertion about the nature.**

The Śūraṅgama gives this principle an exquisitely matched name — "**form no sagely realization**" (or "form no saintly mind"). In every section of warning throughout the fifty skandha-māras, these few characters recur:

> If you form a sagely realization, you receive the host of errancies; if you form no sagely realization, this is named a wholesome state.

The structure of this line is completely isomorphic with the *Brahmajāla*'s cure-formula — "form no sagely realization" is "render no thoroughgoing verdict on perceived objects"; "this is named a wholesome state" is letting the state remain in its flowing quality without being shaped into an object.

Concretely at the formations-aggregate stage, "form no sagely realization" means: when the practitioner sees the thought "self and world are permanent" surfacing, he does not adjudicate it as true or false — he only sees it surfacing; when he sees the thought "annihilation" surfacing, he does not adjudicate it as right or wrong — he only sees it surfacing; when he sees the feeling "five present-nirvāṇa" surfacing, he does not adjudicate whether this is ultimate — he only sees it surfacing. **The seed of every "mad interpretation" is a surfacing thought or feeling. The key to the cure is not "supplying the correct verdict" but "not adjudicating, in the very moment of surfacing."**

The deepest aspect of this cure-principle is that **it dissolves the very position of "the correct verdict."**

It does not say "you should hold the *neither* of Madhyamaka," nor "you should hold the dependent-arising of Yogācāra," nor "you should hold the true-permanence of the tathāgatagarbha." It says "render no thoroughgoing verdict whatever." **Right view is not some correct content; it is the very capacity of "rendering no thoroughgoing verdict on perceived objects."** The *Madhyama Āgama Sacred Path Sūtra* gives an extraordinarily precise definition of right view — "if one sees a wrong view as a wrong view, this is called right view; and if one sees a right view as a right view, this also is called right view." Note the doubleness of this definition: it does not define right view as "holding some correct proposition," but as the very capacity "to discern, in the present, whether the view one is now holding is wrong or right." This is the Āgama root of "form no sagely realization."

From 6.10 to 6.11, the cure-formula has traced a complete arc.

The cure of 6.10 was the Āgama's "**for the sake of severing the three cravings, seek a great teacher**" — proceed forward along the chain of true spiritual friends, discern the mirror, find the true spiritual friend.
The cure of 6.11 is the Āgama's "**know the six contacts as they actually are, and go beyond all those views**" — seek no reading-position at all, and turn instead to observe the flow of the six contacts itself.

The former is the entire purport of the perception-aggregate stage; the latter is the entire purport of the formations-aggregate stage.

From **severing** to **non-grasping.**

### From the Mirror-Student to the Last Demon-Revealing Mirror

At the moment the formations aggregate is exhausted, is the net of the ten māras broken?

By Jiāoguāng's water simile — the formations aggregate is a fine ripple, the consciousness aggregate is flowing water without waves. The fine ripple is broken, and the surface looks still. But **the water still flows.** The danger of the consciousness-aggregate stage lies exactly here: there is no longer any visible wave to misjudge, no longer any obvious thought to pin down, but the flow of the water is still there — **and this subtle flow now looks very nearly like true mind itself.**

The tenth māra, "five present-nirvāṇa," is the extreme of the formations-aggregate "mirror-student" — taking the present, genuine meditative state for the ultimate. What the next chapter's consciousness-aggregate stage unfolds is another kind of mirror-student — taking an object finer than any meditative state for the ultimate. That object is so subtle as to be "seemingly substantial yet vacuous, seemingly existent yet not" (Jiāoguāng's phrase), so subtle that even the doubt "is this true mind?" is swallowed up.

The net of the ten māras looks as if it breaks at the close of this chapter. But there is one last demon-revealing mirror waiting in the next.
