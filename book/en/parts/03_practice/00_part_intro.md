# Part Three · Practice · The Hundred Dharmas and the Eight Consciousnesses · A Parts Diagram for the Path

Part Two gave you a pair of eyes. Prajñā illuminated the five aggregates as empty, illuminated the twelve links of dependent origination as ungraspable, illuminated even prajñā itself as something to be set down. By the time you finished Part Two, you knew *what to see*.

But once you have seen—how do the feet walk?

The Heart Sūtra says the five aggregates—form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness—are the basic classification of lived experience. Five boxes to hold everything. But if you actually sit down and observe your own formations aggregate, you very quickly find that five aggregates are not enough. What you observe is a tangle: sometimes vigor, sometimes laziness; sometimes faith, sometimes doubt; sometimes you cannot even tell whether you are "making an effort" or simply "anxious." The resolution of the five aggregates is too low—like using a map of the world to find the breakfast shop at the end of your street.

What Part Three does is very direct: **it zooms the map in.**

Around the fourth or fifth century, the bodhisattva Vasubandhu wrote a small treatise of only 722 characters—the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas, the Doors of Illumination of the Great Vehicle* (*Śatadharmaprakāśamukha-śāstra*). He took the five aggregates apart into finer pieces: "consciousness" into eight, "formations" into fifty-one psychological qualities plus twenty-four conceptual constructs, "form" into eleven, and then he added six unconditioned dharmas. One hundred dharmas in all. These hundred dharmas are not a glossary of terms to memorize—they are a map for practice. Every component inside your mind has a name, a position, and can be recognized in the awareness of daily life.

The fifteen chapters of Part Three simply spread this map open, region by region.

The first six chapters handle the eight consciousnesses—from the ālaya-vijñāna (life's deepest storehouse of seeds) to manas (the unseen engine of self-grasping) to the sixth consciousness (the only consciousness that can look in a mirror) to the first five consciousnesses (the immediacy of the senses). These six chapters answer one question: **how many layers does your mind have, and what is each layer doing?**

The middle seven chapters handle the fifty-one mental factors—the contents of mind. The universal and object-specific factors are mind's smallest engines, the wholesome factors are the fuel of practice, the root afflictions are the engine of saṃsāra, and the secondary afflictions are the branches and twigs of affliction. To know the mental factors is to know your own patterns of reaction—why you keep turning a small thing over and over in your mind, why you cannot rein in your temper even when you know you should, why the thoughts in meditation pour down like a waterfall and will not stop.

The last two chapters handle form and the dissociated formations—the world you assume is "out there" turns out to be inseparable from the participation of mind; the time, space, gain, and loss you assume exist objectively turn out to be provisional designations of the activity of consciousness. These two chapters will change how you look at the very idea of an "external world."

The final chapter is the destination: the unconditioned dharmas. All the conditioned dharmas taken apart across the first fourteen chapters point, in the end, toward one thing that is not fabricated—suchness (*tathatā*). It has been here all along; it is only obscured by the imagined nature. The whole work of the hundred dharmas is to lift that obscuring away, layer by layer.

---

This is the part of the whole book that most resembles a toolbox. It reads densely; some chapters may need a second pass. That is not your problem—it is simply what Yogācāra is. Yogācāra does not split your attachments open with negation the way prajñā does; it uses affirmation to take your mind apart to the finest grain—eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, three transformations, seeds and their manifestation—every concept as precise as a scalpel.

But the precision is not for show. The precision of Yogācāra exists so that the practitioner can **see accurately**. The moment you can recognize, in ordinary life, "ah—this is restlessness, not vigor," "this is conceit, not confidence," "behind this thought is the self-grasping of manas doing the pushing," practice passes from vague "contemplation" into precise awareness. The more precise the awareness, the more possible the transformation.

Part Two gave you eyes. Part Three gives you coordinates.

We begin with the overview—all dharmas are without self, and what the hundred dharmas are.
