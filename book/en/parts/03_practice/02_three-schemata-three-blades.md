## 3.2 The Three Schemata — Three Blades of Different Resolution

Chapter 3.1 gave you a map: the hundred dharmas. A hundred parts, five drawers, opening with "all dharmas are without self" and closing with "the two kinds of selflessness."

But before we pry those hundred parts open one by one, we have to stop and ask one question. Because this hundred-dharma map is really an enlargement of an older map—an older map called the **five aggregates**. And earlier still, the Buddha drew two others: the **twelve bases** and the **eighteen elements**.

So here is the question: **it is one and the same body-mind—why did the Buddha draw it three times?**

### Three Lists, or Three Blades?

The five aggregates, twelve bases, and eighteen elements are together called, in the tradition, the **three schemata**. All three count the same thing—this body-mind you mistake for a "self." Form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness is it; eye-ear-nose-tongue-body-mind plus form-sound-smell-taste-touch-dharma is it; the eighteen elements, still it.

If they all count the same thing, why count three times?

If the three schemata were merely three arrangements of one content, then learning two extra sets would be nothing but memorizing two extra lists. That is exactly what this book most wants to avoid—piling two more layers of concept on top of "seeing through and letting go." Were that so, the twelve bases and eighteen elements could be thrown away and the five aggregates alone would do.

But it is the other way around. **The three schemata are not three lists; they are three blades of different resolution.**

One and the same body-mind: the aggregates dissect it at one fineness, the bases at another, the elements at a third. And *why* the fineness differs—that is the key to this chapter.

### Disposition: The Buddha Looks Not at Classification, but at Where Your Grasping Is Stuck

The Buddha did not draw these three pictures sitting at a desk wondering "how to classify most completely." Open the *Saṃyukta-āgama* and you find that the three schemata are not laid side by side in one table delivered at once—they are scattered across different passages, spoken to different people, for different problems, one picture at a time.

To those who grasped body-mind as "self," the Buddha taught the five aggregates; to those stuck at the six sense-doors, chasing after objects, he taught the twelve bases; to those deluded about both, he taught the eighteen elements.

In other words, **the three schemata are three different conversations, not a three-column table.** The Buddha faced living persons, one at a time—wherever this person was stuck, he used that blade to loosen it. Where the grasping is, there the blade falls. The tradition has a name for this: **responsiveness to disposition** (對機, *duì jī*)—"disposition" being the listener's present temperament and lodging-place of grasping, and "responsiveness" being meeting him right there.

Strikingly, even the *Heart Sūtra*, illumining emptiness, follows the order of these three schemata. It negates "self" not with a vague blanket "no-self," but **negating right down the three schemata in turn**: first "no form, no feeling, perception, formations, consciousness" (negating the five aggregates), then "no eye-ear-nose-tongue-body-mind, no form-sound-smell-taste-touch-dharma" (negating the twelve bases), then "no eye-element, up to no mind-consciousness-element" (negating the eighteen elements).

Why does prajñā too travel by the three schemata? Because to untie, you must untie where the knot is tied. In the Āgama the three schemata are "opened up to show you"; in the *Heart Sūtra* they are "negated one by one." One opens, one veils—opposite directions, yet the three points where the hand falls are exactly the same.

### Delusion, Faculty, Inclination: One Body-Mind, Three Resolutions

The Āgama is the living scene; by the time of the *Abhidharmakośa*, that scene's wisdom is crystallized into a single line. The bodhisattva Vasubandhu says:

> Because of the threefold difference of delusion, faculty, and inclination, the aggregates, bases, and elements are taught as three.

Meaning: the Buddha teaches the three schemata to meet three kinds of people—**differing in what they are deluded about** (delusion), **differing in keenness** (faculty), and **differing in preference** (inclination).

Take "delusion" first. Some are deluded about the mind—taking the various thoughts and feelings, lumped together, as one "I that feels, thinks, and decides." So the Buddha gives them the five aggregates: he collapses "matter" into one clump but splits "mind" into four—feeling, perception, formations, consciousness—so they can see that the mind they took for a "self" is really four streams, each arising and ceasing on its own, with no master running through them.

Others are deluded about matter—taking the body and outer objects as solidly, independently "right there." So the Buddha gives them the twelve bases: he collapses "mind" into a single mind-base but splits "matter" into the five faculties and five objects, spread into ten, so they can see that the so-called outer object is only a construct of dependent arising at six doorways—apart from the faculty, no object stands on its own.

Those deluded about both, he gives the eighteen elements—matter split into ten, mind into eight, all laid open.

Now "faculty" and "inclination." The same truth: a keen person grasps it in a few words, so give him the most concise—the aggregates; a dull person needs it spelled out, so give him the most complete—the elements; the middling get the bases. That is "faculty." And some are by nature drawn to the brief, others to the thorough—give according to the bent, and the Dharma enters more easily. That is "inclination."

Put the three together: **what one is deluded about, what one can bear, what one prefers—three kinds of person, all different; so one and the same body-mind is cut into three resolutions.** The content does not change; the blade-work changes with the person.

### Three Blades, Falling at Three Places: Open the Mental, Open the Material, Open Both

Here we can say the most crucial layer plainly.

Why does "opening" untie?

Because self-grasping always grasps at a "one"—a permanent, single, deciding "self." And "opening" spreads what looked like a seamless whole into the many, into a heap of conditions leaning on one another. Once you see it is many, is assembled, is arising-and-ceasing in succession, the foothold of "taking it as one self" drops away.

So opening is not giving you a little more to know. **Opening dismantles the false floorboard of "one whole" on which self-grasping stands.**

The three schemata pry up that false floorboard at three different places—

- **The five aggregates open the mental**, dismantling "taking mind as self." Mind is spread into four streams; "mind is one self" can no longer stand.
- **The twelve bases open the material**, dismantling "taking outer objects as real." Matter is spread into the arising of faculty-and-object; "there is a real world out there" is loosened.
- **The eighteen elements open both**, dismantling "taking the knower as self." Even knowing itself is laid open: faculty is no master-knower, object no real known, consciousness no independent knower—the three arise only by leaning on one another, none can be set up as "that I which knows the world."

Three blades, three resolutions, falling at three lodging-places of differing depth.

This is just what this book has been saying all along: joy-in-dharma is not the fullness of "I've understood one more set"; it is the release of "so *this* blade falls right on the grasping I had hidden so long." The three schemata are not three taxonomies to memorize; they are three ways of laying the blade to **dismantle, for you, the same illusion of a "self."**

### Why You Cannot Keep Just One Scheme

A thought easily arises here: since the eighteen elements cut most thoroughly, why not keep just that one and throw away the aggregates and bases?

⚠️ You cannot. And it is precisely "responsiveness" that lets none of the three be missing.

For a keen person who only takes mind as self, the eighteen elements are the long way around—what he needs is the directness of that single aggregate-stroke. Conversely, for someone who clings hard to the body as real, the five aggregates collapse matter into a clump and so skip right past his lodging-place; you have to open the material with the twelve bases to meet him.

**It is not that one scheme is higher, but which scheme meets the lock before you.** Discard any one scheme, and you shut a kind of person outside the gate.

Two further misunderstandings to brush aside here.

First, ⚠️ do not rank the three schemata high to low—thinking the eighteen elements most ultimate, the five aggregates shallowest. More dharmas does not mean higher attainment. For the right person, the five aggregates are an ultimate stroke; for the wrong person, even the eighteen elements are just a detour. **The concise is not shallow, the elaborate is not high; responsiveness is the measure.**

Second, ⚠️ do not hear "responsiveness" as "everyone has their own truth." Responsiveness adjusts the **resolution**, not the truth. The single fact the three schemata point to—body-mind is impermanent, without self, and by nature suffering—does not change with disposition. What changes is only the fineness and the order of entry. Responsiveness is the skillfulness of compassion, not the relativizing of truth.

### Give the Right One, Not More

Set the principle of the three schemata in our own time, and it answers a question modern people most easily get wrong: in studying the Dharma, is more always better?

The three schemata say: no.

Facing someone deluded about mind, the Buddha will not force the eighteen elements on him because they are "more complete"; he gives the five aggregates, because that blade falls right on this person's grasping. **The wisdom of responsiveness is the wisdom of subtraction—not giving you every resolution, but recognizing which one you need right now, and giving only that.**

Like a good doctor facing a lesion: he does not run X-ray, ultrasound, CT, and MRI all at once; he picks the one imaging that fits the location. Resolution is not better-the-higher, but better-the-fitter. (⚠️ This is only an analogy to aid understanding, not a claim that the Dharma equals medical imaging.)

So for you, today, the three schemata leave just two reminders: **no need to crave more**—see clearly where your own grasping lodges, take up the one scheme that meets it for deep contemplation, and do not turn three sets into a burden by practicing them all at once; **and no need to slight the concise**—the five aggregates, though most concise, are already an ultimate stroke for the keen, for those who take mind as self.

Give the right one, not more. This is the compassion of the Buddha's teaching the three schemata, and the taste of dharma you should receive from reading them.

✅ The three's being three is not so you collect two more taxonomies; it is that the Buddha has readied three blades, waiting for you to recognize where your own long-hidden grasping is lodged—in mind, in matter, or in "knowing" itself—and then to take up the one that meets it, and cut.

And when we push that "open both" stroke of the eighteen elements all the way down in the direction of "opening the mental," we find it cannot stop: beneath the six consciousnesses, two deeper layers of mind are at work. That is what the next chapter pries open—the **eight consciousnesses**. The "consciousness" that the three schemata collapsed into a clump, then split into feeling-perception-formations, will, in the hands of Yogācāra, be spread into a full eight layers.
