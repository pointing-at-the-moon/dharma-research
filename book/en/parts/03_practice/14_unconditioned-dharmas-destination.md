## 3.14 The Unconditioned Dharmas — Where the Hundred Dharmas End

You come home.

The key turns in the lock. The lights are still off, but the late sun slants in through the window, leaving the living room half gold and half dark. You have lived here all along; today you only went out for a while—yet this moment of coming back feels like returning to a place you have not seen in a very long time.

By this chapter, the journey through the hundred dharmas feels exactly like that.

From 3.1 to 3.13, this book has taken apart, one layer at a time, the four classes of conditioned dharmas—the transforming power of the mind dharmas, the workings of the mental factors, the projection of the form dharmas, the designation of the factors dissociated from mind. With every layer peeled away, your grip on "self" and "world" loosened a little. By the end of 3.13, the dissociated factors had no resistance left to them at all; conditioned dharmas had thinned out to their thinnest point.

And then? Take one more step—where does it lead?

The answer is not a new place. The answer is: **the very place you have been all along.**

The fifth class of the hundred dharmas—the **unconditioned dharmas**, six of them—is not another stop in the sense of a journey's end. It is the true face of all dharmas, the face that shows once you have seen through the structure of the first four classes. The six unconditioned dharmas—**space, analytical cessation, non-analytical cessation, immovable cessation, cessation of perception and feeling, and suchness**—turn on a single axis: suchness. **Suchness is the real nature of consciousness itself.**

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, locks this destination into a single verse:

> The ultimate reality of all dharmas
> is none other than suchness;
> ever abiding in its nature,
> it is the real nature of consciousness-only.

"The real nature of consciousness-only"—suchness is not some other thing standing outside consciousness; it is **the true nature of consciousness**. All dharmas are transformations of consciousness (consciousness-only), and the nature of consciousness is suchness (real nature). Of all the hundred dharmas, every single one has this same suchness as its real nature.

### "Unconditioned" Does Not Mean "Doing Nothing"

First, let us clear away a misunderstanding that easily trips people up.

In ordinary Chinese, the two characters *wú wéi* ("unconditioned") point toward "non-action"—Laozi's "governing through non-action," staying out of things, letting nature take its course. The Buddhist *wú wéi*—Sanskrit *asaṃskṛta*—means something else entirely: **not fabricated**. Not arising through conditions, not perishing through conditions, not cycling through the four marks of arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing.

Conditioned dharmas are waves, rising and falling with the wind; an unconditioned dharma is the wetness of water—however large the waves, however small, even when they vanish, the wetness is always just so. "Unconditioned" names this "just so," not "doing nothing."

Here the first correction must be made. ⚠️ **The road to the unconditioned dharmas is not "doing nothing"—it is the most strenuous, active practice there is.** The *Saṃyukta Āgama* says it plainly: "What are the unconditioned dharmas? The permanent exhaustion of craving, the permanent exhaustion of aversion and delusion, the permanent exhaustion of all afflictions—these are the unconditioned dharmas. And what is the path-trace of the unconditioned? The Noble Eightfold Path." The content of the unconditioned dharmas is the **permanent exhaustion of the afflictions**, and the road that leads there—the path-trace of the unconditioned—is the **Noble Eightfold Path**. The three wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and cultivation; the six perfections; the gradual training of the ten grounds—every step is an active, "conditioned" effort.

**"Unconditioned" is the goal of practice, not its method.** The method is intensely conditioned; only the goal is unconditioned. The two must not be confused.

The Āgamas also fixed early on the relationship between the unconditioned and the four marks: "The conditioned arises, abides, changes, and ceases; the unconditioned neither arises, nor abides, nor changes, nor ceases." Conditioned dharmas bear the four marks (and 3.13 has shown that these four marks are themselves only designated); unconditioned dharmas transcend the four marks. From the Āgamas down to Yogācāra, this skeleton holds without a break: the conditioned arises and perishes and cycles; the unconditioned neither arises nor perishes.

### The Shape of the Six Unconditioned Dharmas: Five Are Side-Profiles of Suchness, One Is Suchness Itself

The *Hundred Dharmas Treatise* lists the fifth class as six: space unconditioned, analytical cessation, non-analytical cessation, immovable cessation, cessation-of-perception-and-feeling unconditioned, and suchness unconditioned.

These six are not six different "things."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle eight, lays the point out with great clarity: "Space, analytical cessation, non-analytical cessation, and the rest—under which nature are they subsumed? All three may subsume them. As appearances of space and so forth produced by mind, arising in dependence on mind, they belong to the other-dependent nature; ordinary beings, falsely grasping them as truly existent, place them under the thoroughly imagined nature; **but as meanings such as 'space' provisionally established upon suchness, they belong to the perfectly accomplished nature.**"

The key is that last clause. The first five dharmas are different meanings "provisionally established" upon suchness—space unconditioned is the provisional name for suchness "where form meets no obstruction," analytical cessation is the provisional name for suchness "where afflictions are severed," immovable cessation and cessation-of-perception-and-feeling are the provisional names for suchness "in deep absorption," non-analytical cessation is the provisional name for suchness "where conditions are absent." **Suchness is the substance; the first five dharmas are its functions.**

An image. Suchness is like white light; the first five unconditioned dharmas are the different colors that white light refracts into through different prisms. The colors are real (they exist other-dependently), but the white light is the source (the perfectly accomplished nature). The red light you see does not mean some separate light-source called "red light" exists—red light is simply how white light shows under a red prism.

This image answers to an important correction: ⚠️ **the six unconditioned dharmas are not six independent unconditioned entities.** The *Samuccaya* and its *Vyākhyā* said so long ago: "Suchness is provisionally established as three by analytical division, not by virtue of its own nature." The classification of suchness is a provisional designation, not a difference within suchness itself. In the same way, the differences among the six unconditioned dharmas are differences of angle, not of substance.

With this skeleton in place, the meaning of each of the six falls easily into order.

### Space, Non-Analytical Cessation, Immovable Cessation, Cessation of Perception and Feeling — Suchness Showing in Four Settings

These four can be sketched briefly, because each is "suchness as it shows in some particular setting," and none of them is the final home of practice.

**Space unconditioned**—the shallowest of the six. The *Pañcaskandhaka* defines it as "that which accommodates form," merely the provisional name for "where form meets no obstruction." In meditation a sense of "wide-open space" may arise in the mind—and it is very easy to mistake this for having seen suchness. But it is closer to space unconditioned: only the "accommodating state" that appears when mental activity briefly subsides; it is not suchness. **Suchness is not a feeling of openness, not the stillness of "nothing there"**—suchness is the real nature of all dharmas. It is present amid the bustle, present amid the quiet; "at all stages it ever abides in its nature," neither "more suchness" because of a meditative state nor "less suchness" because of distraction.

**Non-analytical cessation**—when conditions are not complete, a dharma simply does not arise. This is not the permanent severance of afflictions (it "does not permanently destroy the latent afflictions") but the absence of the conditions for present activity. The seeds are still there; they merely have, for now, no chance to sprout.

Non-analytical cessation points to an important strategy in practice. Beyond directly countering the afflictions with wisdom (the analytical-cessation route), you can also **actively manage the conditions**, so that the circumstances for unwholesome dharmas to manifest are kept absent—ordering your practice environment, choosing good spiritual friends and a community of practice, building a steady rhythm of daily life. This is the practical counterpart to the "non-combination" discussed in 3.13.

But know this: non-analytical cessation is a stopgap, not the final word. Once the conditions reassemble, the dharma can still arise. True liberation requires analytical cessation—permanent severance at the level of the seeds.

**Immovable cessation**—from the fourth dhyāna upward, where neither pain nor pleasure arises, the quiet state in which the mind is not stirred. The Āgama's "attaining the fourth dhyāna... attaining an immovable mind" is this very state. "Immovable" carries two senses: unmoved by pain and pleasure (beyond the two extremes), and unmoved by worldly dharmas (settled in supramundane concentration). The *Laṅkāvatāra* calls the eighth-ground bodhisattva the "Immovable Ground," and the two resonate doctrinally—the bodhisattva who has attained the acceptance of the non-arising of dharmas is "immovable" not merely in the sense of meditative stillness, but in the sense of irreversibility with respect to suchness.

**Cessation-of-perception-and-feeling unconditioned**—the unconditioned within the attainment of cessation. The first six consciousnesses cease, the defiled portion of the seventh consciousness ceases, and even "perception" and "feeling" come to rest. As 3.13 has already shown, this is a saintly attainment, "beyond ordinary beings."

The most crucial scriptural witness for the attainment of cessation comes from the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle four: "For one who abides in the attainment of cessation, the activities of body, speech, and mind have all ceased, yet the lifespan is not extinguished, warmth has not departed, the faculties are not impaired—**consciousness does not leave the body**... Moreover, if in this state there were no consciousness at all, the person would be like rubble or tile, no longer counted among the living." In the attainment of cessation, the first six consciousnesses and the defiled portion of the seventh stop completely, but **the eighth consciousness still sustains the body**. This is why one in the attainment of cessation is still a living being, not a corpse.

These two unconditioned states revealed in deep absorption share a trap to guard against: ⚠️ **neither immovable cessation nor cessation of perception and feeling is nirvāṇa.** They are temporary unconditioned states within absorption; once one emerges, the mind and consciousness stir again. Non-Buddhists and some practitioners lose their way here—taking deep absorption for liberation, equating a meditative state with the ultimate. The sequence of the Buddha's own passing into nirvāṇa makes the point: he entered, in order, from the first dhyāna up to the attainment of cessation, **then emerged from the attainment of cessation, and only then entered final nirvāṇa**—if the attainment of cessation were itself nirvāṇa, he would not have needed to leave it and enter again.

Each of the four has its place, but not one of them is the final home. Suchness is not inside some special state; suchness is in the real nature of every state.

### Analytical Cessation — The One Most Directly Bound Up with Practice

Of the six unconditioned dharmas, the one closest to practice is analytical cessation (*pratisaṃkhyā-nirodha*).

"Analytical" is the **discerning** function of prajñā-wisdom; "cessation" is the **final non-arising** of contaminated dharmas; together they mean "freedom from bondage"—liberation from the binding of the afflictions. The *Vyākhyā*'s definition wastes no words: "Analytical cessation is the cessation that is liberation, because it **permanently destroys the latent afflictions**."

The key is those four words, "permanently destroys the latent afflictions." As 3.10 and 3.11 have shown, the afflictions have both present activity and seeds; press down the present activity without cutting the seeds, and the afflictions return again and again. Analytical cessation severs them thoroughly at the **level of the seeds**—severed forever, never to arise again.

There is a very deep point here, easily read past: **analytical cessation is not a new purity that practice "creates"; it is the original purity, showing itself once the obscuration is removed.** The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, puts it precisely: "Though suchness is pure in nature, its appearance is defiled; therefore, when defilement is removed, we provisionally speak of a 'new purity,' and this new purity is called the transformation of the basis."

"Though suchness is pure in nature, its appearance is defiled"—the **nature** of suchness has always been pure; only its **appearance**, at the stage of the ordinary being, is overlaid with defilement. At the moment defilement is removed, the so-called "new purity" is spoken of only provisionally—nothing new has been added to suchness itself; the obscuration has simply been taken away, and the purity that was always there can show.

The whole logic of practice is in that sentence. You are not trying to "manufacture" a nirvāṇa; you are trying to **reveal** the nirvāṇa that was always there. You are not trying to "reach" suchness; you are trying to **remove** what obscures it. This is not at all the same model as the ordinary idea of "pursuing a goal" or "becoming a better person"—that model still works by addition, still keeps putting things into the storehouse of consciousness. The direction of analytical cessation is **subtraction**, not addition.

How analytical cessation unfolds across the five stages of practice:

- **The stage of accumulation and the stage of intensified effort**—hearing, reflecting, cultivating, gathering the conditions. The affliction-seeds are still there, but the seeds of right view are growing.
- **The path of seeing**—non-discriminating wisdom first appears, and the seeds of the discriminatively arisen afflictions are severed all at once. For the first time, analytical cessation "shows itself"—one portion of it—in the practitioner's mind.
- **The path of cultivation**—the gradual training of the ten grounds, where the seeds of the innate afflictions are severed step by step. Analytical cessation grows complete ground by ground.
- **The ultimate stage**—the affliction-barrier and the cognitive-barrier permanently severed, analytical cessation made complete—great nirvāṇa.

With every portion of affliction severed, one more portion of analytical cessation "shows." **It is not that suchness has grown larger; it is that the obscuration has grown less.**

From the Āgamas' "the permanent exhaustion of craving, of aversion, of delusion," to the *Heart Sūtra*'s "because there is nothing to attain... far removed from inverted, dreamlike thinking, ultimate nirvāṇa," to Yogācāra's analytical cessation—the same line runs unbroken: sever the afflictions with wisdom, and the liberation that was always there shows itself.

### Suchness Unconditioned — The Real Nature of Consciousness-Only

Only when the six unconditioned dharmas arrive at suchness unconditioned does the whole picture close.

The *Mahāyāna Pañcaskandhaka*'s definition of suchness is the plainest: "What is suchness? **The dharma-nature and the dharma-selflessness of all dharmas.**" Suchness is the dharma-nature, the dharma-selflessness, of all dharmas. The *Samuccaya* unfolds this definition through a sevenfold exchange:

> Why is suchness called suchness? Because its own nature is without change.
> Why is suchness called selflessness? Because it is free of the two selves.
> Why is suchness called emptiness? Because no defilement operates within it.
> Why is suchness called signlessness? Because all signs are quieted within it.
> Why is suchness called the ultimate-real? Because it is the object free of distortion.
> Why is suchness called the supreme meaning? Because it is the domain of the most supreme noble wisdom.
> Why is suchness called the dharma-realm? Because it is the basis of the wondrous dharmas of all śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and buddhas.

Suchness, selflessness, emptiness, signlessness, the ultimate-real, the supreme meaning, the dharma-realm—seven names, seven facets of one and the same thing. The *Prajñāpāramitā* system's list of synonyms for suchness (suchness, dharma-realm, dharma-nature, non-falsity, non-changeability, equality, freedom from arising, dharma-fixity, dharma-abiding, the ultimate-real, the realm of space, the inconceivable realm) answers exactly to these seven.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, takes apart the two characters of "suchness" (*zhēn rú*): "**'True' means real, showing it to be non-false; 'thus' means ever-same, indicating it is without change.** This reality, at all stages, ever abides in its nature, and so it is called suchness." True is non-false; thus is unchanging—suchness is just that which "at all stages ever abides in its nature."

Those three words, "at all stages," are the key. At the stage of the ordinary being, suchness is present; at the stage of the sage, suchness is present; at the fruit of buddhahood, suchness is still present. The ordinary being lacks not a particle of it, the buddha-fruit adds not a particle—**this is the Yogācāra basis for the *Heart Sūtra*'s "neither increasing nor decreasing."**

The *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* fixes the point from another direction: "The ultimate-non-nature revealed by dharma-selflessness—**at all times, at all times, the dharma-nature of all dharmas abides, unconditioned.** Because it is incompatible with all defilement, at all times, at all times, the dharma-nature of all dharmas abides, and is therefore unconditioned." Suchness is not something that only begins to exist when practice begins; it abides "at all times, at all times." Defilement has never truly stained suchness; it only looks stained at the level of the ordinary being's appearance.

The *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* compresses the point as far as it will go: "Because it is one flavor throughout all domains, the perfectly accomplished nature has no established numerical distinctions." Suchness is **one flavor** in every dharma. The suchness of form dharmas and the suchness of mind dharmas are not two; the suchness of wholesome dharmas and the suchness of unwholesome dharmas are not two. One flavor.

If you can see, in any single dharma, its suchness—see that form is, in its nature, thus; that an affliction is, in its nature, thus; that this very thought, right now, is in its nature thus—then you have seen the suchness of all dharmas. Because suchness has only one flavor.

### Suchness Is Not Outside Consciousness: The Twofold Distinction of the Real Nature of Consciousness-Only

Now we come to the place in the whole book most easily misread. The second Middle Way correction must be made.

⚠️ **Suchness is not an entity standing independent outside consciousness.**

If you understand suchness as an "absolute truth" existing independently outside consciousness—like Plato's realm of Forms, or the Christian God, or the Brahma of Hinduism—then you have fallen into **grasping at a dharma-self**. It is the same grasping at real existence, only renamed: before, you grasped at a soul; now you grasp at suchness; the essence has not changed.

Yogācāra, from start to finish, refuses this understanding. The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle eight, says the six unconditioned dharmas "**do not depart from mind**," and all three natures may subsume them. The verse in fascicle nine says "it is the real nature of consciousness-only"—suchness is the real nature of consciousness-only, not something existing apart from consciousness.

Here is a twofold distinction to follow carefully:

> The real nature of consciousness-only is, in brief, of two kinds: one is the **false**, namely the thoroughly imagined; the other is the **real**, namely the perfectly accomplished nature—and to set it apart from the false, we say "real nature."
> Again there are two natures: one is the **conventional**, namely the other-dependent; the other is the **ultimate**, namely the perfectly accomplished—and to set it apart from the conventional, we say "real nature."

The two characters "real nature" carry two distinctions: it is **not the thoroughly imagined** consciousness-nature (that is false), and **not the other-dependent** consciousness-nature (that is conventional). Once both distinctions are made, what remains is "real nature"—**the perfectly accomplished nature**—which is suchness.

So the small equation reads:

> The real nature of consciousness-only = the perfectly accomplished nature = suchness

Three terms, one and the same thing. From the angle of "setting it apart from the false" it is called the real nature of consciousness-only; from the angle of "the third of the three natures" it is called the perfectly accomplished; from the angle of "the real nature of all dharmas, pervading everywhere" it is called suchness.

This equation locks down one thing: **suchness is nowhere else.** It is the real nature of every one of the hundred dharmas. When you were reading 3.1 on the mind dharmas, the suchness of the mind dharmas was already there; when you were reading 3.12 on the form dharmas, the suchness of the form dharmas was already there; when you were reading 3.13 on the factors dissociated from mind, even those thinnest designated concepts had their suchness. It was only obscured by the thoroughly imagined, so you did not see it.

The *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* gives the path to seeing suchness with great clarity: "**Upon the other-dependent characteristic, through the condition of not grasping at the thoroughly imagined characteristic, the perfectly accomplished characteristic can be known.**" Do not leave the other-dependent to look elsewhere for the perfectly accomplished. **Upon** the other-dependent, remove the thoroughly imagined, and the perfectly accomplished shows of itself. The three natures are not three worlds; they are three facets of one and the same dharma.

A flower—the flower is a coming-together of conditions (other-dependent); to grasp the flower as "my flower," as "a flower that blooms forever," is the thoroughly imagined; the real nature of the flower is suchness (perfectly accomplished). An affliction—the affliction arises from conditions (other-dependent); to grasp the affliction as "a real me suffering" is the thoroughly imagined; the real nature of the affliction is likewise suchness. In every dharma, all three natures are present at once.

The *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* points out one more deeply important observation: "**The perfectly accomplished nature should be said to have no abiding place and no support.**" Suchness depends on no dharma. The hundred dharmas of the first four classes all have something to depend on (mental factors depend on mind, form dharmas depend on consciousness's transformation, dissociated factors depend on the relative positions of form and mind); suchness depends on nothing. It is not produced by anything; it is the real nature of all dharmas, not the product of any.

### The Loop of the Hundred Dharmas' Descent Closes

Now we can look back over the whole arc.

> mind dharmas → mental factors → form dharmas → dissociated factors → unconditioned dharmas
> the transformer / the mental content / the projected material aspect / the conceptual designated aspect / the real nature of consciousness

From the mind dharmas to the dissociated factors, the ontological density falls steadily all the way: the power to discern → functional correspondence → resistance → positional designation → not even designation left. With every layer peeled away, the grasping at "real existence" is loosened by one degree. By the end of 3.13, conditioned dharmas had thinned out to mere concept.

Take one more step, and you are at the unconditioned dharmas—not arriving at a new place, but discovering that the real nature of **this whole journey itself** is suchness.

The image of the car comes to its close in this chapter.

The mind dharmas are the driver, the mental factors the driving frame of mind, the form dharmas the road and the scenery, the dissociated factors the map and the timetable—then what are the unconditioned dharmas?

**They are the moment you finally look up and see: the driver, the frame of mind, the road, the scenery, the map—the real nature of all of it is one and the same thing.** The journey is not a movement from this shore to the far shore; you never left home at all.

You have walked the whole terrain of the hundred dharmas, gone the long way around, and found at the end: the place you were going is the place you were always in. The car is still there, the road is still there, the scenery is still there, the map is still there—all of it is there, all of it the other-dependent's existence. But you are no longer lost inside them—because you know their real nature is suchness, and your own real nature is suchness, and **"you" and "the world," at the deepest level, are one and the same real nature.**

The first sentence of the *Hundred Dharmas Treatise* is "all dharmas are without self" (the opening thesis of 3.1); its last class is the six unconditioned dharmas (turning on the axis of suchness). These two sentences are two faces of one thing:

- **"All dharmas are without self"**—this is the **breaking**. It breaks the thoroughly imagined twofold grasping at self and dharmas, taking away the false addition.
- **"The real nature of consciousness-only is none other than suchness"**—this is the **revealing**. The perfectly accomplished, revealed by the two emptinesses, shows of itself.

After the breaking comes the revealing, and what is revealed is suchness. The hundred dharmas begin with breaking and close with revealing—this is the complete message the *Hundred Dharmas Treatise* means to hand you.

Here the fourth, and most important, correction must be made: ⚠️ **understanding the doctrine of suchness ≠ realizing suchness.**

This whole book, including this chapter, operates at the level of the **wisdom of hearing and reflection**. Understanding the doctrine of the six unconditioned dharmas, discerning the relationship between suchness and conditioned dharmas, grasping the equation "the real nature of consciousness-only is none other than suchness"—all of this is **knowing**. **Realizing** is another matter. Knowing is the discriminative understanding of the sixth consciousness; realizing is the firsthand experience of non-discriminating wisdom.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* describes the path of seeing as the "**direct realization** of the truth revealed by the two emptinesses." Direct realization, not understanding. Reading a hundred books on swimming is not the same as being able to swim; reading the discourse on suchness a hundred times is not the same as seeing suchness. What this book can do is draw the map clearly—which junction to turn at, what you will see along the way, which direction home lies in. Jumping into the water—that part you must do yourself.

The next chapter—**3.15 Turning Consciousness into Wisdom: Coming Home**—deals with exactly this step. If 3.14 tells you where "home" is, 3.15 tells you how actually to come home. **The four wisdoms shining complete, the five stages of practice, the direct realization of the two emptinesses**—from reading the map to no longer needing the map, from knowing "all dharmas are without self" to living "all dharmas are without self."

The whole of S3 is this single journey: it opens its brush with "all dharmas are without self," and comes home with "turning consciousness into wisdom."
