## 3.8 Wholesome Mental Factors: The Fuel for Practice

Chapter 3.7 closed by leaving one question open: **where does the fuel come from?**

The engine—the five universals—fires by itself. The steering wheel—the five object-specifics—can turn on demand. But which way the car actually travels depends on what you pour into the tank. The eleven wholesome mental factors are the fuel that can make the car run toward liberation.

### The Eleven Are Not a List but a Structure

First we have to clear up a common misreading.

The *Śatadharmaprakāśamukha-śāstra* lists eleven wholesome factors: trust, conscience, moral fear, non-attachment, non-aversion, non-delusion, vigor, pliancy, non-negligence, equanimity, non-harming. A beginner who sees this list easily takes it for "a collection of eleven virtues"—like eleven brands on a supermarket shelf, where you pick whichever ones suit you.

It is not like that.

These eleven are an **organic structure**, and the order in which they are arranged carries meaning of its own:

- **Trust** comes first—the gateway to the path. Without trust, nothing after it can stand.
- **Conscience and moral fear** come second and third—once trust has arisen, it needs two lines of defense to keep it from regressing.
- **Non-attachment, non-aversion, non-delusion** sit in the middle—the three wholesome roots, the core driving power of wholesome mind.
- **Vigor** follows—the core drive needs something to keep pushing it forward.
- **Pliancy** comes after—once vigor reaches a certain depth, body and mind grow naturally supple.
- **Non-negligence and equanimity** come later still—it takes the stability of meditative concentration to make such fine psychological adjustments.
- **Non-harming** comes last—the ultimate orientation of the whole set of wholesome factors is to benefit others.

Read through the car metaphor from 3.7: trust is the spark that ignites, conscience and moral fear are the seal that prevents leaks, the three wholesome roots are the core fuel, vigor is the injection pressure, pliancy is the efficiency of combustion, non-negligence and equanimity are the voltage regulators, and non-harming is the destination the car is meant to reach. Eleven parts, assembled into a single engine that can run all the way to liberation. Once you understand the structure, the eleven no longer scatter into a pile of fragments in your mind.

### Trust: The First Step onto the Path

Trust heads the eleven. That line from the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* has already said it down to the bone: **"Trust is the origin of the path, the mother of all merit; it nourishes all wholesome dharmas."** Without trust, no wholesome dharma can grow.

But the word "trust" is easily misread. Trust is not blind belief (that is superstition), nor an emotional preference (that is mere fondness). The definition the *Cheng Weishi Lun* gives is precise to the point: **the essence of trust is "mental purity."** Trust is not an emotion; it is a state of the mind. And that state is composed of three aspects:

- **Trust in what is real**: believing that cause and effect are real, that the facts and principles of dharmas are not fictions, not something people made up.
- **Trust in merit**: believing that the Three Jewels genuinely possess pure merit. The Buddha has walked this road, the Dharma is a real road, and the Saṅgha is walking it now.
- **Trust in capacity**: believing that you yourself have the power to walk this road and to arrive.

None of the three can be missing. To have trust in what is real without trust in merit is like believing the map is true but not believing the destination exists. To have trust in merit without trust in capacity is like believing the destination is wonderful but not believing you can get there—it turns into pure gazing upward, with no way to land. To have trust in capacity without trust in what is real is like believing you can run without knowing where the track is.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* also offers a marvelous image: the **water-clarifying jewel**. Drop a water-clarifying jewel into a basin of murky water, and the whole basin clears of itself—not because some impurity has been filtered out, but because the water itself becomes clear. Trust is that jewel: it does not work by scrubbing away some defilement in the mind; it makes the entire state of the mind clean. The reverse is also true. The absence of trust "is itself turbid, and goes on to make the rest of the mind and mental factors turbid"—lack of trust does not merely cloud itself, it pollutes every other wholesome dharma as well.

This is why trust ranks first. If you do not drop the water-clarifying jewel in first, the whole basin stays murky, and no other wholesome dharma can enter.

### Conscience and Moral Fear: Two Lines of Defense, Inner and Outer

Trust has arisen; next it needs the strength to hold its ground, so that it is not worn away in the daily friction of life. This is conscience and moral fear.

Conscience and moral fear are often used together, but their driving force differs:

- **Conscience** relies on "the power of self and dharma"—its strength comes from "self" (one's own dignity as a practitioner) and "dharma" (reverence for the true Dharma). The core phrasing of conscience is: "I am someone who has heard the Dharma—how could I do such a thing?"
- **Moral fear** relies on "the power of the world"—its strength comes from respect for the moral norms of the broader community. The core phrasing of moral fear is: "Anyone who saw this would condemn such conduct."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* sums up the difference in a single phrase: **regarding self and other**—conscience regards the self, moral fear regards the other. Put together, they form the practitioner's complete moral defense: an inner restraint that is self-aware, and an outer restraint that is social.

Conscience without moral fear easily makes a practitioner aloof and proud—recognizing only one's own standard, looking down on the basic norms of the world. Moral fear without conscience easily makes a practitioner dependent—growing negligent the moment no one is watching, slackening as soon as outer pressure eases. Conscience and moral fear must actualize together to form the complete moat.

The Āgamas list conscience and moral fear alongside trust, vigor, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom as the "seven powers." Conscience and moral fear are not soft emotions; they are forces of practice on the same rank as concentration and wisdom. The world calls conscience and moral fear the "two bright dharmas"—without these two, every wholesome dharma loses its foundation.

### The Three Wholesome Roots: The Ground-Color of Wholesome Mind

The three wholesome roots—non-attachment, non-aversion, non-delusion—are the core engine of the wholesome factors, directly countering the three poisons.

To understand the three wholesome roots, the key is to grasp what the word "counter" means in Yogācāra. **It does not mean "eliminate"; it means "replace with the opposite function":**

- **Non-attachment**: not "removing craving," but **non-clinging toward existence and the provisions of existence**. Facing the same existence and the same resources, craving grips tight; non-attachment lets go.
- **Non-aversion**: not "suppressing anger," but **non-hostility toward suffering and the causes of suffering**. Facing the same hardship and the same cause of hardship, aversion resists; non-aversion accepts.
- **Non-delusion**: not "erasing ignorance," but **clear understanding of the principles of all things**. Facing the same truth of things, delusion muddles it; non-delusion sees it clearly.

All three antidotes transform one's attitude toward the very same object—not fleeing the object, but transforming oneself within the situation.

What is special about the three wholesome roots is that they **pervade wholesome mind**—the moment wholesome mind arises, the three wholesome roots are necessarily present. The ground-color of wholesome mind is exactly this: clear (non-delusion), letting go (non-attachment), unangered (non-aversion). Only when these three actualize together does it become a genuinely wholesome mind.

None of the three can be missing:

- Non-attachment apart from non-delusion easily slides into "deluded letting-go"—not knowing why one lets go, just blindly not caring.
- Non-attachment apart from non-aversion easily collapses under pressure and turns into aversion—looking like letting go, but really just suppression.
- Non-aversion apart from non-delusion easily becomes "ignorant tolerance"—able to bear anything, but not knowing what ought to be borne and what ought not.

Only when the three wholesome roots support one another do they form the complete structure of wholesome mind.

### Vigor, Non-Negligence, Equanimity: Sustaining and Balancing

With direction in hand (the three wholesome roots), the next thing needed is the strength to keep pushing forward. This is the role of vigor.

The essence of **vigor** is "courage and fortitude"—"courage" is the force of rising upward, "fortitude" is the force of focused purity. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* lists five subtypes of vigor: donning armor (generating the great vow), engaging in action (taking up practice), not demeaning oneself (not making light of oneself), not retreating (not turning back), and not resting content (not growing complacent). From generating the aspiration to never turning back, vigor runs through the entire course of practice.

But vigor carries one crucial restriction: **it is exclusively of wholesome nature**. In everyday language "diligence" can apply to any direction of effort—"he is very diligent about making money"—but that is not vigor in the Yogācāra sense. Vigor refers only to effort directed toward wholesome dharmas. Exertion directed toward defiled dharmas is not vigor; it is "the striving aspect of craving." Direction is set by the three wholesome roots, and strength is supplied by vigor; neither can be missing.

Once vigor is running, it also needs two balancers: **non-negligence** and **equanimity**.

These two mental factors share one feature—**they have no independent essence of their own**. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* states it plainly: non-negligence is the functional state of vigor and the three wholesome roots in "guarding against [the bad] and cultivating [the good]"; equanimity is the functional state of vigor and the three wholesome roots in "causing the mind to abide in evenness, uprightness, and effortlessness." Take away vigor and the three wholesome roots, and non-negligence and equanimity have nothing left.

So why give them separate names? Because the difference in function needs to be marked. The same force in a different state needs a different name—like "water" and "ice." That force is called vigor when it is sprinting, non-negligence when it is guarding wholesome dharmas from loss, and equanimity when it is settling the mind into quiet stability.

Equanimity has three stages worth noting: **evenness** (the mind does not tilt) → **uprightness** (the mind does not bend) → **effortless abiding** (it can rest without deliberate effort). These three stages correspond to the early, middle, and later phases of practice: a beginner has to maintain balance deliberately, one at the middle phase is naturally upright, and at the later phase the mind abides spontaneously, needing no more exertion.

### Pliancy: The One Wholesome Factor That Does Not Pervade Wholesome Mind

Of the eleven, pliancy is the most special—**it is the only wholesome factor that does not pervade wholesome mind**.

The other ten are present the moment wholesome mind arises; pliancy alone has to wait for "the state of absorption"—the state of meditative concentration—before it appears. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says it clearly: "Ten pervade wholesome mind; pliancy does not. Only in the state of absorption is there pliancy, smoothing body and mind; in other states it is absent."

Everyday wholesome mind (the wholesome mind in giving, in keeping precepts, in feeling conscience and moral fear) has no pliancy, because body and mind are still in a coarse state. The appearance of pliancy marks the passage of practice from "scattered-mind goodness" into "concentrated-mind goodness."

The *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* judges this even more strictly: **meditation that has not attained pliancy is not yet true calm abiding**. The Buddha told Maitreya Bodhisattva that, before bodily pliancy and mental pliancy are attained, all the effort of meditating can only be called "tending toward *śamatha*"—the direction is right, but one has not yet arrived. Pliancy is the threshold of *śamatha*, not a bonus reward for meditating.

Pliancy is twofold, of body and of mind: the suppleness of the body (no rigidity, no fatigue, no heaviness) and the suppleness of the mind (no distraction, no sinking, no agitation). Bodily pliancy and mental pliancy must both be present together; one alone does not count. This is also why pliancy can serve as an objective gauge of the depth of meditation—it does not rest on the practitioner's own claim, but on whether body and mind actually show suppleness.

### Non-Harming: The Foundation of Compassion at the Level of Mental Factors

The last of the eleven is non-harming. It takes **non-aversion as its substance**—non-harming has no independent essence; it is nominally established as a portion of non-aversion.

So why give it a separate name? Because its function differs:

- **Non-aversion**: countering "the anger that ends a being's life"—the anger that drives killing; what it gives is **happiness**.
- **Non-harming**: directly opposing "the injury that distresses a being"—the act of harming sentient beings; what it removes is **suffering**.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* defines the difference in eight precise words: **"Non-aversion bestows happiness; non-harming removes suffering."** These eight words are precisely the complete definition of loving-kindness and compassion—**loving-kindness** is the bestowing of happiness (non-aversion), and **compassion** is the removing of suffering (non-harming).

Why pull non-harming out of non-aversion as its own factor? The *Cheng Weishi Lun* gives three reasons: harming "occurs frequently" (acts that distress sentient beings happen often); harming "obstructs the supreme cause of the highest vehicle" (it obstructs the most fundamental cause of buddhahood, which is great compassion); and the fault of harming needs to be specifically recognized. The three reasons together amount to pulling the function of "removing suffering" out from "bestowing happiness," so that it becomes an independent pillar among the mental factors of the bodhisattva path.

The *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* gives non-harming the highest position: **"Great compassion is the root of all the merit of all buddhas and bodhisattvas, the mother of prajñā-pāramitā, the grandmother of all buddhas."** Prajñā is the mother of buddhas, and great compassion is the mother of prajñā. The final orientation of the bodhisattva path is not one's own awakening but the removal of all sentient beings' suffering. Non-harming is where that ultimate orientation comes to rest at the level of mental factors.

### Two Common Misreadings

Having walked through the eleven once, there are two misreadings that need correcting.

⚠️ **Wholesome dharmas are not a virtue checklist.** Many people understand the wholesome factors as "eleven good qualities"—picking a few to cultivate as if choosing courses. This breaks an organic structure into fragments. The eleven are one formula: trust is the spark, conscience and moral fear are the seal, the three wholesome roots are the core fuel, vigor is the pressure, pliancy is the efficiency, non-negligence and equanimity are the regulators, and non-harming is the destination. Take away one, and the whole engine cannot run. Practice is not cultivating a few of them; it is letting the whole set run together.

⚠️ **Wholesome dharmas do not appear automatically; they must be actively cultivated.** Chapter 3.7 said the universals are "necessarily present"—those mental factors are there whether you cultivate them or not. But among the four kinds of universality, the wholesome factors possess only universality across grounds; they lack universality across times and universality across simultaneous arising—they do not automatically actualize in every moment, nor in every consciousness. This means: **every single arising of wholesome mind is the result of practice.** Without cultivation, no wholesome factor is present. This is why the Āgamas stress that "non-negligence is the root of all wholesome dharmas"—a wholesome dharma left unattended will regress, and practice is precisely not letting it regress.

With this, the fuel set that runs toward the light is fully introduced. But a car's tank, it turns out, has two supplies—one that, once added, lets it run toward liberation, and another that, once added, makes it slide toward the depths of saṃsāra.

In the next chapter, 3.9, we open that other tank: the **six fundamental afflictions**—craving, aversion, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong views. Along with them we take up the four **indeterminate mental factors**—regret, sleep, applied thought, and sustained examination—whose wholesome or unwholesome character is not yet fixed, deciding their direction by the situation.
