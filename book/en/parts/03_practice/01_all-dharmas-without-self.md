## 3.1 All Dharmas Are Without Self—The Hundred Dharmas Are a Map of Practice

Part One asked you: "Who is driving?" Part Two let you see it: the five aggregates are all empty. Part Three takes the car apart and lets you look closely at every component—and then you discover: **there is no driver inside the car.**

### A Little Treatise of Seven Hundred and Twenty-Two Characters

Vasubandhu's *Śatadharmaprakāśamukha-śāstra*—the Treatise on the Doors Illuminated by a Hundred Dharmas—runs to only seven hundred and twenty-two Chinese characters. It opens with two questions: "What are all dharmas? What is meant by being without self?" The first question unfolds into the classification of the five categories of the hundred dharmas—consciousnesses, mental factors, form, dissociated formations, unconditioned dharmas. The second question is answered at the close, drawn together by the "twofold selflessness"—the selflessness of person (*pudgala-nairātmya*) and the selflessness of dharmas (*dharma-nairātmya*).

It begins with "all dharmas are without self" and ends with "the twofold selflessness." Seven hundred and twenty-two characters, a precise closed loop.

Its full title gives away its origin: "A Summary Enumeration from the Section on Essential Topics"—it is a condensed version of the six hundred and sixty dharmas in Asaṅga's *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*. Six hundred and sixty was too many; one hundred is just right: precise enough to guide practice, compact enough to remember.

### "All Dharmas Are Without Self" Is Not a Yogācāra Invention

This phrase has its root in the Āgamas—the Buddha's earliest teachings. The *Ekottarāgama* sums up the framework of the Dharma in four dharma foundations: all formations are impermanent, all formations are suffering, **all dharmas are without self**, cessation is nirvāṇa. It is worth noticing that the first two speak of "formations" (limited to conditioned dharmas), while the third speaks of "dharmas" (taking in everything, including the unconditioned)—the reach of "without self" is wider than that of "impermanent."

But the Buddha's stance toward the question of "having a self" or "having no self" was the Middle Way. The *Saṃyuktāgama* records that when a wanderer asked the Buddha "is there a self?" and "is there no self?", the Buddha gave no answer to either. Afterward he explained to Ānanda: to answer "there is a self" would feed wrong views; to answer "there is no self" would deepen confusion—"the Tathāgata departs from both extremes and teaches the Dharma standing in the middle."

The hundred dharmas inherit exactly this Middle Way spirit. They do not proclaim "there is no self"—that would be the annihilationist view. They display the operating mechanism of "self": when you see clearly how a hundred components come together through conditions, the "fixed, unchanging self" dissolves of its own accord, while the "functioning of conditioned cooperation" goes on working.

### The Arrangement of the Five Categories Is Itself the Path of Practice

After listing the five categories, the treatise explains the logic of their order in a single line: "Because [consciousnesses] are supreme among all; because [mental factors] are associated with them; because [form] is the shadow projected by the first two; because [dissociated formations] are positional distinctions of the first three; because [unconditioned dharmas] are what the first four reveal. Such is the sequence."

This is not only a classificatory note. The "sequence" is itself a road that runs from consciousness to suchness:

**Consciousnesses—supreme among all.** Mind-consciousness is the root of all experience. Practice begins with knowing your own mind. The *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* says "all that exists in the three realms is only one mind" and "all is made by mind alone"; the treatise's placement of the consciousnesses in first position is the systematic unfolding of exactly this point.

**Mental factors—associated with mind.** Mental factors are the "content" of mind—fifty-one kinds of psychological quality, from the neutral to the wholesome to the afflictive. To know the mental factors is to know your own patterns of reaction.

**Form—shadow projected by the first two.** The world you see is not an independently existing objective reality, but the "projected image" of mind and mental factors. This step breaks the dualism of "mind inside, world outside."

**Dissociated formations—positional distinctions of the first three.** Time, space, quantity, gain and loss, arising and ceasing—these concepts you take to exist objectively are in fact provisional designations of the activity of consciousness. This step breaks the sense that concepts are real things.

**Unconditioned—what the first four reveal.** The first four categories are all conditioned (made through causes and conditions). The fifth category is unconditioned (not made). What the first four "reveal" is precisely suchness. After working analytically through all the conditioned dharmas, what the practitioner arrives at is not a new discovery but this: suchness has been here all along.

The five categories begin with consciousness and end with suchness. The hundred dharmas are not a list; they are a road.

### The Three Transformations: The Eight Consciousnesses Are Not Eight Containers

The first of the five categories, the consciousnesses, comprises the eight consciousnesses. But the eight consciousnesses are not eight independent containers. They are one organic whole, operating through the dynamic structure of "three transformations."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* cites the *Triṃśikā* (Thirty Verses): "They depend on transformations of consciousness. These transformations are of three kinds only: called maturation, deliberation, and the discernment of objects."

The first transformation is the ālaya-consciousness (the deep storehouse of mind), the fundamental storehouse of all seeds, which "turns ceaselessly like a torrent"—uninterrupted, yet changing in every instant. The second transformation is the manas-consciousness, the automatic mechanism that ceaselessly grasps the ālaya-consciousness as "self," the engine of self-grasping running twenty-four hours a day. The third transformation is the first six consciousnesses, the six cognitive functions you can directly observe.

The interaction of the three transformations forms a cycle: the ālaya-consciousness holds the seeds; the seeds meet conditions and manifest as the activity of the first seven consciousnesses; the activity of the first seven consciousnesses then perfumes the ālaya-consciousness back into new seeds. This cycle of "seed → manifestation → perfuming → seed" is the basic operating mechanism of life. Chapter 3.3 will unfold this structure in full.

### The Method of Contemplation: The Three Natures

The hundred dharmas tell you which dharmas there are, but knowing the list is not enough—you need a method for looking. That method is the three-nature contemplation (the imagined, dependent, and perfected natures), whose scriptural source the Buddha gave in the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra*.

For the practitioner of the hundred dharmas, the three natures are three levels of contemplation. The imagined nature—what you *think* this dharma is (fixed, independent, "mine"). The dependent nature—how this dharma actually *operates* (arising through conditions, ceasing moment to moment). The perfected nature—the true face of this dharma (the suchness that shows itself once the imagined overlay is removed).

The *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* uses the analogy of a crystal to make the relationship plain: the crystal appears blue, red, green, or gold when placed near colored dyes (the imagined nature); the crystal itself manifests according to conditions (the dependent nature); and the false colors never truly existed in the crystal (the perfected nature). The "blue crystal" you see is not a fabrication—it arises through conditions—but "blue" is not the nature of the crystal.

Every one of the hundred dharmas can be contemplated on these three levels. The three natures are not merely theory; they are the eyes through which you look at the hundred dharmas. The prajñā of Part Two gave you the eyes to see emptiness; the three natures of Part Three give you the eyes to recognize the structure of conditions—and these two pairs of eyes are not two things, but two aspects of one and the same contemplation.

### The Ultimate Aim: The Transformation of the Basis

The analytical work of the hundred dharmas is not done for the sake of classification—its ultimate goal is the transformation of the basis (*āśraya-parāvṛtti*).

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* opens by laying bare the whole chain of cause and effect: **confusion → understanding → severance → attainment.** Confused about what? About the twofold emptiness (the emptiness of person and the emptiness of dharmas). Understanding what? Consciousness-only. Severing what? The twofold obstruction (the afflictive obstruction and the cognitive obstruction). Attaining what? The twofold fruit (liberation and awakening). The classificatory work of the hundred dharmas serves the first step of this chain—freeing you from confusion about how "self" and "dharmas" actually operate.

The definition of the transformation of the basis is breathtakingly precise: the basis is the dependent nature (the conditioned operation of consciousness, always there); what is turned away from is the imagined nature (your mistaken cognition of consciousness); what is turned toward is the perfected nature (the true face of consciousness).

The transformation of the basis is not the building of a new mind, nor the destruction of the old consciousness. It is, on the very same mind, a change in your relationship with that mind. The hundred dharmas are still those same hundred dharmas, but you no longer take them to be "mine," "fixed," or "inherently real."

Two misunderstandings arise easily here. First, supposing that memorizing a hundred terms amounts to "having learned Yogācāra." The hundred dharmas are a tool, not an exam—a hundred terms are only the entry point, just as memorizing the name of every muscle does not make you able to diagnose patients. The value of the hundred dharmas lies in awareness: the moment in daily life when you recognize "ah, this is restlessness," "this is conceit at work," that is when the hundred dharmas begin to function. The terms are the finger; awareness is the moon. And the entire toolkit so carefully assembled across Part Three will, by the time of the Diamond Sūtra in Part Five, be identified as "a provisional designation on the dependent nature"—the toolkit is still there, but your relationship to it will have changed completely. There is no need to rush to put it down now. First cross the river; then set down the raft.

Second, supposing that "all dharmas are consciousness-only" means that mind can alter matter, that thinking makes it so. The "only" in consciousness-only is an epistemological position: all experience is inseparable from the participation of consciousness; you have never contacted anything "outside of consciousness." It does not deny the causal role of external conditions, nor does it claim that mind can manipulate matter at will. What consciousness-only breaks is the attachment to "an independent reality existing outside the mind"; it does not set up the new attachment that "mind can create everything."

But before we take apart these hundred parts, there is an earlier question to ask first—this hundred-dharma map is itself an enlargement of an older map the Buddha drew before it; and for one and the same body-mind, he drew more than that one. Why? Chapter 3.2, "The Three Schemata," answers this first.

Only then do we take apart the first region in earnest—the dynamic panorama of the eight consciousnesses. How do the three transformations work together? How does the ālaya-consciousness hold the seeds, how does the manas-consciousness grasp at "self," how do the first six consciousnesses produce karma?
