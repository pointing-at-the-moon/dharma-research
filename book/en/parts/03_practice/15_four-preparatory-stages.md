## 3.15 The Four Preparatory Stages — From Knowing to Seeing

You have finished reading 3.14.

Close the book. You have now walked the whole map of the hundred dharmas that this book lays out—the eight mind dharmas, the fifty-one mental factors, the eleven form dharmas, the twenty-four dharmas not associated with mind, the six unconditioned dharmas. Every one of them you have understood. You can state the proposition "all dharmas are without self," you can derive "the perfected nature is suchness," and you have passed through the twofold distinction of "the true nature of consciousness-only."

Then you remember the last line of 3.14—

**Reading and understanding ≠ realizing.**

And so the most direct question of all rises up: how do you cross the middle? From "understanding that all dharmas are without self" to "personally seeing that all dharmas are without self," from "grasping the doctrine of suchness" to "suchness manifest within non-discriminating wisdom"—what exactly is this distance? How is it crossed?

This chapter exists to answer that question.

Yogācāra gives this stretch of road a precise name: **the path of application**. Before it lies **the path of accumulation** (gathering the fuel); in the middle lies **the path of application** (four progressively deepening stages: heat, summit, patience, supreme worldly dharma); after it comes **the path of penetration** (the path of seeing). The end of this road is suchness—but suchness is not reached by "arriving at" it. As we will see, this road is more like **drilling for fire**: you drill and drill the wood, and at a certain instant the flame bursts out of itself.

Before unfolding the four preparatory stages, we have to deal with one premise that modern practice culture most underestimates—the path of accumulation.

### The Path of Accumulation: Until the Fire Comes, You Keep Drilling

When a modern reader hears the words "path of application," the ears tend to prick up: ah, so there is a concrete method. The reflexive next move is to want to jump straight in—I've finished the hundred dharmas, so let me start practicing the four investigations.

That is jumping into the wrong place.

In the Yogācāra five-stage path, **the stage before the path of application is the path of accumulation**. "Accumulation"—the Sanskrit *saṃbhāra*—simply means "fuel." A car without fuel will not move, and without it the contemplative insight of the four preparatory stages will not start either.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, puts the matter without any softening:

> The bodhisattva at this stage… though deeply trusting and understanding the meaning of consciousness-only, cannot yet comprehend the emptiness of the apprehended and the apprehender. Dwelling mostly outside the gate, practicing the bodhisattva path externally, the practitioner has not yet the power to suppress or eliminate the latent tendencies produced by the dual grasping.

"Deeply trusting and understanding, yet unable to comprehend the emptiness of apprehended and apprehender"—the bodhisattva at the path of accumulation has no doubt about the Yogācāra teaching, but **conviction is not realization**. "Dwelling mostly outside the gate, practicing the bodhisattva path externally"—still "outside the gate," accumulating through the six perfections the capital needed to enter. There is a precise measure in that line: the path of accumulation is not "not practicing." It is **always practicing**, but what it practices is "the preparation before entering," not "the contemplative insight after entering."

Kuiji, in his *Commentary on the Diamond Sūtra*, uses a vivid image:

> Though in the three stages of wise practice one has unsystematically cultivated the six perfections for one incalculable eon, to enter the holy path requires the Buddha's impetus. Therefore, after the path of accumulation, the name "application" is established. It is like drilling for fire—when the fire is about to emerge, one redoubles one's effort.

"Like drilling for fire"—the whole of practice is fire-drilling. **The path of accumulation is the long, sustained drilling of the wood**; the path of application is the final intensification as the fire is about to emerge; the path of seeing is the instant the flame bursts forth. Without the hours—even the years—of drilling beforehand, that final intensification will not bring out the fire.

The path of accumulation has at least three layers to lay down.

**First, completeness of study and reflection.** The classification of the hundred dharmas, the structure of the three transformations of consciousness, the doctrine of the three natures and the three absences of nature—these must be so well integrated that you can draw on them and deploy them flexibly at any moment in meditation. Not "more or less remembered," but **an instinct of thought**. What 3.1 through 3.14 unfolds is one part of this study content, but **finishing the reading is far from enough**—you must reflect on it again and again, integrate it thoroughly, until the knowledge turns from "information stored in memory" into "a structure present in your reactions."

**Second, the actual ground of discipline and concentration.** The observations of the four preparatory stages are carried out within meditative absorption. Without a stable capacity for calm-abiding and insight, you cannot, on the cushion, even keep tracking "name, referent, intrinsic nature, distinctions"—within five minutes the mind wanders off. This is not a matter of technique; it is that you simply **cannot enter that environment** at all. Asking someone who cannot swim to do precision underwater work in deep water—no amount of fine technical instruction will help.

**Third, the genuine arising of bodhicitta and the aspiration for liberation.** The five Yogācāra stages describe the path of the Mahāyāna bodhisattva. The practitioner at the path of accumulation is not "a scholar interested in Buddhism," but someone who has genuinely awakened the aspiration to seek Buddhahood above and benefit all beings below, together with a deep revulsion toward the suffering of cyclic existence. This motive is the engine of the whole road—without it, even if contemplative insight arises briefly, it lacks the momentum to keep deepening.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says the practitioner at the path of accumulation often meets **three forms of discouragement**: shrinking upon hearing how vast awakening is, shrinking upon hearing how hard the six perfections are to practice, shrinking upon hearing how nearly impossible the transformation of the basis is to realize. The remedy is called **three temperings**—taking those who have already accomplished it as a mirror, examining one's own aspiration, using others' coarser goodness to encourage one's own finer causes. None of this is the least bit archaic; everyone who seriously faces the path goes through this psychological cycle.

Here we make the first—and this chapter's most important—correction: ⚠️ **having studied the hundred dharmas ≠ being able to practice the four preparatory stages straight away.**

In the scriptures the temporal span of the path of accumulation is vast—"one great incalculable eon." The literal number does not matter; the message does: **the foundation cannot be skipped.** Modern people are used to fast-tracking, but the Yogācāra path tells you plainly that forcing the contemplations of the path of application while your provisions are insufficient will not yield the genuine contemplative insight of the four investigations. **It will only yield a heap of "I have already contemplated emptiness" imagined-nature**—one more layer of grasping, more troublesome than not having practiced at all.

This is also why this chapter sits fourteenth in S3, not earlier. The reader must first walk the whole study and reflection of the hundred dharmas before having the ground to understand what the four preparatory stages are talking about. And from "understanding the four preparatory stages" to "practicing the four preparatory stages," there is still a long road in between.

### The Whole Map of the Four Stages: First Empty the Apprehended, Then Empty the Apprehender

With the groundwork of the path of accumulation laid, we can now look at the four stages of the path of application.

First, an overview. The four preparatory stages are **four progressively deepening phases of one and the same path of contemplative insight**, not four meditation techniques you can pick up separately. Their shared work is one thing: using the **four investigations** to observe the name, referent, intrinsic nature, and distinctions of the hundred dharmas—seeing that all four are conventional designations—then using the **four kinds of knowing-as-it-is** to confirm this, until at last both "the apprehended" and "the apprehender" are seen through.

The four investigations are four aspects of the hundred dharmas:

- **Investigation of name**—observing that the "name" of the hundred dharmas (the name-label) is only a linguistic convention. The name "ālaya-vijñāna" is not the actual operation of the ālaya-vijñāna; the tag "craving" is not the activity of the mental factor craving itself.
- **Investigation of referent**—observing that the "referent" a name points to (its object-meaning) is also a construction of consciousness. We assume the name points to an independently existing "thing," but that "thing" is itself a projection of consciousness.
- **Investigation of the conventional designation of intrinsic nature**—observing that the "intrinsic nature" defined for each dharma is a conventional designation. "The intrinsic nature of contact is the bringing together of faculty, object, and consciousness"—this definition is a teaching convenience, not the "true face" of contact.
- **Investigation of the conventional designation of distinctions**—observing that the "distinctions" between one dharma and another are also conventional designations. Wholesome and unwholesome, conditioned and unconditioned—these classifications are coordinates on the map, not the terrain itself.

These four, taken together, are the complete structure of the "imagined nature" discussed in 3.13—**the imagined-nature character of all dharmas just is the conventional designation of name, referent, intrinsic nature, and distinctions.** The *Sandhinirmocana Sūtra* defines it directly: "The imagined character of all dharmas is the conventional establishment of all dharmas through **name, intrinsic nature, and distinctions**, to the extent that it enables verbal expression." What the four investigations look at is exactly this.

The four investigations "see" the conventional designation; the four kinds of knowing-as-it-is "confirm" the conventional designation—not only seeing, but **affirming without regression**. This is the key difference between the two.

Then the combined effect of the two is put most precisely by the *Madhyāntavibhāga*:

> Depending on consciousness, there is something apprehended; from that, the non-apprehending of objects arises;
> depending on the non-apprehending of objects, the non-apprehending of consciousness arises.

Turn it over: first, on the basis of "consciousness exists," you infer "objects do not exist" (emptiness of the apprehended); then, from "objects do not exist," you derive "consciousness too does not exist" (emptiness of the apprehender). Because apprehender and apprehended are **established in mutual dependence**—without an object being cognized, cognition itself loses its ground for independent existence. Just as without "left" there is no "right," without the side that is seen, "the seeing side" cannot stand on its own.

This sequence—**first empty the apprehended, then empty the apprehender**—is the skeleton of the entire path of application. All four stages do this one thing; only the depth differs.

### Heat, Summit, Patience, Supreme Worldly Dharma: One Fire at Four Intensities

Now we can enter the four stages themselves.

**Heat**—drilling brings warmth; the fire has not yet come, but the temperature is rising. Within the "luminosity-attaining concentration" (the meditative state in which the light of wisdom is first obtained), the practitioner, for the first time, uses the four investigations to observe that name, referent, intrinsic nature, and distinctions are conventional designations—not learned from books, but **directly touched within observation**.

The *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* describes this moment: "One sees only mental verbalization, does not see any meaning." The practitioner begins to see: what we took to be cognition of "things themselves" has all along been cognition of "the mental verbalizing-and-discriminating about things." This insight, though not yet stable (regression is still possible), has planted the wholesome root—"heat" is precisely this state of "not yet burning but already warming."

**Summit**—like climbing to the peak of a mountain. The contemplative insight grows more acute and penetrating. The concentration it relies on is called "luminosity-increasing concentration"—the light of wisdom intensifies. The subtlety of the summit stage is that it is **the critical point between advance and retreat**: the insight is already strong, but it has not yet reached irreversibility. Like standing on a mountain peak, you can keep going forward into patience, or you can slip back through the interference of afflictions. Vasubandhu, glossing "summit" in the *Madhyāntavibhāga-bhāṣya*, gives three meanings—the summit can sustain bodily life (the wholesome roots can no longer be severed), the summit is the boundary of retreat (one can fall back), the summit is the boundary of advance (one can ascend). All three meanings coexist, and that is the true condition of the summit stage.

**Patience**—this is the most decisive step among the four preparatory stages.

"Patience" here is not the patience of "endurance"; it is **acceptance, affirmation**—a definitive embracing of the certainty of truth. Within the "truth-conforming concentration," the practitioner deepens from the four investigations into the four kinds of knowing-as-it-is, giving rise to an **irreversible affirmation** of "the emptiness of the apprehended."

The *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra* says: "All meanings are nothing but light; because one sees only mind, **one attains the cessation of the confusion of the imagined**." The "confusion of the imagined" is the deluded cognition that "external objects exist independently." This confusion is severed—and from then on one never reverts to the cognitive mode of naïve realism. This is not an emotional "believing" but a wisdom-based "seeing"; from this point onward, even in daily life, you will not truly forget that "verbal concepts are not the same as reality." The *Abhidharmakośa* states the decisiveness of the patience stage in the briefest terms: "**Because there is no regression, it is named the patience dharma.**"

**Supreme worldly dharma**—the most excellent among worldly phenomena. Because in the very next instant, it will transcend the worldly and enter the path of seeing.

The practitioner at this stage, within the "uninterrupted concentration," on the basis of having affirmed the emptiness of the apprehended, goes further and affirms **the emptiness of the apprehender**—not only is "the object being cognized" empty, but even "the cognizing subject"—that very mental activity which is observing the hundred dharmas and determining consciousness-only—is also not an independently existing reality.

The supreme worldly dharma lasts **only a single instant**. The *Abhidharmasamuccaya* says: "Because the supreme worldly dharma is by nature a single instant that necessarily does not continue, at the very moment of its arising one definitively enters the direct realization." It is not that the practitioner stops here; it is that this single instant itself cannot stop—once it arises, it necessarily passes without interruption into the path of seeing. The *Yogācārabhūmi* fills the tension of this instant to the brim with the fire-drilling image:

> Suppose someone, wishing to use fire for fire's purposes, seeking fire, places dry wood below and a drill above, and with diligence, effort, and valor drills on… As flameless fire suddenly bursts forth, so too should one understand the supreme worldly dharma.

"Suddenly bursts forth"—all at once. After drilling for so long, at this instant, the fire comes out.

Then the *Cheng Weishi Lun* draws the boundary between the path of application and the path of seeing dead-firm with a pair of contrasting verses:

> Path of application: "What is set before the mind as something small, taken to be the nature of consciousness-only—because there is still something apprehended, one does not truly abide in consciousness-only."
> Path of penetration: "When, regarding the object of contemplation, wisdom has nothing whatsoever apprehended, then one abides in consciousness-only, because one has left behind the characteristic of the dual grasping."

The practitioner at the path of application "sets up something small"—building in the mind the cognition "this is consciousness-only." But precisely because there is still a "something small" being set up, there is still "something apprehended"; and as long as there is something apprehended, one does not yet "truly abide in consciousness-only." The non-discriminating wisdom of the path of seeing is **wisdom with nothing whatsoever apprehended regarding its object**—even the cognition "consciousness-only" itself is set down, subject and object both vanish, and suchness is directly realized.

This is the root difference between the path of application and the path of seeing: **the path of application is "consciousness-only with apprehension," the path of seeing is "consciousness-only without apprehension."**

"With apprehension" is not an error—it is the necessary road. You must first establish a firm contemplative understanding of "everything is consciousness-only" (with something apprehended) before you can, in the next step, transcend that very understanding (with nothing apprehended). Just as you must first learn to use the map before, one day, you can set the map down and face the terrain directly.

### "During the Preparatory Phase It Extends Through All the Absorptions; It Reaches Completion Relying on the Fourth"

Here we have to deal with a technical question on which modern practitioners often get stuck: **how deep does the meditative absorption have to be before these contemplations can even start?**

The original wording in the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, fascicle nine, runs:

> The bodhisattva, in developing these wholesome roots of heat and the rest, **though during the preparatory phase [the practice] extends through all the meditative absorptions**, reaches completion relying on the fourth absorption, because one enters the path of seeing relying on the most excellent basis.

This passage has two layers; modern readers often see only the second and miss the first.

**The first layer: "during the preparatory phase it extends through all the absorptions."** In the preparatory phase of practice, the contemplative insight of the four stages **can operate within any meditative absorption**. You do not need to first attain the fourth dhyāna before you can begin the four-investigation contemplation. Whatever level your meditative skill rests at—even just the access concentration before the first dhyāna, or a relatively stable state within desire-realm concentration—as long as you have basic calm-abiding and insight capacity, you can begin to observe the conventionally designated character of name, referent, intrinsic nature, and distinctions.

**The second layer: "it reaches completion relying on the fourth."** The final "completion"—that instant when the supreme worldly dharma passes without interruption into the path of seeing—requires the exceptional stability of the fourth dhyāna as its basis. The fourth dhyāna's "equanimity and purity of mindfulness," free from the joy and pleasure that stir the first three dhyānas, is best suited to bear the extremely subtle contemplative insight of the dual emptiness of subject and object.

Reading both layers together: **starting does not require the fourth dhyāna; completion does.**

So here we make the second correction: ⚠️ **"I haven't attained the fourth dhyāna, so I shouldn't practice contemplation" and "I don't need a foundation, I'll just practice whenever I want"—both extremes deviate from the middle way.** The former takes the ultimate standard as the entry threshold; the latter takes the entry threshold as something that can be skipped—one too high, one too low, and neither is the real situation.

The real situation is this: you can start drilling today. As you drill, your arms grow stronger and the wood grows hotter. "It reaches completion relying on the fourth" describes the condition needed at the moment the flame bursts forth, not the condition needed when you pick up the drill.

For the modern practitioner whose meditative depth is not yet up to standard, the real question is not "Am I qualified to practice the four preparatory stages?" but "Have I, in daily life, **begun to practice awareness of the conventionally designated nature of verbal concepts?**"

You do not need deep meditative absorption to notice your own automatic reactions to labels like "good," "bad," "success," "failure." **Each time you become aware that "this is a verbal designation, not the thing itself," you are doing a micro-practice in the spirit of the four investigations.** Provisions are accumulated exactly like this, one moment at a time. The strength in the drilling arm is trained up exactly like this, one moment at a time.

### The Hundred Dharmas Are the Map; the Four Stages Are the Walking

Back to the original question: reading and understanding ≠ realizing. How do you cross the middle?

Having walked through this chapter, the answer is clear.

The hundred dharmas are the **necessary map**—without them, you would not know what you are observing. "Affliction," "form dharmas," "mental factors," "suchness"—without the doctrine laid out across the previous thirteen chapters, these words are only linguistic labels and cannot move anything in observation. But the map must in the end be transcended—**what the four preparatory stages teach you is to see through, while holding the map in your hands, the reality that the map points toward.**

Heat is seeing that the marks on the map are in fact only marks (name and referent are conventional designations); summit is this seeing-through growing ever more acute; patience is affirming that "the terrain is not the map" (the emptiness of the apprehended, without regression); supreme worldly dharma is seeing that even the self "reading the map" is not an entity (the emptiness of the apprehender).

After that one instant, the map slips from your hands. You have lost nothing—the terrain is still there. It is only that from now on you no longer need the map, because **you are already standing in the terrain.**

✅ The whole road is not for reaching a new place; it is to see for yourself: the place you have always been in is suchness.

Here we make the final correction: ⚠️ **the four preparatory stages are not four meditation techniques that can be picked up separately**, the way you might choose "mindfulness of breathing" or "loving-kindness meditation" as standalone modules to train. They are four progressively deepening phases of one path of contemplative insight. If heat is unstable, you cannot enter summit; if the four investigations are not matured, the four kinds of knowing-as-it-is cannot arise; without the patience stage's affirmation of the emptiness of the apprehended, the supreme worldly dharma's emptiness of the apprehender cannot follow. **This is not a combination of techniques; it is the organic growth of wisdom.**

The next chapter—**3.16 Transforming Consciousness into Wisdom: Coming Home**—takes up the question: once the fire bursts forth, what then? The path of seeing is only the beginning, not the end. After the path of seeing there is still the path of cultivation across the ten grounds, and still the path of completion in the fruit of Buddhahood. **The four wisdoms in full clarity**, the transformation of the basis across the eight consciousnesses, the move from effortlessness to the universal deliverance of all—that is the full landscape of the entire road.

If 3.14 told you where home is, and 3.15 told you how to walk there, what 3.16 has to tell you is: once you are home, what you will have become. The last chapter of S3 gathers the whole journey back to the most ancient of Buddhist promises—**afflictions are themselves awakening, birth-and-death is itself nirvāṇa; not going to a new place, but becoming who you originally are.**
