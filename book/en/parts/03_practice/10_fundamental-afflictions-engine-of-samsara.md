## 3.10 The Fundamental Afflictions and the Indeterminates: The Engine of Saṃsāra

In 3.9 we finished with the group of fuels that drives the vehicle toward liberation. But the fuel tank actually holds two sets, and this is the other one—the cluster of impurities that makes the vehicle slide deeper into saṃsāra.

### The Six Fundamental Afflictions Are Not a List, but a Structure

The Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas lists six fundamental afflictions: **craving, aversion, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong views**. Just like the eleven wholesome mental factors, these six are not isolated negative qualities. They are an **organic system of defilement**, with its own layers and structure:

- **Delusion (ignorance)** is the root—the deepest ground on which all defilement rests; the other five afflictions all grow out of it.
- **Craving and aversion** are the twin wings—one reaches outward to grasp (craving), one pushes outward to repel (aversion); one pull and one push make up the basic dynamic of saṃsāra.
- **Conceit and doubt** are the subtle obstacles—harder to notice than craving and aversion, and harder to sever as well.
- **Wrong views** are the systematic error at the level of cognition—they unfold into five kinds: view of self, extreme view, wrong view, view-grasping, and grasping at rules-and-observances.

Continuing the vehicle metaphor from 3.8 and 3.9: if the wholesome mental factors are the clean fuel that drives the vehicle toward the light, the fundamental afflictions are the impurities mixed into that fuel—each kind throws the engine off in a different way, but added together they push it all in one direction: **saṃsāra**.

### Delusion: The Root of Saṃsāra

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* gives a remarkably precise definition of delusion: **"Darkness regarding all principles and things."** A fundamental darkness toward both things and principles—this is the core of delusion.

Delusion is not "stupidity" (that is a matter of intelligence), nor "lack of education" (that is a matter of information). Delusion is a kind of **darkness at the level of existence itself**: being unclear about "who I am," unclear about "how the world works," unclear about "the relationship of cause and effect," unclear about "impermanence, suffering, emptiness, non-self"—these fundamental unclarities are delusion.

Delusion is judged to be "the basis of all contamination"—every other affliction takes delusion as its ground. Without delusion, craving cannot arise (craving presupposes the illusion that "this thing has the value I want"); without delusion, aversion cannot arise (aversion presupposes the illusion that "this is an enemy harming me"). The five wrong views take "defiled wisdom" as their very substance—every distorted way of knowing grows out of delusion.

This is why delusion is placed first. It is not the "most severe" of the six afflictions (in terms of severity, aversion is judged exclusively unwholesome, while delusion runs across both the unwholesome and the morally neutral), but it is the **most fundamental** one—the other afflictions cannot be uprooted because the root is still there.

### Craving and Aversion: The Twin Wings

Craving and aversion are the most direct driving forces of saṃsāra. The *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s definitions are remarkably symmetrical:

- **Craving**: "Attachment to existence and its conditions; its nature is defiled clinging; its function is to obstruct non-attachment and produce suffering."—a powerful adhesion to existence and the resources that sustain existence.
- **Aversion**: "Resentful hostility toward suffering and its sources; its nature is hostility; it obstructs non-aversion, generates agitation, and serves as the basis for evil conduct."—a powerful resistance to painful situations and the conditions that bring them about.

**The structure is perfectly symmetrical**: craving is the force that "pulls toward," aversion is the force that "pushes away." One pull and one push, and the wheel of saṃsāra begins to turn.

The defining feature of these twin wings is that they can **arise spontaneously**—they need no deliberate thinking; they can arise on the spot. See delicious food and craving arises directly; bump into something hard and aversion arises directly—the first five consciousnesses already carry craving and aversion (along with the delusion they jointly depend on), with no need for the sixth consciousness to reason it out. When the *Cheng Weishi Lun* determines that "the five consciousnesses have only three: craving, aversion, and delusion—because they lack discrimination," this is exactly its point.

But spontaneous does not mean out of control. Precisely because craving and aversion can arise spontaneously on the spot, the very first checkpoint of practice is this: **in the instant that craving or aversion arises, can you be aware of it?** If you can be aware, then craving and aversion arise and that is all—like a gust of wind passing through. If you cannot be aware, craving and aversion link up into thought after thought of continuous emotion, then issue forth as speech and bodily action, and fall onto the assembly line of karma.

### Conceit and Doubt: The Subtle Obstacles

Among the six afflictions, conceit and doubt are harder to sever than craving and aversion. Their mode of operation is finer, and they often disguise themselves as something else—conceit can pass itself off as "reasonable self-confidence," and doubt can pass itself off as "rational caution."

On the tenacity of conceit in practice, the Āgamas record a deeply moving story—**the testimony of the Elder Khemaka**.

Khemaka was gravely ill, and fellow monks came to visit him, asking whether he had seen the Dharma. Khemaka answered: "I no longer regard form as self, feeling as self, perception as self, formations as self, consciousness as self—the five aggregates are self, the self possesses the five aggregates, the five aggregates are within the self, the self is within the five aggregates—all of these I have seen through." At the level of view, he was entirely correct.

But he went on to say: "Yet regarding the five appropriated aggregates, self-conceit, self-desire, and self-tendency remain uncut, unknown, unabandoned, unvomited."—that current of "self-conceit," that current of "self-desire," that "habitual sense of mine" upon the five aggregates was still there. He explained it with an image: the cloth has already been washed clean, but the fragrance of flowers still lingers.

Khemaka then described the remedy: **continue, upon the five aggregates, to "advance in contemplating their arising and passing away."** Continuously and repeatedly observe the arising and passing of each feeling, each perception, each formation—until self-conceit, self-desire, and self-tendency are all eliminated.

The power of this story lies in how clearly it distinguishes two levels—**"seeing"** (correct understanding at the level of view) and **"severing"** (thorough clearing at the level of affective and habitual residue). Khemaka's "seeing" was already in place, but his "severing" still needed time. This is the classic distinction that Yogācāra would later develop into **"discriminative arising" and "innate arising."**

Doubt has a similar depth. Doubt is not merely "doubting some particular thing," but a fundamental hesitation toward cause and effect, toward the Three Jewels, toward the path of practice. The Āgamas say the "nutriment" (nourishing cause) of the doubt-hindrance is "unwholesome attention toward the three times"—vacillation regarding past, present, and future. The "non-nutriment" (antidote) of doubt is "contemplation of dependent origination"—not relying on emotional belief, but on accurately observing the structure of cause and effect.

### The Five Wrong Views: Systematic Error at the Level of Cognition

Wrong views unfold into five kinds. These five are not an arbitrary enumeration but five systematic positions of distorted cognition:

- **View of self** (*satkāya-dṛṣṭi*): grasping the five aggregates as self and as belonging to self. This is the most fundamental misidentification of identity—seeing the momentarily arising-and-passing phenomena of body and mind as a stable "I."
- **Extreme view**: grasping the extremes of "eternalism" or "annihilationism." Either believing there is an eternal self (eternalism), or believing that after death there is nothing at all (annihilationism). Both miss the dependently-arisen reality of the Middle Way.
- **Wrong view**: denying cause and effect—denying karmic retribution for good and evil, denying the noble path, denying the existence of the Three Jewels.
- **View-grasping**: clinging to one's own held views as supreme, looking down on other views. Intellectuals are especially apt to get stuck here.
- **Grasping at rules-and-observances**: clinging to a non-cause as cause, a non-path as path—imagining that some ritual, some prohibition, some special practice technique can by itself attain liberation. The austerities of non-Buddhist paths and meaningless taboos all belong to this category.

The common substance of the five views is **"defiled wisdom"**—they all operate upon the "wisdom mental factor," only with the direction wrong. This is why "clever people" are especially prone to fall into the five views: the more refined the reasoning, the harder the distorted view is to shake. The reason the five views are called the "sharp afflictions" (sharper than the "dull afflictions" of craving, aversion, and the rest) is precisely that they take wisdom as their substance—once the direction tilts, the sharp blade wounds in turn.

### Discriminative Arising and Innate Arising: Severance on the Path of Seeing and the Path of Cultivation

All afflictions can be divided into two classes, and this is the distinction in the whole Yogācāra theory of affliction that carries the most guidance for practice:

- **Discriminatively-arisen afflictions**: induced by **external conditions**—hearing a false teacher's doctrine, reading a misguided book, keeping bad company—this kind of affliction "is induced by the force of false teachings and the like," and can be severed by right view at the **path of seeing**.
- **Innately-arisen afflictions**: **arising spontaneously since beginningless time**—needing no external condition, manifesting automatically from the deepest layer of seeds. This kind of affliction can only be gradually cleared through long-term meditation at the **path of cultivation**.

The five views and doubt are **exclusively discriminatively arisen**—they all require distorted reasoning to be constructed, and therefore can also be dismantled through correct reasoning. Craving, aversion, delusion, and conceit run across **both discriminative and innate**—they have versions that are taught (discriminatively arisen) and versions that arise without being taught (innate).

This distinction explains why practice has the two stages of "path of seeing" and "path of cultivation." The stream-enterer at the path of seeing severs the discriminatively-arisen view of self, extreme view, wrong view, view-grasping, grasping at rules-and-observances, and doubt—all errors at the level of view. But the innate portion of conceit, craving, and aversion is still there, and Khemaka is the representative of someone standing at exactly this position. The progress of the path of cultivation is the gradual wearing-away of these innate habitual tendencies—like washing the fragrance of flowers out of a cloth.

⚠️ There is an important correction here: **knowing does not equal severing.** Khemaka had already correctly "seen" that the five aggregates are not self, but his "severing" was still on the way. Having finished this chapter and understood the structure of the six afflictions does not mean those afflictions have vanished in you—understanding is the starting point, not the destination. What an academic paper can do is draw the map; you still have to walk the road step by step yourself.

### The "Four Non-Universalities" of Affliction: The Doctrinal Ground for the Possibility of Practice

In 3.8 we analyzed the "four universalities" of the universal factors (universal in moral quality, in realm, in time, and in concomitance), and in 3.9 we analyzed how the wholesome factors possess only "universality in realm" and lack the other three. The fundamental afflictions of this chapter are **non-universal in all four**—lacking all four:

- **Not universal in moral quality**: afflictions are exclusively defiled—aversion is exclusively unwholesome, the remaining nine run across both the unwholesome and the morally neutral, but never the wholesome.
- **Not universal across realms**: aversion exists only in the desire realm (in the form realm and above it is subdued by the power of concentration, and aversion does not arise); the rest, though running across all three realms, appear in the higher realms only in a faint, morally-neutral form.
- **Not universal in time**: afflictions are intermittent—you are not craving every moment, not angry every moment. (The four afflictions of the manas-consciousness are the exception—they run perpetually and unbroken, but that is the special condition of the seventh consciousness.)
- **Not universal in concomitance**: the ālaya-vijñāna is entirely uncorrelated with affliction (it merely holds seeds and never manifests afflictions); the seventh consciousness has only four, the first five consciousnesses have only three, and only the sixth consciousness runs across all ten.

The doctrinal significance of these "four non-universalities" is enormous: **if afflictions were all-pervading, eternal, and everywhere, practice would have no space.** Precisely because afflictions are conditioned, bounded, intermittent, and limited—because there are places they cannot enter (the ālaya-vijñāna), times when they cannot arise, and corners where they cannot persist—awareness has space, treatment is possible, and transformation has a direction.

⚠️ Here, in passing, a correction of another common misreading: **afflictions are not an enemy to be wiped out.** If you treat afflictions as an enemy, practice becomes a war—and this very attitude of "wanting to wipe out the enemy" is itself aversion. The *Śūraṅgama-sūtra* puts it precisely: "If the host is confused, the guest seizes the advantage."—afflictions are the guest; awareness is the host. The power of affliction comes from your being "confused" about it (identifying with it, rejecting it, resisting it), and the way to treat it is "awareness" (seeing it, not following it, not resisting it). Seeing the structure of affliction is the precondition for transforming affliction.

### The Four Indeterminates: A Neutral Gearbox

Beyond the six afflictions, the Hundred Dharmas also list four **indeterminate mental factors**—regret, sleep, applied thought, and sustained examination. Why are they called "indeterminate"? Because they are neither necessarily wholesome (like the eleven wholesome factors) nor necessarily defiled (like the six afflictions); **their direction depends on the user.**

- **Regret**: remorse over what was done in the past. **Wholesome regret** is repentance—regretting after doing something bad is the starting point of reform. **Unwholesome regret** is regretting the good—regretting after doing something good (for example, begrudging a gift after giving it), which erodes the power of merit. But whether wholesome or unwholesome, regret "obstructs calm"—it pulls the mind back into the past and cannot rest in the present. The emphasis on "letting go" in meditation does not mean never repenting; it means letting go once repentance is complete, otherwise the regret itself becomes an obstacle to meditative stillness.
- **Sleep**: its nature is obscuring and contracting. **Wholesome sleep** is moderate rest restoring energy, a necessary condition for diligence. **Unwholesome sleep** is torpor and excessive sleep, one of the five hindrances. Sleep "obstructs insight"—in a state of torpor one cannot carry out deep and subtle observation.
- **Applied thought** (coarse deliberation) and **sustained examination** (fine deliberation): the coarse and fine thinking functions of the mind. **Wholesome applied thought and sustained examination** is contemplating the Dharma in accordance with principle and observing body and mind. **Unwholesome applied thought and sustained examination** is scattered fantasy and the repeated chewing-over of afflictions. Applied thought and sustained examination "take a portion of volition and wisdom as their substance"—they are not independent entities but the operation of volition and wisdom upon verbalized inner thinking.

Following the vehicle metaphor: the four indeterminate factors are the gearbox. Regret is reverse gear, sleep is neutral, and applied thought and sustained examination are manual shifting. The gears themselves are neither good nor bad; what matters is how they are used.

By this point, the six fundamental afflictions and the four indeterminates have all been walked through once. The six afflictions are the core impurities in the engine of saṃsāra; the four indeterminates are the gearbox—neutral tools that aid practice when used well and obstruct it when used badly.

But there is still one more layer of the affliction system left uncovered: if the fundamental afflictions are the core impurities inside the engine, what about **the exhaust and the derivative problems given off as the engine runs**?—that is what the next chapter takes up, the **twenty subsidiary afflictions**. From the chain reactions of wrath, resentment, spite, concealment, deceit, and flattery, to the meditation obstacles of restlessness, torpor, distraction, and corrupted discernment, the subsidiary afflictions unfold the entire ecology of the affliction system.
