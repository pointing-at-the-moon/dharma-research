## 3.15 Transformation into Wisdom — Coming Home

You are still home.

The sunset of 3.13 has faded. The sky has turned dark, you switched on the lamp, and the whole living room brightened. The sofa, the table and chairs, the photographs on the wall, the distant streetlight beyond the window—everything is no different from what you saw when you first walked in. But in this moment, the way you see them has changed.

This is what this chapter is about.

3.13 told you where home is—suchness is the true nature of consciousness. 3.14 told you how to get there—the path of accumulation, the four preparatory practices, the path of seeing. What 3.15 has to tell you is this: once you are home, what changes.

The part of the answer most likely to surprise you is this: **nothing has changed, and yet everything has changed.**

The eight consciousnesses are still those same eight consciousnesses—no new "enlightened mind" descends from the sky to replace your original cognitive system. Form is still form, mental factors are still mental factors, your eyes and ears and nose and tongue and body and mind go on working just as before. But this same set of eight consciousnesses, once transformed from outflow-laden to outflow-free, **is the four wisdoms.** Not a different system swapped in, but the same system running at a different frequency.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* states this with great precision:

> Wisdom, though not consciousness, transforms in dependence on consciousness. Because consciousness is primary, we speak of attaining wisdom through transforming consciousness. In the outflow-laden state, wisdom is weak and consciousness is strong; in the outflow-free state, wisdom is strong and consciousness is weak. To encourage sentient beings to rely on wisdom and relinquish consciousness, it is said that by transforming the eight consciousnesses one attains these four wisdoms.

Wisdom is not consciousness, but **transforms in dependence on consciousness.** In the outflow-laden state, the power of consciousness outweighs that of wisdom, so affliction has the upper hand; in the outflow-free state, the power of wisdom outweighs that of consciousness, so wisdom has the upper hand. Yogācāra builds its entire system for one purpose: to encourage sentient beings to **rely on wisdom and relinquish consciousness**—not to annihilate consciousness, but to let wisdom take the lead.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* sets down the purpose of its composition at the very opening, and echoes it in a single sentence at the close:

> By transforming affliction one attains mahānirvāṇa; by transforming the cognitive obstruction one realizes supreme awakening. **The establishment of consciousness-only is intended for sentient beings to attain these two fruits of basis-transformation.**

This sentence is the master key to the entire S3 series. Miss this point, and the hundred dharmas are merely a precise psychological taxonomy—interesting, but inconsequential. Grasp it, and the hundred dharmas become **a map for coming home**—where every dharma is not a destination but a waypost.

The work of this chapter is to describe the landscape after the map has been used: **the four wisdoms in full luminosity**, **the two fruits of basis-transformation**, and **the direct realization of the two selflessnesses.**

### The Four Wisdoms—Portraits of the Transformed Eight Consciousnesses

The eight consciousnesses transforming into the four wisdoms is not a matter of eight things being annihilated and replaced by four. It is each of the eight consciousnesses corresponding to itself across two frequencies—outflow-laden and outflow-free—within one and the same substance.

**Eighth consciousness → great perfect mirror wisdom.**

3.3 compared the ālaya-vijñāna to "the hard drive of life"—holding the seeds, receiving the perfuming, the root basis of all. This whole set of functions remains **entirely intact** after transformation. The difference is that the defiled seeds on the hard drive are forever severed, leaving only pure seeds; the manner of holding the seeds shifts from "the attachment-storehouse of self-love" to "purely pure and perfect virtue."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s description wastes no words: "The mind associated with great perfect mirror wisdom—this mind departs from all discrimination; its object and its mode are subtle and difficult to know; it neither forgets nor is confused; toward all the characteristics of objects, its nature and characteristics are pure, departing from all defilement, purely pure and perfect virtue, holding seeds and serving as basis—**like a great round mirror reflecting all forms.**"

"Like a great round mirror reflecting all forms"—the mirror is still a mirror; the dust on its surface has been wiped clean. From "hard drive" to "bright mirror," it is the same eighth consciousness, its quality utterly transformed.

The *Verses on the Structure of the Eight Consciousnesses* gives this transformation a rhyming poem:

> Before the Immovable Ground, the "storehouse" name is at last relinquished; after the vajra path, maturation is emptied. The great mirror, stainless, arises at the same moment, universally illuminating ten directions of dust-mote lands.

Before the eighth **Immovable Ground**, the eighth consciousness relinquishes the name "ālaya" (because the meaning of "the attachment-storehouse of self-love" no longer applies); after the vajra path, even the function of the "maturation consciousness" ceases; finally the great perfect mirror wisdom and the stainless consciousness arise at the same moment, "universally illuminating ten directions of dust-mote lands."

From 3.3's "hard drive of life" to this moment's "the great mirror, stainless, arises at the same moment," it completes the longest single arc of the entire S3 series.

**Seventh consciousness → equality wisdom.**

3.4 explained that the core function of *manas* is **perpetual deliberation**, its content being the grasping of the eighth consciousness's perceiving-aspect as a self. This function remains **unchanged** after transformation—*manas* still deliberates perpetually. What changes is the **content** of the deliberation: from "everything is mine" to "all sentient beings are entirely equal."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says: "The mind associated with equality wisdom—this mind observes all dharmas, self and other, all sentient beings, **as entirely equal.** Great compassion and the like constantly accompany it."

The word "perpetual" is still there—the perpetual of perpetual deliberation. But its object expands from "the self" to "all sentient beings." Self-grasping is not annihilated; it is **turned over**—the same drive that "ceaselessly chases a single target and will not let go," which once chased "the self," now chases "all beings." This is why the Mahāyāna calls this wisdom **equality** wisdom—not "no distinctions," but "all regarded alike."

*Manas* was once the most stubborn of the consciousnesses; after transformation, it is the most unshakable compassion.

**Sixth consciousness → wondrous observing wisdom.**

3.5 said the sixth consciousness is "the only consciousness that can look in the mirror"—reaching across all three kinds of object, taking in all three modes of cognition, operating across all three natures. This mirror's capacity remains **undiminished** after transformation. The difference is that the direction in which the mirror reflects shifts from "grasping outward, adding embellishment" to "observing as things truly are, neither adding nor subtracting."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*: "The mind associated with wondrous observing wisdom—this mind **skillfully observes the particular and universal characteristics of all dharmas, turning without obstruction.** It encompasses the gates of immeasurable retention and concentration … within the great assembly it can manifest boundless distinctions of function, all attained with mastery, **raining down the great Dharma rain, severing all doubt.**"

"Raining down the great Dharma rain, severing all doubt"—this is the wisdom with which the Buddha teaches the Dharma. The sixth consciousness was once the principal site of your delusion (its pervasive imagining is the fiercest, its defilement the deepest); after transformation, it is the principal instrument for delivering sentient beings.

**First five consciousnesses → wisdom of accomplishing what must be done.**

3.6 explained that the first five consciousnesses are actual objects, direct perception, neutral as to good or evil—the direct windows of the senses. After transformation, these windows **remain.** The eye still sees, the ear still hears, the nose and tongue and body go on working. The difference is that these senses shift from "receivers driven by karmic force" to "skillful instruments manifested for the benefit of beings."

The *Cheng Weishi Lun*: "The mind associated with the wisdom of accomplishing what must be done—**for the sake of benefiting and gladdening sentient beings, this mind universally manifests in the ten directions various transformative activities of body, speech, and mind, accomplishing what was to be done according to the power of original vows.**"

The Buddha's three karmas of body, speech, and mind—from everyday walking, standing, sitting, and lying down to teaching the Dharma and delivering beings—at the level of the wisdom of accomplishing what must be done, none of these is the contrivance of karmic force; they are skillful manifestations arising from the power of original vows.

This is the full panorama of the four wisdoms in luminosity. **Not one of the eight consciousnesses is annihilated; not one of the four wisdoms is newly created.** The same set of mental activities, transformed from outflow-laden to outflow-free, has its name changed from "consciousness" to "wisdom."

### The Sixth and Seventh Transform in the Cause; the First Five and the Eighth Reach Completion in the Fruit

Among the four wisdoms there is an important temporal sequence with real significance for practice, which the *Verses on the Structure of the Eight Consciousnesses* states in a single line:

> The sixth and seventh transform during the causal stage; the first five and the eighth reach completion in the fruition stage.

**"Transform during the causal stage"**—the sixth and seventh consciousnesses begin their transformation already in the **causal ground** (the course of practice). **"Reach completion in the fruition stage"**—the first five consciousnesses and the eighth consciousness are not perfected until the **fruition of Buddhahood.**

Why? Because the sixth consciousness and the seventh, *manas*, are the **main force of practice**—

The sixth consciousness is the subject that contemplates: the four investigations, the four realizations of actuality, the non-discriminating wisdom of the path of seeing—all of these are the sixth consciousness at work. If this subject does not transform first, who is there to practice?

The seventh consciousness, *manas*, is the root of self-grasping: if you do not shake *manas*, you cannot shake the deepest attachment. So the moment the first mind of the first ground at the path of seeing arrives, a large portion of *manas*'s discriminated self-grasping is severed first; from the eighth Immovable Ground onward, the innate self-grasping too is perpetually crushed by "effortless practice."

The first five consciousnesses and the eighth, by contrast, are **passive**—the first five transform along with the sixth and seventh on which they depend; the eighth, as the root basis, must wait until all obscurations are cleared away before it can finally be perfected as the great perfect mirror wisdom.

This carries a thoroughly practical implication: **what you can work on directly is the sixth and seventh consciousnesses.** Do not imagine "I must transform all eight consciousnesses at once"—that is impossible, and unnecessary. You work on the awareness of the sixth consciousness (hearing, reflecting, observing, meditating), and you work on observing the self-grasping of the seventh, *manas* (noticing, moment by moment, that automatic reaction that "everything is about me")—and the transformation of the remaining four consciousnesses will **come of itself, in its own time.**

This is the very same thing spoken of at the close of 3.14: you do not need to wait until you are deep in meditative absorption to begin practicing—every time you become aware that "this is a verbal designation, not the thing itself," every time you become aware that "this reaction is *manas*'s self-grasping, not the fact itself," you are driving forward the transformation of the sixth and seventh. **The strength in the arm that drills wood for fire is built up exactly this way, a little at a time.**

### Two Fruits: Mahānirvāṇa and Mahābodhi

The "fruit" of transforming consciousness into wisdom is of two kinds, and the *Cheng Weishi Lun* draws an exceedingly fine distinction: **revealed** and **generated.**

**Mahānirvāṇa is revealed**—it was there all along, only covered over by obstruction. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* says: "Though this is originally pure in its own nature, it is rendered unmanifest by being covered with adventitious obstruction. When the true noble path arises and severs that obstruction, its characteristic is made to appear—this is called attaining nirvāṇa. **This is designated upon suchness's departure from obstruction**, and so its substance is none other than the pure dharma-realm."

Nirvāṇa is not some new thing you "obtain"; it is the suchness that was there all along, kept unmanifest under the covering of the afflictive obstruction. Once the obstruction is severed, it "appears"—just as the sky was always blue but hidden by clouds; you need not "manufacture" the blue sky, you need only wait for the clouds to disperse. But "waiting for the clouds to disperse" is not passive waiting—you must use the wind of prajñā to blow away the clouds of affliction.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* lists four kinds of nirvāṇa, of which two are the ones you most need to remember.

**Nirvāṇa originally pure in its own nature**—"shared equally by all sentient beings, neither identical with nor different from all dharmas." This is an exceedingly profound piece of news: **you are in nirvāṇa right now.** You simply do not know it. That "state after liberation" you have been searching for—at the level of your true nature, you have never once left it. 3.13's "you have been home all along" finally lands here in doctrinal terms—not a metaphor, but a plain statement of the truth.

**Non-abiding nirvāṇa**—this is the ultimate of the bodhisattva path: "not abiding in saṃsāra, not abiding in nirvāṇa, benefiting and gladdening sentient beings to the end of future time."

This "double non-abiding" is crucial. To abide only in nirvāṇa is to be one who saves only himself—liberated, but indifferent to beings; to abide only in saṃsāra is to remain in the round of rebirth. The bodhisattva, by the wisdom of prajñā, does not abide in saṃsāra; by the power of great compassion, does not abide in nirvāṇa—a foot on each side, yet abiding on neither.

The *Dàzhìdù Lùn* has a marvelously vivid image: "Like a great dragon—its tail in the great ocean, its head in the empty sky—producing lightning and thunder and bringing down a great rain." The dragon's tail plunges into the ocean (skillful means not departing from the world), its head reaches into the empty sky (prajñā not abiding in the world), and it produces lightning and brings down rain (benefiting beings). This great dragon is the living image of non-abiding nirvāṇa.

**Mahābodhi is generated**—the seeds were there all along, but pressed down by obstruction so they could not sprout. The *Cheng Weishi Lun*: "Though it originally has seeds capable of generating it, the cognitive obstruction blocks them so they do not arise; by the power of the noble path, that obstruction is severed, allowing them to arise from their seeds—this is called attaining bodhi."

Bodhi is not something you fetch from outside—within your ālaya-vijñāna there are originally outflow-free seeds, only pressed down by the cognitive obstruction like a stone laid over the seeds. Move the stone away, and the seeds sprout of themselves. The content of mahābodhi is precisely the arising of the four wisdoms described above.

Taking these two fruits together: **nirvāṇa is the "departing-from-obstruction" face** (the covering is removed), **bodhi is the "manifesting-virtue" face** (what was there all along arises). The same basis-transformation, viewed from the angle of removal, is called nirvāṇa; viewed from the angle of manifestation, is called bodhi. Not two things, but two faces of one and the same thing.

### Affliction Itself Is Awakening: The Most Easily Misread, and Deepest, Line

Here a crucial correction must be made, to guard against a very common misreading: ⚠️ **transforming consciousness into wisdom is not "annihilating affliction and obtaining a new pure mind."**

Consciousness is not annihilated; it is **transformed.** Affliction is not annihilated; rather, once the **cause** of affliction (pervasive imagining, *parikalpita*) is broken, the very same mental activity **manifests as wisdom.**

The *Vimalakīrti Sūtra* states this in the starkest terms:

> The afflictions are the site of awakening, because one knows them as they truly are; sentient beings are the site of awakening, because one knows non-self; all dharmas are the site of awakening, because one knows the emptiness of all dharmas.

**Affliction itself is the site of awakening**—not finding a site of awakening somewhere outside affliction, but "knowing as it truly is" that affliction itself is the site of awakening. The eight consciousnesses are not annihilated but transformed; affliction is not annihilated, but once the causal activity of affliction is contemplated as it truly is, **it is, in that very moment, the operation of wisdom.**

This is entirely the same thing as 3.13's line, "suchness is not outside consciousness." You do not need to reach "a place without affliction" before you can practice—contemplating in the very midst of affliction is the practice of basis-transformation.

The *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* pushes this news to its furthest extent:

> At the moment of initial aspiration, one immediately attains perfect awakening.

Initial aspiration and the attainment of perfect awakening are the two ends of one and the same thing—cause and fruit share a single substance. This single thought of "I want to see clearly" that you rouse in this very moment already contains every seed of perfect Buddhahood. **The starting point is the destination**; the difference lies only in the depth to which it has unfolded.

### The Opening-and-Closing Circle of the Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas

Now we can look back over the whole frame of the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*.

The very first line with which Vasubandhu opens his *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*:

> As the World-Honored One said: **all dharmas are without self.**

And the final line:

> "Selflessness" is, in brief, of two kinds: first, the selflessness of the person; second, the selflessness of dharmas.

The whole treatise opens with "non-self" and closes with "two kinds of selflessness." From 3.1 to 3.14, this book has walked fourteen chapters between these two sentences, unfolding the entire content of the hundred dharmas—eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, eleven form dharmas, twenty-four dissociated formations, six unconditioned dharmas, exactly one hundred.

**The selflessness of the person** (personal non-self)—among these hundred dharmas, you cannot find one permanent, unitary, autonomous "self." The eight consciousnesses are not "self"; the mental factors are not "self"; form is not "self"; the dissociated formations are not "self"; suchness is not "some person" either. Examine the hundred dharmas one by one, and not a single dharma is **you.**

**The selflessness of dharmas**—not only can "self" not be found; "dharmas" themselves are not self-existing entities. Every dharma is a dependently arisen coming-together of conditions, with no independently self-existing fixed essence—3.11 has already argued that form is the object-aspect projected by consciousness, 3.12 that the dissociated formations are conceptual designations upon states, 3.13 that the unconditioned dharmas are not separate from mind. The operation of dharmas is real (the dependent nature exists); the substance of dharmas is illusory (the imagined nature is empty).

The realization of the two selflessnesses is not of the same level. The śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas first realize personal non-self (breaking self-grasping, severing the afflictive obstruction, realizing nirvāṇa); the bodhisattva, on the basis of realizing personal non-self, goes one step further, realizing the selflessness of dharmas (breaking dharma-grasping, severing the cognitive obstruction, realizing bodhi). **The Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas is a Mahāyāna treatise, and what it ultimately points toward is the dual realization of the two selflessnesses.**

This is the beauty of the treatise's opening-and-closing circle: P01 opens its brush with "all dharmas are without self," which is the **proposition**—a claim that needs to be directly verified. 3.15 closes with "the two selflessnesses," which is **realization**—the proposition, after being examined through a hundred dharmas, observed through the four preparatory practices, and passed through the non-discriminating wisdom of the path of seeing, turned into wisdom personally verified. From proposition to realization, what lies between is exactly this entire road of practice.

Here a second correction must be made: ⚠️ **finishing the S3 series does not amount to realizing the two selflessnesses.**

What this book does is the work of "the wisdom born of hearing"—the work of the path of accumulation. You have come to understand the four wisdoms in luminosity, you have come to understand the five stages of practice, you have come to understand the two kinds of nirvāṇa—and these acts of "coming to understand" have planted, in your ālaya-vijñāna, seeds oriented toward the Dharma body. The *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* states the importance of these seeds very clearly: "This hearing-perfuming, whether of low, middling, or high grade, should be known as a seed of the Dharma body."—even the most preliminary hearing of the Dharma is a seed of the Dharma body.

But a seed is not the fruit. From seed to flower and fruit, there still lies the entire road of the wisdom born of reflection, the wisdom born of cultivation, the four preparatory practices, the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the ultimate stage. **The treatise ends here; practice begins here.**

### S1 Trust → S2 Understand → S3 Practice

Now we can pull the lens back further still—Part I, Part II, Part III, the three parts taken together, what are they?

**Part I, Trust**—initial aspiration. It answers one question: why do you begin? It establishes the motivation, direction, and basic faith-understanding of practice.

**Part II, Understand**—prajñā's illumination. With the *Heart Sūtra* as its axis, it illuminates that the five aggregates are all empty, breaking the phantom of pervasive imagining. Prajñā is the eye that "contemplates emptiness."

**Part III, Practice**—the Yogācāra toolkit. With the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* as its axis, it takes all dharmas apart into a hundred components, identifies each one by one, then observes through the four preparatory practices, practices through the five stages, takes the four wisdoms in luminosity as its goal, transforms consciousness into wisdom, and comes home. Yogācāra is the map that "contemplates the existent."

Three words, the frame of three parts:

**Prajñā contemplating emptiness + Yogācāra contemplating the existent = the Middle Way.** Prajñā without Yogācāra easily falls into "pernicious emptiness" (mistaking emptiness for sheer nothingness); Yogācāra without prajñā easily loses itself in a labyrinth of terms (the hundred dharmas becoming a game of taxonomy). Only the two united are the Middle Way.

**Without trust, understanding has no motive force; without understanding, practice has no direction; without practice, trust and understanding are both empty talk.** The sequence of the three parts is not arbitrary; it reflects the inner logic of practice.

The meaning of the *Avataṃsaka*'s "at the moment of initial aspiration, one immediately attains perfect awakening" surfaces here once more: within the initial aspiration of S1-P01, every seed of the perfect Buddhahood toward which S3 ultimately points is already contained. The starting point and the destination are one and the same thing—from "believing that all dharmas are without self" (Part I) to "understanding that all dharmas are without self" (Part II) to "realizing that all dharmas are without self" (Part III), the same proposition at three depths.

When the three parts are walked through, the first layer of the map is drawn—**who is driving** (the eight consciousnesses), **how to drive** (the mental factors), **what the road is** (form), **what the map is** (the dissociated formations), **where home is** (the unconditioned dharma, suchness), **how to return** (the four preparatory practices, transforming consciousness into wisdom).

Trust, understand, practice—one complete toolkit for the bodhisattva path.

### Put the Map Away

But this trilogy is not the whole road of practice.

Part IV—**Inquiry**—takes this entire Yogācāra toolkit before a question it has never been asked: **does AI have the eight consciousnesses?**

This is not just chasing a trend. AI, as a system that "can speak, can reason, can remember, and seems able to think," constitutes an unprecedented test of Yogācāra. If Yogācāra's definition of "consciousness" is clear, it ought to be able to tell you whether AI is a sentient being, whether it transmigrates, whether it can practice, and how you ought to relate to it. This is exactly what the next part will do: take out the criteria of the eight consciousnesses, check AI against them point by point, and see whether Yogācāra still stands before modern technology, or whether it must be understood anew.

The road after that is longer still: Part V's *Diamond Sūtra* will take apart every one of the hundred dharmas that Part III so carefully built up—"not the hundred dharmas, therefore named the hundred dharmas"; Part VI's *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* will teach you to diagnose for yourself where your practice has reached and whether it has gone astray; Part VII's *Lotus Sūtra* will tell you the ultimate news of the three vehicles converging into one; Part VIII's *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* will unfold the dharma-realm dependent origination of "one is all."

All these are matters for further on.

For now—

You are still home. The lamp is lit. The sofa, the table and chairs, the photographs on the wall, the distant streetlight beyond the window—all of them are here.

**Put the map away.**

The map has served its purpose. From this moment on, it is the terrain itself—that is, **this single thought of mind that is your own**—that begins to speak.

It has been speaking all along. You simply could not understand it before.

From the next part onward, the focus of this book turns from "what you have learned" to "what you begin to notice."

The hundred dharmas end here.
