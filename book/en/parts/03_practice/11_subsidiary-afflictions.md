## 3.11 Subsidiary Afflictions—The Branches of Suffering

In 3.10 we dealt with the **core contaminants** in the engine—the six fundamental afflictions. But once an engine runs, it is not only the contaminants themselves that cause trouble. The engine also gives off a whole array of **exhaust fumes**, **vibration**, **wear**, and **noise**. These are not new contaminants. They are secondary phenomena, thrown off by the core contaminants in the course of everyday running. The Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas analyses these secondary phenomena one by one and names them the **twenty subsidiary afflictions**.

The Cheng Weishi Lun gives the subsidiary afflictions a precise verdict: **"They are exclusively positional variants and homogeneous outflows of afflictions—hence they are called subsidiary afflictions."** Subsidiary afflictions are not new sources of defilement. They are the **positional variants** of the fundamental afflictions—the same affliction wearing a different face under different circumstances and at different intensities—and the **homogeneous outflows** that stream naturally from the fundamental afflictions, effects of the same character.

The meaning this carries for practice is enormous: **sever the root, and the branches wither of their own accord.** You cannot sever wrath, because the essential nature of wrath simply is aversion. But once aversion is severed, the very basis on which wrath exists has vanished. This is the doctrinal ground for "cut the root, don't trim the branches"—the force of practice should be placed at the root, not spent fighting each surface phenomenon one at a time.

Yet this does not mean the subsidiary afflictions are unimportant. Quite the opposite: **subsidiary afflictions are the very form of affliction the practitioner meets first.** You do not sit down on the cushion and immediately notice "my fundamental ignorance is at work." What you notice is "I just lost my temper at him" (wrath), "I can't stop turning over what she said" (resentment), "my mind keeps wandering during meditation" (distraction). The subsidiary afflictions are the clinical symptoms; the fundamental afflictions are the pathology. You have to recognise the symptoms before you can trace your way back to the cause.

### Three Tiers: Not a Grading of Size, but a Grading of Reach

The Hundred Dharmas sort the twenty subsidiary afflictions into three classes—ten minor, two medium, eight major. This is not a grading of size (it does not mean the minor are "smaller" and the major "larger"); it is a difference of **reach**, of how widely each pervades:

- **The ten minor** (wrath, resentment, concealment, spite, envy, avarice, deceit, flattery, cruelty, self-intoxication)—arise individually. Only one comes up at a time; their character is coarse and fierce, and each needs a concrete trigger. You will not be both wrathful and envious at once—wrath is a present eruption, envy is a recoil at the sight of another's good fortune; the two are structurally different and cannot stack.
- **The two medium** (shamelessness, moral recklessness)—pervade every unwholesome mind. The moment an unwholesome mind arises, shamelessness and moral recklessness are there as its backdrop.
- **The eight major** (restlessness, torpor, distrust, sloth, heedlessness, corrupted mindfulness, distraction, corrupted discernment)—pervade every defiled mind. The unwholesome mind has them, and so does the obscured-but-indeterminate mind (such as the perpetual self-grasping of manas).

A tree makes the picture clear: the ten minor are like the fruit on the branch tips—only one ripens at a time, each with its own flavour (sour wrath, bitter resentment, acrid spite). The two medium are like the bark—if the tree is a diseased tree, the bark is invariably diseased too. The eight major are like contamination in the soil—if the soil is not clean, everything that grows from it carries that contamination, whether it is a diseased tree or a tree that looks healthy but is poisoned through.

Keeping the vehicle metaphor of 3.8–3.10: the minor afflictions are the different exhaust fumes the engine emits under different operating conditions (sometimes black smoke, sometimes a stinging gas, sometimes an invisible poison)—one kind at a time, depending on the condition of the moment. The two medium are a broken filter on the exhaust pipe—whenever the engine is doing unwholesome work, the filter is certain to be broken. The eight major are a fundamental lubrication failure in the engine itself—as long as the contaminated vehicle keeps driving, then whatever it does there is vibration (restlessness), sluggishness (torpor), and wear (distraction).

### The Three Beats of Aversion: Wrath → Resentment → Spite

The clearest structure among the ten minor is the **three-beat unfolding of aversion.** The Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra gives this group a precise sequence of definitions:

> When the fetter of anger first arises, it is called anger. When anger grows and one deliberates and holds it in the mind without resolution, this is called resentment. When the mind is decided and has no fear or hesitation, this is called spite.

Three beats: **wrath** (the present eruption) → **resentment** (sustained rumination) → **spite** (coarse expression).

Take an everyday scene. Someone cuts you off in traffic (the adverse condition), and a wave of anger surges up on the spot—this is **wrath**. Wrath is lightning: it comes fast, and it can go just as fast. If you notice it and let it go in that moment, wrath stays nothing more than wrath. But if you reach the office still thinking "how could that person be so unreasonable," chewing the incident over and over—this has become **resentment**. Resentment is a banked fire: it does not burn on the surface, but it goes on simmering in the heart, draining energy. And if by evening you are complaining to your family, your tone growing coarser and your words sharper—this has overflowed into **spite**. The Cheng Weishi Lun describes spite as "uttering wild, crude, and base words, stinging others like wasps"—stinging people the way a wasp does.

**One small matter—being cut off in traffic—can, fermented through the three beats of wrath → resentment → spite, ruin the mood of an entire day.**

Where is the point of intervention for practice?—**at wrath.** If, in the moment wrath arises, you can recognise "this is a portion of aversion at work," resentment will not arise, and spite has no footing at all. If you miss the instant of wrath and fall into resentment, it takes far more force to pull yourself out; and once spite has burst forth, the deed is done and the karma is made—you can only clean up afterward, you can no longer prevent. This is why meditative traditions place such weight on awareness of the prior moment: a single instant's difference in the precision of awareness makes a tenfold difference in the difficulty of the remedy.

### The Three Layers of Deception: Concealment → Deceit → Flattery

Another clear structure among the minor afflictions is "deception"—unfolding in three layers: toward oneself, toward others, and toward the relationship.

- **Concealment**—hiding one's own faults ("fearing loss of profit and reputation, hiding is its nature"). This is **self-deception**—before admitting you have done wrong, you first deceive yourself into "I have no problem."
- **Deceit**—fabricating virtue before others ("feigning possession of virtue, duplicity is its nature"). This is **deceiving others**—once the self-deception is in place, you project the fabricated image onto other people.
- **Flattery**—contorting yourself to ingratiate within a relationship ("contriving unusual manners, treacherous crookedness is its nature"). This is **deception by ingratiation**—to manipulate how others judge you, you will distort even your own conduct and speech.

What all three share in their harm is the **severing of authenticity**: concealment keeps you from seeing your own true condition, deceit keeps others from seeing your true condition, flattery keeps the relationship from any true exchange. All three take craving (craving for profit and reputation) and delusion (blindness to consequences) as their essential nature.

The most dangerous of these in practice is flattery. The Cheng Weishi Lun judges flattery's activity to be that it "prevents one from receiving proper teaching"—however a teacher instructs a flattering student, it is wasted, because the student is performing rather than learning. In the Dharma, the guidance of a spiritual friend is an exceedingly precious contributing condition, and flattery cuts that condition off at the source—it is not that the teacher does not teach, but that the student's mind has already bent into a shape that cannot receive proper teaching.

### The Remaining Minor Afflictions: Envy, Avarice, Self-intoxication, Cruelty

The remaining four each have their own independent structure. They form no sequence, but each has its point:

- **Envy** (aversion as its nature)—intolerance of another's flourishing. Seeing another do well, the response is not sympathetic joy but recoil and grief. Envy's activity is to make oneself suffer—it does not necessarily harm its target, but it always harms oneself.
- **Avarice** (craving as its nature)—the inability to wisely relinquish. Toward wealth, toward the Dharma, toward one's time, one cannot bear to share. The Āgamas list envy and avarice together as two of the nine fetters, and the Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra says, **"Giving breaks the fetter of avarice; compassionately regarding the recipient, it eliminates the torment of anger; envy's hostility ceases"**—generosity is the single remedy that counters envy and avarice at once.
- **Self-intoxication** (craving as its nature)—note: this differs from conceit. Conceit is "I am better than you" (a relative comparison); self-intoxication is "I am magnificent" (an absolute inflation, needing no one to compare against). Conceit needs a reference point; self-intoxication needs none—it is a pure intoxication with oneself.
- **Cruelty** (aversion as its nature)—the damaging and tormenting of sentient beings. Its difference from aversion: aversion is an attitude (inward rejection), cruelty is a behaviour (actually damaging others). The wholesome factor "non-harming" in 3.9 is precisely the reversal of this dharma.

### Shamelessness and Moral Recklessness: The Entry Pass for the Unwholesome Mind

In 3.9 we said that shame and propriety are the **moat** of the wholesome factors—with shame and propriety present, the unwholesome mind cannot get in. Here in 3.11 the mirror image comes into view: **shamelessness and moral recklessness are the entry pass for the unwholesome mind**—without shamelessness and moral recklessness, the unwholesome mind cannot get through the gate.

Why do the two medium afflictions **pervade every unwholesome mind**? Because the very arising of an unwholesome mind already presupposes some degree of collapse in the moral defences. To give rise to wrath, you must first disregard the faults of wrath (shamelessness) and disregard its social consequences (moral recklessness); to give rise to envy, you must first feel no shame at envy—otherwise the moment envy stirred, shame and propriety would halt it on the spot.

The Cheng Weishi Lun's definitions are exactly symmetrical:

- **Shamelessness**: "not considering oneself or the dharma, dismissing the worthy and good is its nature"—disregarding one's own standard of character, disregarding the standard set by the Dharma's teaching. The collapse of the inner standard.
- **Moral recklessness**: "not considering the world, esteeming what is violent and evil is its nature"—disregarding the judgement of the world, disregarding how others perceive one. The collapse of the outer standard.

What they share is "not being ashamed of faults"; where they differ is one inward and one outward—a person with shame and propriety holds both lines of defence, the inner and the outer, while shamelessness and moral recklessness mean both lines give way at once.

The Āgamas state a remarkably precise causal chain: **ignorance → shamelessness and moral recklessness → wrong views → the eightfold wrong path.** Shamelessness and moral recklessness are the pivot of this chain—they are the **junction** between "fundamental confusion" (ignorance) and "concrete wrongdoing" (the arising of wrong views, the walking of the eightfold wrong path). Without the permission of shamelessness and moral recklessness, ignorance does not convert directly into concrete unwholesome conduct.

The Ekottarāgama puts it even more bluntly: **"There are two wonderful dharmas that protect the world"**—shame and propriety. Without these two dharmas, human conduct falls back to pure instinct. This is not a moral sermon; it is a statement of fact: shame and propriety are the bottom line of what makes a human being human.

### Restlessness and Torpor: The Dynamic Balance of Meditation

Among the eight major afflictions, the pair that bears most directly on meditation is restlessness and torpor. The Cheng Weishi Lun's definitions reveal a precise, mirror-symmetrical structure between them:

| | Restlessness | Torpor |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Makes the mind not tranquil | Makes the mind not capable |
| Specific character | Agitation | Heaviness |
| Wholesome factor obstructed | Equanimity-of-application, śamatha (calming) | Pliancy, vipaśyanā (insight) |

Restlessness is a mind too active (the fire too strong, it will not settle); torpor is a mind too heavy (the fire too weak, insight cannot rise). Add distraction (the fire running wild) and you have the complete spectrum of meditative obstruction.

⚠️ Two common misreadings need correcting here: **restlessness is not the same as "being alert," and torpor is not the same as "being tired."** Restlessness is an affliction-laden agitation—it is not the efficient focus of good work, nor the joy of the Dharma in meditation, but a restless turbulence driven by affliction. Torpor is a delusion-laden heaviness—it is not the body's legitimate fatigue, but the mind's incapacity in the face of wholesome dharmas. The key to telling them apart: restlessness obstructs calming, while the vigor found in wholesome practice does not obstruct calming; torpor obstructs insight, while the rest the body physically needs does not obstruct insight. You must not suppress every excitement as restlessness, nor reproach every weariness as torpor—that runs to the opposite extreme.

The fire simile of the seven awakening factors offers a dynamic model for the remedy:

- When the mind is **torpid**, add the three factors of investigation-of-dharmas, energy, and joy—dry kindling, to coax a small fire into flame.
- When the mind is **restless**, add the three factors of tranquility, concentration, and equanimity—damp charcoal, to bring a blazing fire down.
- The **mindfulness factor assists all of them**—mindfulness is the thermostat, monitoring the flame at every moment, adding kindling when kindling is needed and damp charcoal when that is needed.

The beauty of this model is that it does not treat restlessness and torpor as enemies to be destroyed; it treats the whole of meditation as the regulation of a flame. A fire too strong is no sin, a fire too weak is no sin—the loss of regulation is the problem. Mindfulness (the very "mindfulness" among the wholesome factors of 3.9) is the central pivot of this regulating system.

### Distrust → Sloth → Heedlessness: The Mirror of the Wholesome Chain

Another clearly structured group among the major afflictions is **distrust, sloth, and heedlessness**—they are exactly the mirror image of the wholesome chain **faith → vigor → non-heedlessness** in 3.9.

- **The wholesome chain**: faith (inner purity, conviction in wholesome dharmas) → vigor (courageous engagement in the wholesome) → non-heedlessness (the composite quality of guarding against the defiled and cultivating the pure).
- **The defiled chain**: distrust (inner murk, no conviction in wholesome dharmas) → sloth (no courage in the wholesome, but instead industrious in defiled matters) → heedlessness (unable to guard against the defiled or to cultivate the pure).

This contrast reveals a deep fact: **the wholesome and the defiled are not two different systems but two directions of the same set of mental factors.** You travel either toward faith (faith → vigor → non-heedlessness) or toward distrust (distrust → sloth → heedlessness). There is, in truth, no middle ground—only the brief gap before a choice is made.

The Cheng Weishi Lun gives distrust a distinctive judgement: **"Its own characteristic is murky, and it further makes murky all other minds and mental factors, like an extremely filthy thing that fouls itself and fouls others."** Among all defiled dharmas, distrust alone is not merely a problem in itself—it simultaneously contaminates every other mental activity arising alongside it. Faith, by contrast, is like a water-clearing pearl—dropped into murky water, it not only stays clear itself but turns the surrounding water clear. The one and the other are perfectly symmetrical.

Heedlessness has no independent nature of its own—the Cheng Weishi Lun says plainly that **"heedlessness has no separate substance,"** that it is a nominal composite of sloth together with the three of craving, aversion, and delusion. But precisely because heedlessness is a composite, it **carries the destructive force of all four at once**—within a single heedless state there is sloth, and there is craving, aversion, and delusion each at work. This is why the Āgamas call heedlessness "the path of death" and call non-heedlessness "the trace of ambrosia." Heedlessness is not a single problem; it is the resonance of four forces—sloth and the three poisons.

### Corrupted Mindfulness, Distraction, Corrupted Discernment: The Object-Specific Factors Defiled

The last three of the eight major afflictions—**corrupted mindfulness, distraction, corrupted discernment**—have the most interesting feature of all: they are **not independent new qualities, but the distorted forms that mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom—three of the five object-specific factors—take once they are defiled.**

- **Corrupted mindfulness** = mindfulness defiled—"toward all that is taken as object, the inability to clearly recollect is its nature." Mindfulness is originally the function of "clear recollection without forgetting"; defiled, it becomes a clear recollection of defiled matters and a forgetting of wholesome dharmas—a bad event remembered firmly, while the dharma one ought to cultivate cannot be brought to mind at all.
- **Distraction** = concentration defiled—"toward all that is taken as object, it makes the mind adrift." Concentration is originally "single-pointedness upon the object"; defiled, it becomes the agitation of a mind that ceaselessly changes its object.
- **Corrupted discernment** = wisdom defiled—"toward the contemplated object, erroneous understanding is its nature." Wisdom is originally the function of "discriminating analysis of the contemplated object"; defiled, it becomes misjudgement—everything it looks at comes out distorted.

The Cheng Weishi Lun marks this structure in a single line: **"Corrupted mindfulness, distraction, and corrupted discernment are subsumed under the object-specific factors when reversed."** The essential nature of these three is the reverse face of three object-specific factors (mindfulness, concentration, wisdom).

The implication for practice runs deep: **you do not need to "manufacture" mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom separately**—they have been present all along, only distorted, in the defiled state, into corrupted mindfulness, distraction, and corrupted discernment. Practice is not building up the wholesome from zero; it is **turning** the defiled quality back toward the pure direction. The fact that you have the capacity for corrupted mindfulness is itself proof that you have the capacity for mindfulness—because the two are two directions of the same mental-factor position. This is a fact that brings real relief.

### Two Misreadings That Easily Lead Astray

⚠️ **The first misreading: treating the subsidiary afflictions as a checklist of bad moods.** The everyday words "angry," "jealous," "lazy" overlap with the subsidiary afflictions but are not identical to them. The subsidiary afflictions are technical concepts, with precise attributions of essential nature, of reach, and of which consciousness each accompanies. Wrath is not merely "being angry"—it is "directed at a presently disagreeable situation, provoked outburst is its nature," with aversion as its essence, arising individually, nominally existent, exclusively unwholesome, belonging only to the desire realm, accompanying only the sixth consciousness. To downgrade the subsidiary afflictions into everyday emotional vocabulary is to lose the precision of the Hundred Dharmas as an analytical tool, and to miss the point of intervention for practice.

⚠️ **The second misreading: treating the subsidiary afflictions as enemies to destroy.** If you treat the subsidiary afflictions as enemies, practice becomes a war—and this very attitude of "wanting to destroy the enemy" is itself aversion (indeed, cruelty). All twenty subsidiary afflictions are without independent self-nature—they are exclusively the positional variants and homogeneous outflows of the fundamental afflictions; what cannot be severed is not them, but the root behind them. Wrath does not need to be "destroyed"; what is needed is to be aware of its essential nature (aversion), aware of the essential nature of aversion (delusion), aware of what delusion rests upon (the view of self)—going inward, layer by layer, toward the root. **You cannot clear away the leaves on their own—even if you strip them all, it lasts only a moment; the root remains, and the leaves grow back.** Real practice is not trimming branches; it is loosening the soil, pulling the root, changing the soil, planting a new seedling.

### The Theory of Mental Factors Comes Full Circle

We have now walked once through all fifty-one mental factors of the Hundred Dharmas—

**The five universal factors** (3.8) are the mind's smallest engine, the five basic functions any act of consciousness must possess; **the five object-specific factors** (3.8) are the advanced engine, the aspiration, resolve, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom that start up on demand; **the eleven wholesome factors** (3.9) are the clean fuel, the three wholesome roots and eleven dharmas that make up the driving force of practice; **the six fundamental afflictions** (3.10) are the core contaminants, craving-aversion-delusion-conceit-doubt-wrong-views, the engine of saṃsāra; **the four indeterminate factors** (3.10) are the gearbox, the four of regret, sleep, investigation, and analysis, whose direction depends on the one using them; **the twenty subsidiary afflictions** (this chapter) are the derivatives of the core contaminants—the ten minor arising individually, like the different kinds of exhaust; the two medium pervading the unwholesome mind, like a broken filter; the eight major pervading the defiled mind, like the engine's own failure of lubrication.

The overall message of this map: **the mental factors are not something outside the mind; they are the cluster of functions the mind invariably brings with it each time it arises.** Every moment of mind that comes present has a number of mental factors corresponding to it at once; the direction—wholesome or unwholesome—depends on which cluster of factors dominates in that moment; and practice is not setting up a separate training ground outside the mental factors, but practising choice within the combination of mental factors in every single moment—whether to move toward the wholesome factors or toward the defiled ones.

But the mental factors are only "functions upon the mind." Up to this point, the whole analysis has stayed on the **subjective side**—the driver (the eight consciousnesses) and the driver's states of mind (the mental factors). There is a whole **objective side** still to be opened: **the road this vehicle travels, the scenery along the road, the very material of the vehicle and the road itself**—what are all these? The next chapter takes up the third category of the Hundred Dharmas—**form dharmas**. The position of Yogācāra is radical in the extreme: the road and the scenery, too, are manifested by consciousness.
