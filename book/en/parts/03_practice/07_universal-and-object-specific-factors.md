## 3.7 The Universals and the Object-Specifics—The Smallest Engine of Mind

The end of 3.6 left a promise: to open the second drawer of the Yogācāra toolbox, the mental factors.

What are mental factors? They are **the psychological functions that necessarily, or conditionally, arise alongside consciousness whenever it operates**. The eight consciousnesses are the driver; the mental factors are what the driver is doing—alerting, contacting, feeling, identifying, constructing, hoping, judging, remembering, focusing, analyzing… These actions are not optional add-ons. As long as consciousness is moving, they are moving with it.

The *Śatadharmaprakāśamukha-śāstra* sorts all mental factors into fifty-one, in six groups. This chapter opens only the first two groups: **the five universals** and **the five object-specifics**, ten in all. Why begin with these ten? Because together they form "the minimal complete structure of mind." Once you see these ten clearly, you have seen how every single moment of thought comes to be at its deepest level.

### A Quick Sketch of the Six Groups

First, a map of the whole, so you know where this chapter sits:

- **The five universals**: contact, attention, feeling, perception, volition—**present in every moment**. This chapter.
- **The five object-specifics**: aspiration, resolve, mindfulness, concentration, discerning wisdom—**arising only under conditions**. This chapter.
- **The eleven wholesome factors**: trust, vigor, conscience, shame… the fuel that turns the mind in a wholesome direction. 3.8.
- **The six root afflictions**: craving, aversion, delusion, pride, doubt, wrong views—the engine of saṃsāra. 3.9.
- **The twenty subsidiary afflictions**: the various secondary defilements that branch off from the root afflictions. 3.10.
- **The four indeterminates**: regret, sleep, applied thought, sustained examination—undecided as to wholesome or unwholesome, depending on the situation. Handled together with 3.9.

There is a logic to how the six groups are arranged: from "present in every moment" (the universals), to "activated by conditions" (the object-specifics), to "directional control" (the wholesome and the afflictions), to "secondary distortion" (the subsidiaries), to "decided by circumstance" (the indeterminates). This chapter begins with the two most fundamental groups.

### The Instant Your Phone Buzzes

Before we go further, let us take one microscopic slice, to make "the five universals co-arise simultaneously" concrete.

You are sitting in the office. Your phone buzzes once in your pocket.

**Attention**—the mind turns automatically toward that buzz. You have not yet judged who sent it, you have not even noticed that you are reacting, and already the direction has shifted toward it.

**Contact**—the body-faculty senses the pressure of the vibration; body-consciousness receives it. Faculty, object, and consciousness—the three meet.

**Feeling**—a faint wave of emotion. Depending on what you have been waiting for lately, this wave might be the pleasant feeling of anticipation (good news is coming), the painful feeling of anxiety (which difficult person is it now), or a flat, neutral feeling.

**Perception**—a thought has already surfaced in your mind: "It's X," "Is it a message or a notification," "How many times today?" Taking the image, designating the name.

**Volition**—your fingers have already started reaching toward your pocket, or you have already decided not to look just yet. The constructing is in motion.

This whole process finishes within a second or two. You did not "first attend, then contact, then feel…" step by step in order—they **co-arise simultaneously**. They are only logically dependent on one another: contact is the basis of feeling, feeling is the precondition of perception, perception is the basis of volition. Like the foundation, walls, and roof of a finished house, which all exist at the same moment, yet structurally "the foundation supports the walls, the walls support the roof"—an order that cannot be reversed.

This is the five universals. They need no "starting up" from you. As long as consciousness is moving, they are present.

### The Five Universals: The Smallest Engine

"Universal" means "pervading everything." The *Cheng Weishi Lun* uses the "four universalities" to measure them: universality of nature (pervading wholesome and unwholesome), universality of ground (pervading the three realms), universality of time (without interruption), and universality of association (pervading all eight consciousnesses)—the five universals possess all four. Every other group of mental factors possesses at most one or two. Possessing all four means this: the five universals are **the unconditional foundational structure**, necessarily present in every consciousness, every instant, every realm and ground, and every wholesome or unwholesome nature.

Each of the five has its own definition:

**Contact**—arising from the meeting of faculty, object, and consciousness, it causes mind and mental factors to contact the object together. The "the meeting of three gives rise to contact," which we met in 3.6 when discussing the first five consciousnesses, is exactly this contact. It is the shared basis of feeling, perception, and volition.

**Attention**—alerting the mind to arise, it draws the mind toward the present object. The mind does not turn toward anything on its own; it is attention that pulls the direction.

**Feeling**—receiving the agreeable, disagreeable, and neutral aspects of an object, it gives rise to the three feelings of painful, pleasant, and neutral. The single most important teaching of the Āgamas hangs right here: "feeling conditions craving"—the moment feeling arises, if it goes unnoticed, it flows automatically into craving, and then grasping, becoming, birth, aging-and-death unfold link by link like a chain. The breakpoint of practice lies exactly between feeling and craving.

**Perception**—taking the image of an object, designating the name. From "I see a shape" (taking the image) to "this is a flower" (designating the name), the perception factor is at work. It is neutral in itself, but it is also the earliest seedbed of imagined grasping—every conceptual fixation grows out of here.

**Volition**—causing the mind to construct, it is the launcher of wholesome and unwholesome karma. The formation aggregate is the six bodies of volition; the "formations" among the five aggregates do not refer broadly to all mental activity, but specifically to "volition." The full argument for volition and the three types of karma (bodily, verbal, mental) was already unfolded in 3.5 under "in activating body and speech, it alone is supreme," when we discussed the sixth consciousness; we will not repeat it here.

The five co-arising together form "the minimal complete structure of every moment of thought."

### Attention: The Exact Location of Awareness

Among the five universals, one deserves to be pulled out and looked at on its own: **attention**.

In 1.6 there was a line: **awareness is itself a turning**. At that time it was only a description of a phenomenon—you knew that awareness has the power to change things, but you did not know where, in the structure of the mind, it actually sits. Now a precise answer can be given: **awareness is itself a turning—it is attention operating in the right direction**.

The essence of attention is "alerting the mind"—rousing the mind from its un-roused state and drawing it toward the object. It is already a universal; it is already present in every moment—the question has never been "is there attention," but "which way is attention turned?" Yogācāra divides attention into "appropriate attention" and "inappropriate attention": turned toward reality, toward wholesome dharmas, toward an object held clearly—that is appropriate; turned toward distortion, toward defilement, toward a scattered place—that is inappropriate. The whole operability of practice lands, almost entirely, on this fork in the road.

⚠️ One misreading to correct here: **attention is not the same as concentration of focus**. In everyday language, "paying attention" usually means the function of consciousness fixing on some object—and that is closer to the object-specific factor "concentration," or to the outward expression of consciousness and attention working together. Attention itself is more basic: it is the very pivot of "turning the mind toward," and it does not necessarily come with conscious self-awareness. Attention operates even in sleep (the ālaya-vijñāna's five universals have never stopped); attention operates even when the mind wanders, only it has turned toward somewhere else. Focus is the result of consciousness being mobilized; attention is the prior function that makes the mobilizing possible.

### The Five Object-Specifics: The Higher-Gear Engine

"Object-specific" means "taking a specific, distinct object." Unlike the universals, which are "present at all times," the object-specifics arise each on its own only when their particular object is encountered. Among the four universalities they possess only the first two (pervading the three natures, pervading the three realms); they lack universality of time and universality of association.

The definitions of the five:

**Aspiration**—hoping for a desired object. The thing you want to do, the cup of tea you want to drink, the book you want to finish—the drive behind all of them is aspiration. It is not inherently defiled; aspiration pervades the wholesome and the unwholesome, and aspiration toward wholesome dharmas is the fuel of vigor.

**Resolve**—holding firm and immovably to a determined object. When a person "believes something without a shred of doubt," that is resolve at work. It gives the mind a power of "not being shaken"—but if the direction is wrong, it becomes wrong resolve, and the one who firmly believes a wrong view actually has the stronger resolve.

**Mindfulness**—clearly remembering, without forgetting, an object previously experienced. Mindfulness is not ordinary memory—memory is the perception factor calling up seeds; mindfulness is keeping something already learned (a previously experienced object) clearly in hand. The "mindfulness" of right mindfulness is exactly this mindfulness factor. Without mindfulness, the mind loses its object.

**Concentration**—on an observed object, causing the mind to focus on a single point without scattering. The practice of calm (*śamatha*) is the strengthening of the concentration factor.

**Discerning Wisdom**—discerning and deciding regarding an observed object. Distinguishing, judging, selecting—this is the tool of insight (*vipaśyanā*). The one object-specific factor that the seventh consciousness associates with is discerning wisdom—when it grasps the ālaya-vijñāna as a self, what it uses is discerning wisdom (a discerning wisdom turned in the wrong direction). Discerning wisdom pervades the three natures; wrong discerning wisdom is also discerning wisdom, which is why cleverness is not the same as discerning wisdom—when the direction is wrong, the stronger the discerning wisdom, the greater the harm.

Pull out mindfulness, concentration, and discerning wisdom, and you will find they are precisely the core of the thirty-seven factors of awakening—the mindfulness-faculty, mindfulness-power, mindfulness-limb-of-awakening, and right mindfulness; the concentration-faculty, concentration-power, concentration-limb-of-awakening, and right concentration; the wisdom-faculty, wisdom-power, dharma-discernment-limb-of-awakening, and right view. The precise location, within mental-factor theory, of the entire Buddhist path of practice lies right here, in the five object-specifics.

### The Core Paradox: A Neutral Engine, Wholesome or Unwholesome Assigned on the Spot

Place the universals and the object-specifics side by side, and a paradox surfaces that is crucial for practice.

**The five universals are themselves neutral**—no wholesome, no unwholesome. The same "volition" can create the wholesome karma of saving someone or the unwholesome karma of harming someone; the same "attention" can run in the appropriate direction or in the distorted direction. The tool is faultless; whether wholesome or unwholesome is decided by how the universals pair up with other mental factors (the wholesome factors or the afflictive factors). This is the "wholesome or unwholesome assigned on the spot" we quoted in 3.5 when discussing consciousness—and only here does that line truly become operable.

⚠️ Another common misunderstanding to correct here: **the five universals are not "mental activity that only consciousness has."** They are necessarily present in all eight consciousnesses. Even the deepest, stillest ālaya-vijñāna associates only with the five universals—it is just that the ālaya-vijñāna's contact is so subtle it has no painful or pleasant quality, its feeling is solely neutral feeling, its perception takes no names, and its volition creates no wholesome or unwholesome karma. The five universals are "the minimal configuration for consciousness to operate," from the deepest ālaya to the most active first five consciousnesses, without exception. This means: you **cannot make the universals stop**. However deep the meditative absorption, however fine the calm-and-insight, as long as consciousness is present, the universals are present.

If they cannot be stopped, where does practice find its leverage?

—**Not in extinguishing the universals, but in actively activating the object-specifics.** Mindfulness, concentration, and discerning wisdom above all.

The universals give you the material: you will inevitably have contact, inevitably have feeling, inevitably have perception, inevitably have volition. This material is **already there**—you do not need to manufacture it, and you cannot avoid it.

The object-specifics give you the tools: you can use mindfulness to pull the mind steadily back to its object; use concentration so the mind is not swept off by the force of feeling; use discerning wisdom to see clearly which way the next step should turn; use resolve to hold an upright view firm and unshaken; use aspiration toward wholesome dharmas to keep yourself moving forward.

This is the structural reminder Yogācāra offers the practitioner: **saṃsāra is automatic** (the universals are inevitable, and the chain of "feeling conditions craving" runs on its own as long as it goes unnoticed), **liberation takes effort** (the object-specifics are not inevitable; mindfulness, concentration, and discerning wisdom must be actively activated and steadily strengthened).

Now we have both the engine and the steering wheel. The engine is the five universals, turning inevitably; the steering wheel is the five object-specifics, and you can choose which way to turn it.

But one thing is still missing: **fuel**.

The steering wheel knows to turn toward the wholesome, and the engine has power—but where does the positive drive come from that keeps this car moving steadily toward liberation? —From **the eleven wholesome factors**. Trust, vigor, conscience, shame, non-attachment, non-aversion, non-delusion, pliancy, non-negligence, equanimity, non-harming. In the next chapter, we open this group.
