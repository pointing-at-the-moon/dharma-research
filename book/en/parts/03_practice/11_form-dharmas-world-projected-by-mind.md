## 3.11 Form-Dharmas — The World Projected by Consciousness

You step out your front door.

Sunlight lands on your face: a touch-domain. Somewhere distant, a bird is calling: a sound-domain. Flowers are blooming along the path — red, yellow, the shape of the petals, the height of the stalks: a form-domain. You lean down to smell one, and a smell-domain rushes in. You pluck a mint leaf and place it on your tongue, and a taste-domain unfolds across the tongue-faculty.

The five faculties meet the five objects, and consciousness arises to discern. This is the part of daily experience that seems least worth doubting — "I see the flower," "I hear the bird," "I feel the sun." If someone told you "the flower is not out there; the flower is inside consciousness," most people would suggest that person get some rest.

But this is precisely what *Yogācāra* has to say.

The claim sounds arrogant. It is not. It does not say "there is nothing out there" (that would be nihilism), and it does not say "whatever you imagine becomes real" (that would be magical mind-power). It says something far more precise: **everything you experience as form — color, sound, fragrance, the feel of things — has never once existed as "external matter standing independent of consciousness." It is the "semblance of an external object" that consciousness projects as it works — looking as though it is out there, while from beginning to end it is inside consciousness.** Sentient beings mistake this projected aspect for an independently existing substance, just as a person in a dream takes the dream landscape for the outside world.

What this chapter unfolds is the full doctrine of that claim, and the Hundred Dharmas' precise eleven-fold classification of form.

### The Ordering of the Hundred Dharmas Is Itself a Verdict

The five categories of the Hundred Dharmas are arranged in this order: **mind dharmas (the eight consciousnesses) → mental factors (the fifty-one) → form-dharmas (the eleven) → factors dissociated from mind (the twenty-four) → unconditioned dharmas (the six).** Form ranks third, and this is not a casual arrangement.

The five categories follow a logic running from agent to product, from cause to effect. First there is **consciousness** (the transformer); as consciousness works, various **mental factors** accompany it (the qualities of mind); the activity of consciousness projects the **form** it takes as its object (what is transformed); that activity is then conceptualized into various nominally posited **relationships** (factors dissociated from mind); and finally one returns to consciousness's own **true nature** (the unconditioned). Form ranks third precisely because it is judged to be the product of consciousness — not the external cause of consciousness, but its output.

This is one long narrowing of perspective running through the whole of Part III:

- 3.1–3.6 unfolded the eight consciousnesses — "who is driving" is now clear;
- 3.7–3.10 unfolded the fifty-one mental factors — "what mindset the driver brings" is clear too;
- 3.11 unfolds form — "the road the car runs on, and the scenery along it" is now opening up.

But here Yogācāra throws down its greatest lever: **the road and the scenery are also projected by consciousness.** The practitioner is not driving through an objective external world but moving within a "world-aspect" projected by their own consciousness. This thesis dismantles not only the misreading of form but a deeper assumption underneath it — the dualistic split between "I" and "the world."

### The Eleven Form-Dharmas: Two Sets of Faculty-and-Object, Plus One Independent Construction

The eleven form-dharmas fall into three groups:

- **The five faculties** — the eye-faculty, ear-faculty, nose-faculty, tongue-faculty, body-faculty.
- **The five objects** — form-domain, sound-domain, smell-domain, taste-domain, touch-domain.
- **Form included in the mental base** — special form-dharmas independently constructed by the mind-consciousness, of five kinds (minimal form, maximal spatial form, form derived from undertaking, form arising from conceptual construction, and form arising from meditative mastery).

The five faculties and five objects are the field in which the first five consciousnesses operate — the eye-faculty takes the form-domain, the ear-faculty takes the sound-domain, and so on. This is the layer everyone experiences every day.

The eleventh, **form included in the mental base** (*dharma-āyatana-paryāpanna-rūpa*), is a refined category unique to Yogācāra. It is not apprehended by the first five consciousnesses, only by the sixth (the mind-consciousness), yet it still possesses form's "resistant character" rather than being pure concept. Specifically:

- **Minimal form** — matter analyzed repeatedly down to its smallest unit, the atom (*paramāṇu*);
- **Maximal spatial form** — the faint, distant, barely discernible color-shadows of remote space (cloud, smoke, dust, mist);
- **Form derived from undertaking** — the imperceptible form generated by the force of precept-reception, vow, or meditative concentration (the precept-essence one receives in taking vows is of this kind);
- **Form arising from conceptual construction** — the scenes of dreams, the pictures of imagination, the things seen in hallucination;
- **Form arising from meditative mastery** — the form-aspects projected by the power of meditative concentration.

This fifth kind — **form arising from meditative mastery** — deserves particular attention. It points to a fact: **when practice reaches a certain depth, the power of concentration itself can project form-dharmas.** This is not mysticism but a confirmation, run backward, of the thesis that form is consciousness-projected. If form is consciousness-projected to begin with, then when consciousness enters the stage of meditative mastery, consciousness can naturally project form-aspects in a freer way. The difference between everyday form and meditative form is not "real versus false" but a difference in the mechanism and the degree of mastery with which form is projected.

### From the Āgamas to Yogācāra: The Doctrinal Reversal of Form

The definition of form underwent a fundamental reversal from the Āgamas to Yogācāra.

The **Āgamas** give form its plainest definition: **"All that is form is the four great elements and form derived from the four great elements; this is called form"** — form is nothing other than the four great elements of earth, water, fire, and wind (*mahābhūta*), and all the matter the four elements produce. In the Āgamas the Buddha's practice teaching on form concentrates on contemplating the three marks:

> "Form is impermanent. The impermanent is suffering. What is suffering is not-self. What is not-self is also not mine."

Form arises and ceases moment by moment (impermanent), cannot give beings lasting security (suffering), cannot be governed by beings (not-self), and so there is no "whose it is" at all (not-mine). This is the liberation path's way of contemplating form — contemplating the form aggregate to generate disenchantment and turn toward nirvāṇa.

But the Āgamas do not explicitly deny that form exists independently of consciousness. Their concern is **how to be liberated**, not **the ontological status of form**. That further question waits for the Mahāyāna to open directly.

The **Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra** delivers the most direct verdict:

> "If one understands that all dharmas are seen only by mind, then there are no external objects, all being equally without characteristics."

All dharmas are seen only by mind; apart from consciousness there is no independently existing matter. A Laṅkāvatāra verse goes further: "Because beings see external objects, therefore I teach consciousness-only" — the Buddha taught consciousness-only precisely because beings universally mistake what they see for external objects. If beings already knew it was consciousness-only, the teaching would not need to be given.

The **Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra** delivers the decisive doctrinal judgment:

> "I teach that what consciousness apprehends is solely what consciousness projects."

Everything consciousness apprehends — including every form-dharma before your eyes — is what consciousness manifests. This line is the scriptural ground of the entire Yogācāra doctrine of form.

The **Avataṃsaka Sūtra** compresses the whole teaching into a single immortal verse:

> "Mind is like a master painter, able to paint all the worlds. The five aggregates all arise from it; there is no dharma it does not create."

Mind is the painter, painting out the whole world you experience. The five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — are all the work of this painter. Form, as one of the five aggregates, is naturally within the painting, not outside it.

This trajectory from the Āgamas to the Mahāyāna is not "the Buddha contradicting himself" but the teaching unfolding in stages. The Āgamas address the cessation of suffering, so contemplating form as impermanent, suffering, and not-self is enough; the Mahāyāna addresses the fundamental reconstruction of cognition, so it must also make clear the ontological status of "what form is." The two do not conflict.

### The Refined Faculty and the Supporting Faculty: The Visible Eye Is Not the Seeing Faculty

The most easily misunderstood of the eleven form-dharmas are **the five faculties** — because in ordinary language, "eye" means both the visible organ and the "capacity to see," and the two get fused. Yogācāra pulls them apart with great clarity.

*Saṃyukta Āgama* Sūtra 322 gives the foundational definition of the five material faculties:

> "The eye is an internal sense-base, a refined materiality derived from the four great elements, invisible, yet offering resistance."

"Refined materiality" — this is the essence of the five faculties. The five faculties are pure form (also called the dominant faculty, or the refined faculty), the truly subtle structure that can generate consciousness. It has three features: **derived from the four great elements** (it still belongs to the category of form), **invisible** (it cannot be seen by the naked eye), and **offering resistance** (it has obstructive quality, able to meet objects and generate consciousness).

Set against it is the **supporting faculty** — the organ visible to the eye (the eyeball, the ear, the nasal cavity, the tongue, the skin). The supporting faculty's role is to **carry and protect the refined faculty**; it cannot directly generate consciousness. The visible eyeball is not "the eye that can see" but the "container" that carries the eye that can see.

This distinction resolves an old puzzle: why does a dead person's eyeball remain, yet cannot see? Why do some people have intact eyeballs but go blind when the optic nerve is damaged? Because "what can see" was never the eyeball, but the refined faculty embedded throughout the whole visual system. The supporting faculty can be intact while the refined faculty is damaged; the moment the refined faculty loses its function, the basis for consciousness to arise is gone.

In Yogācāra's ontology, **the five refined faculties are the intimate aspect-portion of the eighth ālaya-vijñāna** — it projects the five faculties from its own seeds, and the first five consciousnesses then arise in dependence on these five faculties. The layered structure of dependence runs like this: the ālaya-vijñāna (the transformer) → the five refined faculties (its intimate aspect-portion) → the first five consciousnesses arising in dependence on the faculties to take objects → the five objects (the aspect-portion that consciousness apprehends). From the deepest root to the surface, everything is consciousness's projection.

⚠️ A cross-disciplinary boundary needs correcting here. What modern neuroscience calls **sensory receptors** (the photoreceptor cells of the retina, the hair cells of the cochlea, the olfactory receptors, and so on) run in striking structural parallel to the refined faculty as a functional description — both are the truly subtle structures that generate sensory signals, and both are distinct from the protective organ-casing around them. But their ontological status is fundamentally different: neuroscience presupposes that the material world really exists and that receptors are objective biological structures; Yogācāra holds that form is consciousness-projected and that the refined faculty is the aspect-portion of the eighth consciousness. The two can illuminate each other; they cannot prove each other. Do not use neuroscience to "verify" the refined faculty, and do not use the refined faculty to "predict" neural mechanisms.

### Shared and Unshared Seeds: Why We See "the Same World"

If form is consciousness-projected, an immediate question arises — **why does the table we see look the same?** If each person projects their own separate world, two people's tables ought to be completely different. Why can we discuss "this table" together, walk around it, and use it in common?

Yogācāra's answer to this is exceedingly precise — the mechanism of **shared seeds and unshared seeds.**

The eighth ālaya-vijñāna holds two kinds of seeds. **Unshared seeds** belong to one sentient being alone and project that being's own **body-with-faculties** (the five faculties, the body). An individual's body belongs only to that individual, not to anyone else; this is the unshared. **Shared seeds** are seeds perfumed in common by the collective karma of many beings and held in common among them, and they project the **vessel world** (*bhājana-loka*) — the mountains, rivers, and earth, the tables, chairs, and buildings, the whole material environment.

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* gives this mechanism an elegant simile: **"like the light of many lamps, each pervading yet appearing as one"** — like a room lit by many lamps, each shining on its own, but because the rays overlap, it looks like a single sheet of light. The "whole illumination" of the room is not made by one single "super-lamp"; it is the result of each lamp's own light overlapping.

The vessel world is the same. Two people seeing "the same table" is not because a table really exists independently outside both of them, but because their shared seeds are similar enough that each projects a "mutually resembling" table. "Mutually resembling, their locations not differing" — the two tables are not one table, but they resemble each other closely enough to be used as one.

This mechanism also explains several old puzzles:

- **Why do ghosts and spirits not see what humans see?** — The shared seeds of beings in different realms differ too greatly, so the vessel worlds they project differ too greatly. What a hungry ghost sees as "water" is pus and blood; what a human sees is clear water — it is not the water that changes, but the seeds that differ and project differently.
- **Why is collective karma collectively borne in a disaster?** — Collective karma perfumes shared seeds, and the vessel world that shared seeds project naturally bears those disaster-aspects in common.
- **Why does the depth of the cosmos, where no being has ever set foot, still "exist"?** — Because the collective karma of the entire realm of sentient beings ceaselessly perfumes the seeds of the "vessel world"; it does not depend on someone "looking" to make it exist.

The crucial meaning of the shared-seeds mechanism is this: **Yogācāra is not solipsism.** "Form is consciousness-projected" does not mean "I alone am making this world" — it means "countless beings each project, through their own consciousness, similar worlds in dependence on collective karma, and we meet one another within this overlapping illumination."

### Form Is Aspect-Portion: Its Place in the Four-Part Structure

Form's place in the four-part structure is perfectly clear. The *Cheng Weishi Lun*, volume two:

> "The semblance of an object of apprehension is called the aspect-portion; the semblance of a subject of apprehension is called the seeing-portion."

When consciousness arises, its own substance turns into two faces — the face that looks like "the object apprehended" is called the **aspect-portion** (*nimitta-bhāga*); the face that looks like "the consciousness that apprehends" is called the **seeing-portion** (*darśana-bhāga*).

**All form is aspect-portion.** The five faculties are the aspect-portion of the eighth consciousness, the five objects are the aspect-portions apprehended by their respective consciousnesses, and form included in the mental base is the aspect-portion of the sixth consciousness. This is a clean, decisive verdict — **no form-dharma is ever an "apprehender," and no form-dharma is ever outside consciousness.**

This is why "consciousness-only, no external objects" does not mean "there is nothing at all." Form as aspect-portion **is** there — the body hurts, the wall blocks your path, the sun burns your skin — these experiences are real. What is not real is the way beings understand these experiences: assuming they are "external things standing independent of consciousness," assuming "I am inside the world," assuming "the world is an objective container and I am the person inside it." Yogācāra's correction is this — it is not "I" inside the world, but "the world" inside "my" consciousness (more precisely, both "I" and "the world" are different faces of the consciousness-substance transforming itself).

### Two Common Misreadings

⚠️ **The first misreading: equating Yogācāra with the idealism of "whatever you imagine becomes real."** It supposes that since all is consciousness-only, changing your thoughts changes matter — that when you fall sick, holding the thought "not sick" will cure you; that when you lack money, visualizing a mountain of gold will make you rich.

Correction: Yogācāra is an **epistemological** position (the constructive nature of consciousness), not an **ontological** magical idealism. "Consciousness-projected" is strictly governed by the laws of cause and effect: seeds actualize, conditions converge, and only when the four conditions are complete does anything arise (the efficient condition, the immediately antecedent condition, the objective condition, the dominant condition). You cannot project a mountain of gold whose seed is not in the ālaya-vijñāna, because there is no corresponding cause and condition. Thought can change the direction in which consciousness operates (long practice changes the direction in which seeds are perfumed), but it cannot create matter out of nothing. "All is created by mind alone" means that the arising of every dharma turns in dependence on mind — not that mind can bypass the law of cause and effect and conjure things from the void.

⚠️ **The second misreading: taking "consciousness-only, no external objects" to be the nihilism of "there is nothing out there."** It supposes that since form is consciousness-projected, form does not exist, the material world is a hallucination, and need not be taken seriously.

Correction: Yogācāra has never denied the "existence" of form; what it denies is form's "independent existence apart from consciousness." Form as aspect-portion is there — an other-dependent existence, not the substantial existence of the imagined character, and not the annihilationist nothingness of cut-off emptiness. The principle of the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* applies precisely here: **"One does not use the wisdom of emptiness to destroy form and make it empty, nor does emptiness exist because form has been destroyed"** — emptiness is not used to abolish form, and it is not that form is abolished and only then does emptiness appear. Form's mode of existence is "other-dependent" — arisen from conditions, projected by consciousness, neither substantially real nor entirely absent. You still have to pay the rent, still have to care for your body, still have to dodge the car speeding toward you — practice does not cancel these experiences; it stops misrecognizing them, within these very experiences, as substances apart from consciousness.

These two misreadings keep flipping into each other — now swelling into "whatever you imagine becomes real," now sinking into "everything is fake." Both sides are wrong. The Middle Way position is this: form is consciousness-manifested, but consciousness's projection is governed by conditions; form is not substantially real apart from consciousness, but neither is it nothing at all. It is, yet not substantially; it is empty, yet not absent.

### From Visible Projection Toward Invisible Nominal Construction

By now, the first three of the five categories of the Hundred Dharmas — mind dharmas, mental factors, and form-dharmas — have all been walked through once. Mind dharmas and mental factors are the "subjective face of consciousness" (the transformer and its accompanying functions); form-dharmas are the "objective-face projection of consciousness" (the object transformed). Together, these three cover the great majority of daily experience.

But two categories of the Hundred Dharmas remain unaddressed: **factors dissociated from mind** (twenty-four dharmas) and **unconditioned dharmas** (six dharmas).

Form, though consciousness-projected, still has a "resistant character" after all — it can be seen, heard, touched, bumped into. The **factors dissociated from mind** to be handled in the next chapter do not have even that — they are **purely nominal constructs**, without substance, without resistance, without form-aspect. Words like "time," "direction," "number," "life," "class-similarity" (what makes a human a human), "the nature of being an ordinary being" (what makes a worldling a worldling) — these are used in daily speech with perfect ease, but they do not point to an independent thing. They point to a set of relationships nominally posited after consciousness's activity has been conceptualized.

From form to factors dissociated from mind is a step from "visible projection" to "invisible nominal construction." The Hundred Dharmas is walking, step by step, from "existence" toward "emptiness" — form is existence (the projected aspect-portion), factors dissociated from mind are a thinner existence (purely nominal designations), and finally, at the unconditioned dharmas, what is to be touched is the true nature of consciousness, beyond all conditioned dharmas.
