## 8.2 The Place of Yogācāra in the Five-Teaching Classification — Between the Final Teaching and the Complete Teaching: a Staircase, or a Container?

Open Fazang's *Treatise on the Distinct Doctrinal Categories of the One Vehicle of the Avataṃsaka*, fascicle one, and read the line "the holy teachings, in their ten-thousand differentiations, are essentially of five kinds." In that instant, nearly every reader who has been through a modern education draws a staircase in the mind:

```
                                    ┌─── perfect teaching (Avataṃsaka)
                              ┌─── sudden teaching
                        ┌─── final teaching (tathāgatagarbha)
                  ┌─── initial teaching (Yogācāra, Prajñāpāramitā)
            └─── Lesser Vehicle teaching
```

Once the staircase is drawn, several conclusions follow almost on their own. The perfect teaching stands above the sudden, the sudden above the final, the final above the initial. Avataṃsaka surpasses tathāgatagarbha; tathāgatagarbha surpasses Yogācāra; Yogācāra surpasses the Lesser Vehicle.

And that whole apparatus of Yogācāra you spent a dozen chapters learning back in Part III before walking into Part VIII — the hundred dharmas, the eight consciousnesses, the fifty-one mental factors, seeds and active manifestation — on the staircase reading, that is precisely the position Part VIII leaves behind. It is "the place Avataṃsaka will eventually lead you out of."

If that reading were correct, then the toolbox of Part III could be thrown away by the time you reach Part VIII.

What this chapter argues is this: **the staircase reading was never Fazang's intention to begin with.**

⚠️ One line must be drawn first. To say that the staircase reading was not Fazang's intention is not to say that no gradation stands among the five teachings at all. In fascicle one of the *Treatise* Fazang plainly establishes a root teaching and a branch teaching, and says outright that these are "also named the ultimate and the expedient" — measured against the one vehicle, the three vehicles and the Lesser Vehicle are all expedient. This layer of the provisional and the real, the chapter does not cancel. What the chapter blocks is only the reading of that gradation as a ladder of rank along a single axis — for in the very passage where Fazang speaks of root and branch, he also says "root and branch fused into one, a single great skillful means." Root and branch stand together, the provisional and the real stand together: that is Fazang as he actually is.

### The five teachings are not a staircase

Fazang does, in fascicle one of the *Treatise*, arrange the five teachings as: Lesser Vehicle, initial teaching of the Great Vehicle, final teaching, sudden teaching, perfect teaching. None of this is in dispute. Within it, Yogācāra (together with Prajñāpāramitā) is classified as the initial teaching; the tathāgatagarbha corpus (the *Awakening of Faith*, the *Laṅkāvatāra*, the *Śrīmālā*, the *Ratnagotravibhāga*) as the final teaching; Avataṃsaka itself as the perfect teaching.

But if you read only as far as this arrangement and close the book, all you have seen is the surface order.

Elsewhere in that same fascicle, where Fazang reorganizes the entire scheme using the framework of the "common one vehicle" and the "distinct one vehicle," he writes a line that a thousand years of readers have passed over:

> One then comes to know that those very teachings of the three vehicles are not other than the distinct one vehicle. Why is this so? Because they are the very things being pointed at. There is no other matter at all.

**"Because they are the very things being pointed at."** These few words are the hinge of the whole argument.

The character *mù* (目, "to point at" / "the thing pointed at") is exquisitely chosen. It is both a verb (to point, to look, to aim at) and a noun (the eye, the object seen, the target aimed at). When Fazang writes "because they are the very things being pointed at," he is saying two things at once — the three vehicles are what the distinct one vehicle "aims at," and the three vehicles are also what the distinct one vehicle "sees."

In other words, **the distinct one vehicle is not a higher level standing above the three vehicles; it stands in the position of the "pointer," pointing at the three vehicles as its "referent."** A pointer with no referent is empty — there is nothing to point at. A referent with no pointer is isolated — it cannot be indicated. Pointer and referent are the two ends of a single act of pointing at the moon; neither can be spared.

Between the perfect teaching (Avataṃsaka) and the three vehicles (including the initial teaching of Yogācāra and the final teaching of tathāgatagarbha) there is no higher and lower. They are the two ends of a single act by which the dharma-realm points at itself.

Fazang has an even plainer line, where fascicle one treats the "mutual gathering and interpenetration of the five teachings":

> These five teachings, mutually gathered and interpenetrated, have five meanings. First, perhaps gathered as one — namely, that root and branch are fused together as a single great skillful means.

At the highest level the five teachings are in fact "one," a single "great skillful means" — in Sanskrit, *upāya*, expedient means. Note the wording. By the common understanding of Buddhism, skillful means is not ultimate truth; it is an expedient structure set up for a particular set of listeners. Fazang himself classes the entire five teachings — the perfect teaching included — as skillful means. **The ultimate truth is the dharma-realm itself, and the dharma-realm is not exhausted by any one teaching — not even the perfect teaching.**

The perfect teaching is not the top of a staircase. It is the broadest version, tuned so that some receiver can hear it.

### Three reception bands

To see the container clearly, the best way in is the structure of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra itself.

The "seven locations and nine assemblies" of the eighty-fascicle Avataṃsaka — the bodhi-seat, the Hall of Universal Light, the Trayastriṃśa heaven, the Yāma heaven, the Tuṣita heaven, the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven, back to the Hall of Universal Light, and the Jeta Grove — move the place of preaching from the earth up to the second, third, fourth, and sixth heavens of the desire realm, and back down to earth again. These nine assemblies are not a sequence in time; they are the structure of **one and the same teaching given simultaneously in different places**. The single doctrinal content of one Avataṃsaka Sūtra is received simultaneously, in different heavens, by receivers at different levels.

Map this fact back onto the three layers of the classification, and the staircase collapses:

- **The initial teaching** (Yogācāra) corresponds to the reception band of desire-realm human consciousness. Desire-realm beings are pulled around by gross sense-objects; they need the fine-grained structure of consciousness — the eight consciousnesses, the fifty-one mental factors, seeds and active manifestation, the four conditions and three natures — before they can "see" how they are getting stuck. Yogācāra is the highest-resolution map for the desire-realm human band.
- **The final teaching** (tathāgatagarbha) corresponds to a more refined reception band. Once a receiver has seen, through Yogācāra, the construction of his own consciousness, the next question rises of itself — what is the suchness on which that construction rests actually doing? Is suchness inertly unmoving, or does it participate by following conditions? The final teaching is the layer that answers this, turning suchness from "static substance" into "dynamism following conditions." It corresponds to the meditative receivers of the form realm, and to the human practitioner who has finished the basic analysis of consciousness and begun to ask ontological questions.
- **The perfect teaching** (Avataṃsaka) corresponds to the dharma-realm's own native broadcast band. When a receiver is neither stuck in the gross objects of the five desires nor boxed in by the concept "the substance of suchness," what he directly receives is the phenomenon-to-phenomenon non-obstruction of the dharma-realm itself. The perfect teaching describes not "another, higher level," but **"what the dharma-realm is saying when no receiver-filter is applied at all."**

These are three different reception bands, not three ranked grades.

There is no high and low among the three layers, because there is no high and low among the receivers. A desire-realm human receiver needs the fineness of Yogācāra — not because he is inferior, but because this simply is his reception band. To tell a desire-realm human to read Avataṃsaka directly and skip Yogācāra is to tell a radio that can only pick up FM broadcasts to receive a satellite signal — what he hears will be noise.

This is the root reason this book spends the whole of Part III, fourteen chapters, on Yogācāra. **Part III is not a low-grade version of Part VIII. Part III is the necessary precondition for Part VIII to be receivable at all in the desire-realm human band.** Without Part III, Part VIII is only a beautiful poem that never touches the ground; with Part III, the self-determined mutuality of the broad and the narrow in Avataṃsaka is the present-moment actuality of a single buddha-name in your ordinary day.

### Three crucial differences between a staircase and a container

The difference between a staircase and a container can be said in three sentences.

**First, a staircase implies upward replacement; a container does not imply replacement.** Climb to the third step, and you no longer need the first. But the three-layer container is not like that — standing in the dharma-realm of Avataṃsaka, the eight consciousnesses and the fifty-one mental factors have not vanished; they have only been embraced within the wider context of phenomenon-to-phenomenon non-obstruction. Fazang himself states it plainly in fascicle two: "the profound dependent origination of the one mind is endowed with five doctrinal gates." A single mind is endowed with five layers of doctrinal gate. The five gates are not five different minds; they are five aspects of one and the same mind.

**Second, a staircase has an absolute summit; a container has none.** A staircase implies that the perfect teaching is the summit, the ultimate truth. But the container does not permit this reading — "complete" is not "highest," it is "broadest." If the "complete" of the perfect teaching is read as "the most complete version," it reverts at once to the top of a staircase. Fazang's "perhaps gathered as one — namely, that root and branch are fused together as a single great skillful means" is precisely what blocks this misreading. Even the perfect teaching itself, in the final view, is skillful means.

**Third, a staircase assumes that broader is truer; a container assumes no such thing.** The unspoken rule of the staircase is: the broader the level of description, the closer to the truth. But the container assumes nothing of the kind — the narrow description is true for the narrow receiver, the broad description is true for the broad receiver, and there is no truer or falser, no higher or lower, between them.

Take the most concrete example. When a desire-realm human practitioner meets a stray thought in meditation, what he needs is not the final-teaching-style description "this thought is empty in nature, fundamentally not a thing" (for him this is only knowledge, with no working traction); what he needs is "which mental factor is this thought — craving, conceit, or doubt? Whose object is this mental factor — the sixth consciousness or the seventh?" This is the Yogācāra (initial teaching) mode of description, and for this desire-realm receiver, this mode is the one that is "true" (it can produce real effects in experience).

If you judge the Yogācāra description "non-ultimate" and urge this reader to "skip ahead and read Avataṃsaka directly," what he is handed is a broad description that cannot be turned into practice within his own reception band — and for him, that broad description is false (it produces no real effect in experience).

**"True" and "broad" are two independent axes.** The staircase model merges them into one axis (broad = true), and this is the staircase model's fundamental error.

⚠️ One widespread historical fantasy should be corrected here in passing. Contemporary Buddhist discourse often carries an unconscious narrative: that the Huayan school defeated the Yogācāra school in classificatory pedagogy, and that the standing of the perfect teaching was established in the course of that contest. **In the historical record, that contest never took place.** Kuiji (the founding patriarch of Yogācāra) passed away in 682; Fazang's *Treatise* was composed after 685–688, three to six years later — Kuiji could not, physically, have responded to Fazang's classification. There was no debate between the two founding patriarchs of the two schools, only succession in time. "Huayan surpasses Yogācāra" is a retrospective imagination of the Song dynasty and after, not a fact of the Tang. If even between the founding patriarchs there was no real contest, why should a contemporary reader keep this fictional victory in mind?

### Camera focal lengths — a temporary staircase

⚠️ This passage uses the analogy of camera focal lengths as a way in. It is only a heuristic staircase; once you have entered the hall, the staircase should be pulled away.

A single camera can mount three lenses of three focal lengths to photograph the same scene.

The macro lens corresponds to the initial teaching (Yogācāra): photographing a small insect on a single leaf, you can see the bristles on its legs, the grid of its compound eye, the detail of its mouthparts. It captures the finest structure, but it cannot capture which forest the tree stands in.

The normal lens corresponds to the final teaching (tathāgatagarbha): photographing the tree on which the leaf grows, you see the whole posture of the tree, the relation of root and crown. It captures the individual and its environment, but not the inner detail, and not the layout of the whole forest.

The wide-angle lens corresponds to the perfect teaching (Avataṃsaka): photographing the whole forest in a single panoramic frame, you see the relations among the trees, between forest and valley, the structure of the entire ecology. It captures the overall layout, but it cannot capture any single insect on any single leaf.

The three lenses are not in a hierarchy. The macro is not "an inadequate wide-angle," and the wide-angle is not "a macro that fails at detail." A photographer who carries only a wide-angle cannot photograph the bristles on the insect's legs; one who carries only a macro cannot photograph the panorama of the forest. **Complete photography needs all three lenses carried at once.**

⚠️ This analogy has three failure-points that must be drawn at once. One: a lens implies a photographer standing outside, switching lenses — but the heart of the mutuality of the broad and the narrow is that there is no "outside"; the dharma-realm has no external observer. Two: a lens implies the three focal lengths are switched one after another — but the three-layer reception is simultaneous, not sequential. Three: a lens implies "the same scene photographed three ways" — but the heart of the container thesis is that the three layers describe different existential objects, not merely three resolutions of one object.

As you read this, remember: the lens is only a temporary staircase propped at the entrance to the container thesis; once you have entered the hall, the staircase should be pulled away.

### The practice gate: praising the Tathāgatas — from outward pointing to inner confirmation

The doctrine laid out, we enter the body.

This chapter corresponds to Samantabhadra's second vow, praising the Tathāgatas. In the closing fascicle of the *Practices and Vows of Samantabhadra*, this vow unfolds like this:

> In each and every particle of dust, throughout all the buddha-fields of the ten directions and the three times — particles as numerous as can be exhausted in the entire dharma-realm and in space — there are buddhas as numerous as the particles of dust of all worlds; and around each and every one of these buddhas there is an ocean-assembly of bodhisattvas… With a wondrous tongue-faculty surpassing that of the goddess of eloquence, from each and every tongue-faculty there issues an ocean of inexhaustible voices, and from each and every voice there issues an ocean of all phrasings, with which I praise the ocean of qualities of all the Tathāgatas, throughout the entire future without interruption, pervading the dharma-realm without remainder.

Note the three nested oceans: one tongue-faculty issues an **ocean of inexhaustible voices**, one voice issues an **ocean of all phrasings**, and the praise is offered to the **ocean of qualities** of all the Tathāgatas. Each level's "one" expands into the next level's "innumerable," and the next level's "innumerable" is in turn the "one" of the level below it. This structure is not linear (from few to many); it is the self-determined mutuality of the broad and the narrow — "the divided is the undivided, the undivided is the divided." Any microscopic unit (one faculty, one voice, one phrasing) is at the same time broad (containing the innumerable) and narrow (being just this one).

But this chapter is going to bring that doctrinal structure of the broad-and-narrow down into the body, into one extremely simple act — into a single recitation of "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha."

To bring it down, we first need to see a quiet reversal.

You who have walked this far, answer one question honestly: when you now recite "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha," is what your mind is actually doing the same thing it was doing when you recited this line before you ever encountered the Dharma?

For nearly every reader who has walked this far, the honest answer is: it is not the same thing.

At the beginning, when you recited this line, your mind was performing the act of "an ordinary being expressing reverence to a distant awakened one." You stood here, the Buddha stood far away over there, an immeasurable kalpa of distance lay between, and your praise was a vocal event of looking up from a low place to a high place.

You who have walked to Part VIII — when you recite this line, what happens in your mind has changed. The butterfly effect of the initial aspiration in Part I, the form-is-emptiness of Part II, the hundred-dharmas-gate of Part III, the tathāgatagarbha of Part VI — this whole sequence of teaching has quietly reorganized your inner reference frame. When you now recite "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha," what your mind is actually doing is something else:

**If the Buddha's nature and my own nature are one and the same, and if the Buddha can attain these self-determined freedoms, then the possibility of these freedoms is also in me. My present praise is not only the gazing-up at a distant other; it is also the confirming of something already within me, intrinsically there from the start.**

Note this reversal. Your praise has shifted from **outward pointing** (pointing at the distant Buddha) to **inner confirmation** (confirming the Buddha-nature intrinsic to yourself). The six syllables "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha" have not changed at all in sound or form, but their function in your inner life has changed — from "an outward act of veneration" to **the reverse movement of establishing faith in yourself.**

This is the deepest cultivational reading of "in each and every particle of dust there are buddhas as numerous as the particles of dust of all worlds." A particle of dust is not a tiny physical unit; it is "this one concrete present moment" — this one reader, this one breath, this one buddha-name. The buddhas as numerous as the particles of all worlds are not innumerable buddhas far away; they are "what is recognized in this present moment as intrinsically pervasive, and of the same nature as you." When that recognition occurs, the narrow (this one particle) and the broad (the buddhas of all worlds) are intrinsically undivided.

Here there is a further structure, very deep and very easily misread — **when the faith you establish in yourself is sincere, the dharma-realm praises you back.**

⚠️ On the shallow reading, this line is easily taken to mean "the Buddha, an external personal deity, has noticed your devotion and is handing you a positive evaluation." That reading is wrong — it puts the Buddha back into the position of "external other," directly contradicting the inward reversal just described.

The correct reading is this: when the faith you establish inwardly is sincere (not performed, not coerced, not anxiously seeking confirmation), the very sincerity of that faith generates a **resonance** within the structure of the self-determined mutuality of the broad and the narrow. This resonance is not a Buddha outside awarding you points; it is the dharma-realm itself, in this single "narrow" act of self-recognition, simultaneously displaying its "broad" face. At certain moments — not necessarily at the moment of praising; possibly days, months, or years later — you will feel that something has responded to you. This is not a hallucination, nor psychological suggestion; it is the concrete manifestation, in your reception band, of the intrinsic structure of the mutuality of the broad and the narrow.

The form of the response varies from person to person. Some receive a clear insight in meditation; some meet a key person or book in daily life; some feel, in a single moment, an inexpressible something quiet their inner restlessness; some find an unaccountable steadiness in the face of an impossible decision. All of these are the dharma-realm's resonance to your own inner self-recognition — the broad's call-and-response to the narrow.

But this resonance has one strict condition — **it occurs only when the self-recognition is sincere.** If your praise still has the surface structure of "I am an ordinary being, the Buddha is a sage, I am asking the Buddha for a favor," no resonance occurs. Not because the dharma-realm is stingy, but because a false praise has not opened the reception band at all. The structure of the broad and the narrow requires the "narrow" face genuinely to acknowledge that it is itself the "broad," before the resonance has a matching band on which to land. **The dharma-realm plays no favorites — but neither does the dharma-realm transmit a signal into a closed receiver.**

This is also why so many practitioners have recited buddha-names for an entire lifetime and yet feel "nothing has answered." Not because the Buddha is stingy, but because the inner structure of the praising has never turned. Every recitation has been outward pointing; none has become inner confirmation. Every line has pushed the Buddha further away; not one has let the Buddha be recognized within.

If you have not yet experienced this counter-praise, do not be anxious — it does not mean you have done anything wrong. It only means the direction of your praising is still in the quiet process of turning. Keep on: one line, sincere, present-and-complete. The turning will happen of itself.

### A cultivation pointer: one line, sincere, present-and-complete

Praising the Tathāgatas as a daily practice is most easily misunderstood as "I must say many words of praise — with enthusiasm, with persistence, without stopping." This reading turns the "broad" of the mutuality of the broad and the narrow into "quantitatively large" — say more, say longer, say more often. But the structure of the sūtra is not this. "From each and every tongue-faculty there issues an ocean of inexhaustible voices" does not mean you must strain to make your tongue-faculty produce inexhaustible voices; it means that when you sincerely praise a single line, **the intrinsic structure of that one line is already an ocean of inexhaustible voices** — you do not need to perform addition.

To make it concrete as a daily practice: in the morning, on waking, place your palms together and recite one line, "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha." **In the moment of this single line, the mutuality of the broad and the narrow is already complete** — you do not need to make up the next ninety-nine, you do not need to stretch it to an hour, you do not need to mobilize every bodhisattva to praise alongside you. One line, sincere, present-and-complete. If you then wish to recite another, that is a separate present-and-complete moment in its own right, not an extension of the first.

"Throughout the entire future without interruption" likewise does not mean "you must strain to keep praising without stopping"; it means "the moment of sincere praise intrinsically pervades the three times" — and this pervading is not done by you; it is the structure of the broad and the narrow in operation. What the practitioner is to do is not to strain to pervade the three times, but not to block this intrinsic pervasion.

The most common inner obstacle is: "Is my praising any use? Is the praise of an ordinary being like me worthy of the ocean of qualities of the Tathāgatas?" The root of this obstacle is reading the broad and the narrow as large and small — how could a being as small as I am be worthy of qualities as great as those? The reply of the mutuality of the broad and the narrow is: you are not "worthy of"; your praise is itself part of the ocean of qualities. Between your praise and the ocean of qualities there is no question of "worthy or not" — because that question presupposes the two are separate things, whereas the structure of the sūtra is that the two are intrinsically not separate.

The deepest formula is a single line: **when you praise, look inward, do not look outward.** When you recite "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha," do not picture the Buddha far away in some pure land — let this buddha-name be recognized in your own chest, let the word "Buddha" point at the something intrinsically there in you (not your small self, but the praising-thing already there behind the small self).

Praising in accordance with the dharma, thus is unobstructedness.

### Closing

This chapter ends here.

Fazang's five-teaching classification is not a value staircase; it is the dharma-realm itself broadcasting simultaneously across different reception bands. Among Yogācāra (initial teaching), tathāgatagarbha (final teaching), and Avataṃsaka (perfect teaching) there is a container relation of the self-determined, unobstructed mutuality of the broad and the narrow — not an order of high and low. With his own eight words, "because they are the very things being pointed at, there is no other matter at all," Fazang locates the three vehicles (Yogācāra included) as the intrinsic content of the distinct one vehicle (Avataṃsaka), not as a lower tier to be transcended.

Here the staircase reading is blocked by Fazang's own text.

This chapter pairs the gate of the self-determined, unobstructed mutuality of the broad and the narrow with praising the Tathāgatas, setting out from the structure of "in a single particle of dust are contained buddhas as numerous as the particles of dust of all worlds" and arriving at the present moment of your own single buddha-name. By now you may have noticed — the object this chapter has kept pointing at, in both the doctrinal layer and the practice layer, is suchness in its original awareness. Yogācāra describes it as the fine-grained map at the level of desire-realm human consciousness; tathāgatagarbha describes it as the dynamic structure at the level of suchness following conditions; Avataṃsaka describes it as the full likeness at the level of the dharma-realm's own ground. The three layers describe one and the same original awareness of suchness, which has never left your present moment.

The next chapter's question will be more concrete — if all three layers are intrinsically operating at once, then in the bodily acts of ordinary life, how do they happen simultaneously? Take the simplest example: offering a stick of incense, a cup of water, a bouquet of flowers. How is this most ordinary bodily act, at one and the same time, an offering of phenomena (the concrete thing and act), an offering of principle (the flowing-forth of suchness following conditions), and an offering of dharma (the whole dharma-realm of phenomenon-to-phenomenon non-obstruction)? How does the "simultaneity" of the three layers unfold at the level of the body?

That is what the third chapter takes up.

> Every chapter of this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an oversight; it is a reverence. A three-dimensional brush cannot write a ten-dimensional object. All it can do is mark clearly the place where the brush is set down — so that the reader knows that, beyond this line, what remains is the reader's own dharma-eye.

This chapter's brush stops here.
