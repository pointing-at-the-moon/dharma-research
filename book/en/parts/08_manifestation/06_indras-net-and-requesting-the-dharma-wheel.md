## 8.6 The Gate of the Realm of Indra's Net—Entreating the Turning of the Dharma-Wheel: Separation Is an Illusion, So Nothing Needs to Be Transmitted

At the end of the previous chapter we walked from the dyad into the layer-upon-layer. Host-and-companion perfect-clarity is the symmetrical mutual mirroring between you and me; Indra's net is the infinitely many of you, the infinitely many others, mirroring one another in infinitely many layers. This chapter enters that infinite layering.

The core claim of this chapter can be written in a single sentence:

**A single act of entreating the Dharma in one place is already an entreaty in all ten directions, because the dharma-realm never posited "separation" as a premise to begin with, and so nothing needs to be transmitted across any distance.**

Separation is an illusion, so nothing needs to be transmitted.

### The Seed in the Āgamas: Brahmā Knows from Afar

Before we step into the Indra's net of the Avataṃsaka, let us first go back to the āgamas—and see how the act of "entreating the Dharma" was recorded in the very earliest Buddhist scriptures.

The first sūtra of fascicle ten, the "Chapter on Entreaty," of the *Ekottarāgama* records the inner activity of the Buddha shortly after his awakening. Because the profound Dharma is difficult to fathom and difficult to comprehend, and because he was concerned that people would not believe and receive it, the Buddha inclined toward silence. The turning point of the story comes in the next movement:

> At that moment, Brahmā, in the Brahmā-heaven, knew from afar what the Tathāgata was thinking. As swiftly as a strong man flexes and extends his arm, he vanished from the Brahmā-heaven and arrived before the World-Honored One, where he bowed his head to the Buddha's feet and stood to one side.

Notice two details.

First, Brahmā "**knew from afar**" what the Tathāgata was thinking. This is not ordinary knowing; it is a direct cognition that crosses the two realms of human and Brahmā, requiring no linguistic transmission.

Second, Brahmā came "**as swiftly as a strong man flexes and extends his arm**," vanishing from the Brahmā-heaven and arriving before the World-Honored One. This simile emphasizes *instantaneity*—in the time it takes to flex and extend one arm, Brahmā has already traveled from the Brahmā realm to the bodhi tree in the human world.

Together, these two action-structures hint at one thing: **between the entreaty for the Dharma and the teaching of the Dharma, no crossing of physical distance was ever required in the first place**. Brahmā does not need to write a letter, does not need to dispatch a messenger, does not need to wait for the Buddha's thought to reach the Brahmā realm—Brahmā simply knows from afar. This knowing-from-afar is not an empowerment through supernatural power; it is that the structure of thought is itself not subject to the limitation of spatial separation.

This is the earliest seed of Indra's net. The āgamas do not use the vocabulary "Indra's net," nor do they develop the full logic of mutual identity and mutual entry—the āgamas only record the event, and the event itself carries the structural seed. What the Avataṃsaka later does is to draw this seed out of the narrative and rename it in structural language.

### The Progression of the Three Verses in the "Xianshou" Chapter

The formal scriptural passage of the Avataṃsaka on Indra's net occurs in the "Xianshou" chapter (fascicle fourteen). It is a sequence of three connected verses.

The first is the **Ocean Seal Samādhi verse**:

> The forms of beings all differ; their deeds, sounds, too are measureless. All this is able to be manifested—the majestic power of the Ocean Seal Samādhi.

This speaks of "**the power to manifest**"—the forms, deeds, and sounds of beings in the ten directions can all appear simultaneously within the samādhi, as seals impressed upon the ocean.

The second is the **Avataṃsaka Samādhi verse**:

> Within a single mote of dust one enters samādhi, accomplishing samādhi in all motes of dust; yet that mote does not increase—within the one, inconceivable kṣetras universally appear.

This speaks of "**the power to contain**"—entering samādhi within a single mote accomplishes samādhi in all motes; that mote does not increase, yet inconceivable buddha-lands universally appear within the one mote. From "the power to manifest" to "the power to contain," the structure deepens by one layer—it is no longer merely that the ten directions appear together at one place; rather, that one place *is itself already* the ten directions.

The third is the **Indra's-net verse**:

> Within that one mote, many kṣetras: some with Buddhas, some without; some defiled, some pure; some vast, some narrow…
> **some like heat-shimmer in the open wilderness, some like Indra's net in the heavens.**
> As manifested within the one mote, so too is every mote of dust.

This speaks of "**mutual identity and mutual entry**"—the ten directions and the ten directions manifest one another together, without obstruction.

The three verses form a clear progression: power to manifest → power to contain → mutual identity and mutual entry.

But within the Indra's-net verse there is a structure easily overlooked. Look at the fourth line:

**"Some like heat-shimmer in the open wilderness, some like Indra's net in the heavens."**

Within one and the same verse, one and the same rhythm, one and the same "some… some…" parallel construction, two similes are juxtaposed.

These two similes each carry an independent tradition in the Mahāyāna scriptures:

- **Heat-shimmer in the open wilderness**—from the *Prajñāpāramitā* corpus, indicating that all dharmas are like the water-image formed by heat-haze in the wilderness, seemingly existent yet actually nothing. It is an *emptiness-simile*.
- **Indra's net in the heavens**—specific to the Avataṃsaka, indicating that all dharmas are like the jewels upon Indra's net in the palace of Śakra, each jewel mirroring and containing every other. It is a *mutual-containment-simile*.

One says "not," the other says "is"—one dismantles substantial existence, the other establishes mutual manifestation.

The sūtra juxtaposes these two similes within a single verse not as a rhetorical arrangement, but **as a structural definition**. Indra's net is, from the outset, not a substantial interlocking, but an illusory mutual manifestation. The jewels can mutually mirror one another precisely because the jewels themselves are like heat-shimmer in the open wilderness—empty in self-nature, still in intrinsic essence. **Only because they are empty can they contain; only by containing can they mutually manifest; only by mutually manifesting do they form the net.** Apart from the emptiness-simile, Indra's net slides into the physicalist misreading of "a connective lattice"; apart from the mutual-containment simile, the heat-shimmer simile remains at the Hīnayāna level of "all is nothing" and cannot account for the rich manifestation of the dharma-realm. Only with both similes juxtaposed is the doctrine of Indra's net complete.

This reading has a cross-chapter corroboration within the eighty-fascicle Avataṃsaka. The "Shidi" chapter, in describing the bodhisattva's cultivation through the ten grounds, gives this line: "**Dwelling in the ground of one-nature non-duality… attaining the Buddha's ten powers, entering the Indra's-net dharma-realm.**" The condition for a bodhisattva to "enter" the Indra's-net dharma-realm is "dwelling in the ground of one-nature non-duality." One-nature non-duality is precisely another name for emptiness. **The sūtra itself marks it: the admission ticket to the Indra's-net dharma-realm is not "seeing the jewels of the net" but "dwelling in one-nature non-duality."**

Indra's net is, from the outset, a concretization of emptiness—not some substantial structure existing apart from emptiness.

### The Two Seals of Zhiyan and Fazang

Zhiyan (602–668), the second patriarch of the Huayan school, in his *Huayan yisheng shixuan men*, was the first to name "the Gate of the Realm of Indra's Net" as one of the ten profound gates. Zhiyan's core definition is:

> It is like a group of mirrors mutually illuminating one another: the images of all the mirrors are seen within a single mirror; and within those images, further images of all the mirrors appear; and within each such image, further images again. Thus images manifest within images, layer upon layer, constituting an inexhaustibility that is itself inexhaustible.

Note that his primary analogy is "**a group of mirrors mutually illuminating**," not "jewel mirroring jewel." The image within a mirror is itself an unreal reflection, not a substance, and from this unreal reflection further unreal reflections arise, forming inexhaustibility. This choice stands in one lineage with the dual-simile structure of the "Xianshou" chapter—Zhiyan saw the structural depth of the dual-simile juxtaposition and internalized it into the core definition of the gate-name.

At the end of his commentary on the gate, Zhiyan added a crucial sealing-off:

> If this were explained in the manner of the Mahāyāna in general, it would be said that through supernatural transformation the great and the small are mutually entered… This is not the same as the doctrine of the One Vehicle.

Zhiyan explicitly refuses to read the mutual identity and mutual entry of Indra's net as "supernatural transformation," and positions it instead as "**the essence is of itself always thus**"—layer-upon-layer inexhaustibility is not some supernatural mechanical interaction, but a natural property of dharma-realm-arising itself.

This is the first seal, set in the seventh century. What Zhiyan seals off is this: do not read Indra's net as some kind of supernatural mechanics.

Fazang (643–712), the third patriarch of the Huayan school, in his *Huayan jing tanxuan ji*, adds a second seal upon Zhiyan's:

> Like the heavenly jewels of Śakra's net, clear and luminous, mutually reflecting one another, with reflections arising within reflections, without end… **it is not what mental cognition can reach by calculation.**

The phrase "not what mental cognition can reach by calculation" marks the realm of Indra's net directly out of the valid range of conceptual thought.

Zhiyan seals off the physicalist misreading; Fazang seals off the conceptual misreading. The two patriarchs, front to back, constitute the Huayan school's twofold line of defense against misreading Indra's net.

The importance of these two seals will be put to immediate use in the next section—for contemporary readers love nothing more than to draw physicalist analogies to Indra's net.

### The Absence of the Premise of Separation

⚠️ The cross-disciplinary analogies below serve strictly as heuristic tools, not as declarations of theoretical equivalence. The load-bearing limits of any analogy must be recovered back within the sūtras and treatises themselves.

Indra's net is perhaps the single image in the entire Avataṃsaka most liable to physicalist misreading by contemporary readers. Imagining each maṇi-jewel as a qubit, jewels mirroring jewels as quantum entanglement, one jewel containing all as the holographic principle—this package of analogies has become the standard online narrative.

⚠️ **Quantum entanglement** describes a pair of particles in an entangled state: when one is measured, the state of the other immediately manifests a correlation, no matter how far apart the two are in space. Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance," precisely because he was still thinking within the classical framework of "some kind of transmission mechanism is needed between two separated objects."

⚠️ **The holographic principle** originates in black-hole thermodynamics and later received technical expression in string theory: all the information within a volume can be completely encoded on the boundary of that volume. The popular version—"every fragment of a holographic plate contains the entire image of the plate"—is an optical-level phenomenon.

To liken these two concepts to Indra's net is superficially alluring. But the analogy commits a structural error: **it presupposes that the jewels are first separate, and only then mirror one another.** Huayan's Indra's net was never this structure—the mutual mirroring of jewels is not "transmission" but, as Zhiyan said, "the essence is of itself always thus."

This is not Huayan "knowing earlier than physics." It is that Huayan and physics are asking **entirely different questions**—physics asks "why are apparently separated things nonetheless correlated," while Huayan asks "why do we presume beforehand that there is any separation at all." The two domains of discourse do not overlap, and forcing them to overlap produces the bidirectional misreadings "physics has proved the Buddha-Dharma" and "the Buddha-Dharma is an ancient version of quantum mechanics." The former dwarfs the Buddha-Dharma into a pre-scientific intuition; the latter dwarfs physics into a footnote of the Buddha-Dharma—neither side is respected.

Now this structure can be pushed to its strongest form:

**The surprise of physics is the result of physicists' consciousness, operating within the desire-realm reception band, being driven by its own premise structure. The dharma-realm is never surprised, because the dharma-realm just is Indra's net from the beginning.**

Surprise is not a property of the fact itself; it is the reaction of a cognitive subject, operating under a given premise structure, when it encounters a counterexample. The violation of Bell's inequality, the holding of quantum entanglement—as physical facts these are simply "the world as it is." They provoke surprise only because the consciousness receiving them operates within a premise-frame that presupposes separation. Two thousand years ago, Huayan never erected that premise—and so, for Huayan, there is nothing that needs to be surprising.

⚠️ This sentence is not a belittlement of physics. The physicist's surprise is a faithful response to the tradition of the discipline, not a defect of personal cognition. To demand of physicists "do not be surprised" is neither reasonable nor respectful—it would amount to demanding that they abandon the working language of their discipline. Point-to-the-Moon only points out, from the standpoint of Huayan, where the structural root of this surprise lies, and why the same facts, within another working language, do not provoke the same surprise. Both languages have their legitimacy; both domains of work deserve respect.

### The Common Reading of Entreating the Turning of the Dharma-Wheel, and Its Demotion

Now we can return to Samantabhadra's sixth vow.

The common reading of entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel in Chinese Buddhism is: the practitioner encounters a virtuous master and requests him to remain in the world, requests him to ascend the seat and teach. This reading has its practical significance and cannot be wholly denied.

But if left at this level, entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel becomes merely a social request-gesture—you are the requester, the master is the requested, between you lies a physical, one-directional petition-relation, disconnected from the ten-direction dharma-realm. Read this way, entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel is demoted from one of Samantabhadra's ten great vows into a daily ceremony, losing the full doctrinal weight it carries as a practice-gate of the perfect teaching.

⚠️ We must distinguish this plainly from that surface reading: **entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel is not requesting a particular master to lecture on a particular sūtra; it is, in each present moment of thought, the acknowledgment that "separation is an illusion, so nothing needs to be transmitted."**

When the first thought arises in you at dawn, that thought is itself already an entreaty for the Dharma—because that thought is already in Indra's net, and all the jewels upon the net mirror one another simultaneously in that one thought; one jewel moves, all jewels move; one place entreats the Dharma, the ten directions entreat the Dharma. You need not wait for some external master, some specific sūtra, some particular Dharma-seat—**the Dharma is already turning; it needs only to be acknowledged.**

Here we must clear up an easily-made misunderstanding: you do not need first to reach some level of realization before this single entreaty becomes a joint entreaty of the ten directions. The mutual mirroring of Indra's net is what Zhiyan's seal calls "the essence is of itself always thus"—not a realm accomplished through cultivation. Whether your present thought-entreaty is already the joint entreaty of the ten directions does not depend on your realization-level; **it depends on whether you acknowledge that the structure is already thus.**

Acknowledgment just *is* the practice; non-acknowledgment remains at the surface level of petition. This acknowledgment is not the affirmation of a philosophical proposition; it is a present, actual act—just as, in the moment of knowing from afar, Brahmā performed no calculation, no effort, no preparation; the knowing-from-afar was itself already complete.

### Practice-Gate: Three Daily Scenes

The three scenes below are not "methods of practice"; they are things you are already doing—only now with one added layer, the posture of **acknowledgment**.

**Scene One: The First Thought at Dawn.**

When you awaken from sleep and consciousness has just stabilized, that first thought is the opening daily-practice point for entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel. The traditional reading will say: at this moment one should visualize the Buddhas and bodhisattvas, arouse bodhicitta, chant the morning liturgy. All correct—but each is an "added action." This chapter's reading adds one further layer: **no action need be added; only acknowledgment—this thought is itself already the jewel through which the ten-direction jewels jointly entreat the Dharma.** After acknowledgment, the traditional morning liturgy may be chanted as before, but the posture of chanting has changed—from "I am chanting" to "the ten directions chant together; this jewel of mine resonates with them."

**Scene Two: Prostrating Before the Buddha Image.**

When you prostrate in the shrine hall, in the instant the forehead touches the mat, the traditional reading will say: at this moment visualize the Buddha-body pervading the void. This chapter's reading states it more precisely: **it is not "visualizing" the Buddha-body pervading the void; it is "acknowledging" that the Buddha-body is already, originally, pervading the void.** Visualization is contrivance; acknowledgment is view. When you acknowledge that this one prostration is the simultaneous prostration of the ten-direction jewels, the prostration is no longer an isolated individual action but a single structural self-manifestation of Indra's net. Entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel is lived out within this action—not entreated before, not entreated after; the present moment of the prostration itself *is* the entreaty.

**Scene Three: Facing Favorable and Adverse Circumstances.**

When you, in the course of life, meet favorable or adverse conditions, the rise and fall of emotion is the most immediate test. The traditional reading will say: meet favorable and adverse with the mind of equanimity. The direction is correct, but in execution it often becomes suppression. This chapter's reading proposes: **acknowledge that the favorable-and-adverse circumstance is itself the dharma-realm's present Dharma-teaching upon this jewel.** The favorable is not a reward; the adverse is not a punishment; both are a self-manifestation of Indra's net. If in that moment you can acknowledge "this is the dharma-realm now turning the Dharma-wheel upon this jewel of mine," then the favorable-and-adverse are no longer objects of emotion but the content of the teaching. This acknowledgment **does not cancel emotion, but it restores emotion from "my reaction" to "the manifestation of the dharma-realm."**

These three scenes are not an exhaustive list; they are demonstrations. You may extend entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel, along the same principle, to any daily-practice point—what matters is not the number of scenes but whether the posture of "acknowledging the structure as already thus" is maintained in each.

⚠️ A methodological self-limitation. The three scenes above are only demonstrations; **they do not replace the foundational practice-path constructed in Parts One through Seven of this book.** Without the foundational practice-path, "acknowledgment" alone is not enough—it would slide into the danger of "Chan sickness" or "empty talk of perfect fusion." The posture of acknowledgment presupposes that you already possess the foundational practice-path, and only adds upon it one further layer of Huayan view. The two do not conflict, but neither can replace the other.

### Closing

This chapter ends here.

Indra's net is not a dharma-realm structure external to you; it is the structure within which you already stand in every present action. Entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel is not an outward-petitioning action; it is the natural unfolding of the Indra's-net structure upon this jewel that is you—when you acknowledge it in the present, each thought-moment of entreaty is itself the present self-manifestation of "one jewel contains all jewels."

The original awakening of suchness—the face of it that has always pervaded the ten directions and the three times, that has always been clear and luminous within all the jewels at once—never needed you to "attain" it. It only waits for you to put down the premise of "separation." What did Brahmā's knowing-from-afar accomplish? Nothing. The knowing-from-afar was always already complete—Brahmā only acknowledged it.

The Dharma-wheel has always been turning. Indra's net has always been self-manifesting. All you need do is acknowledge.

The next chapter will push this chapter's spatial structure into temporal structure—Indra's net is the spatial mutual mirroring among the jewels; the next chapter looks at the temporal mutual containment of one thought holding the three times. The vow "entreating the Buddhas to abide in the world" is paired with "the gate of subtle mutual containment and establishment"—why can the Buddha's abiding in the world be entreated by a single thought of yours? Because the separation of time is, just like the separation of space, an illusion: the Buddhas of the past, the Buddhas of the present, and the Buddhas of the future were never truly apart within the subtle structure of the dharma-realm.

> Every chapter in this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an oversight; it is an act of reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write its way to a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where the pen is set down—so the reader knows that, beyond this line, one must rely on one's own dharma-eye.

The pen of this chapter stops here.
