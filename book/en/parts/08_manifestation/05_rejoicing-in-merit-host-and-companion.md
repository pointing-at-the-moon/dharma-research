## 8.5 Rejoicing in Merit—Why a Single Thought of Rejoicing Is Already Host and Companion Perfectly Luminous

At the end of the last chapter we said that the moment the covering is opened and you reconnect to the field of conditions, the next movement to surface naturally is rejoicing.

What this chapter has to handle is—**why it should be rejoicing**.

### Three Doubts

The phrase "rejoicing in merit" raises three objections in almost every beginning reader who hears it.

**First**: why should rejoicing carry merit? You hear of someone else doing good, and your heart lifts in sympathy for a moment—does this really count? No money given, no labor spent, no time invested; on what basis do you share in another's merit?

**Second**: surely this is too easy? If rejoicing alone produces merit, does it not amount to "cheap free-riding"? Others labor at their practice, and I just stand beside them and applaud?

**Third**: why does the Avataṃsaka Sūtra pair this one vow with "the gate of host-and-companion in perfect luminous endowment"? Why is what looks like the most abstruse of the ten profound gates matched with what looks like the easiest of the ten vows?

These three questions are interlocked. The first belongs to the level of doctrine—what is the ground for saying rejoicing carries merit at all? The second belongs to the level of ethics—if it does, does the practice not become frivolous? The third belongs to the level of structure—how does this correspondence mesh within Huayan teaching?

This chapter answers all three, but the mode of answer is neither apologetic ("actually it's quite hard, you know") nor dogmatic ("the scriptures simply say so"). It draws the two ends of the question—the Āgama ontology of karma and the Huayan structural account of the dharma-realm—to the same table, to see how they mesh into one complete answer.

The answer is best stated in advance: **the merit of rejoicing lies not in external form but in *cetanā* (volition); and once *cetanā* genuinely arises, then because of the host-and-companion perfect-luminous structure, this single thought necessarily encompasses all the roots of goodness throughout the dharma-realm. The Āgama ontology at the base prevents rejoicing from being read as cheap free-riding; the Huayan dharma-realm account at the summit prevents rejoicing from being read as a private exercise in feeling. Only when both tiers mesh is the problem resolved.**

### The Āgamas: The Substance of Karma Is "Volition"

To understand Huayan's "rejoicing in merit," one must—counterintuitively—first return to the Āgamas, to see how the Buddha, in the earliest stratum of teaching, defined karma and merit.

The *Cetanā-sūtra* of the *Madhyama-āgama* gives the most direct statement on the substance of karma:

> If there is karma performed with volition, I declare that one must necessarily experience its result, whether in the present life or in a later one. If there is karma performed without volition, I declare that one need not experience its result.

"Performed with volition" means "performed with *cetanā*." Here the Buddha locks the very condition for karmic establishment onto a single Sanskrit word—*cetanā*, rendered into Chinese as "thought" or "volition," meaning will, decision, the active orientation of the inner mind. **Where there is volition, there is karma; where there is no volition, there is no karma.** A person who in sleep unconsciously kicks another has no volition, and no karma forms; a person who, awake and deliberately, kicks another has volition, and karma forms. The motion is the same; the quality of the karma is entirely different.

Also in the *Madhyama-āgama*, the *Upāli-sūtra* records a debate. The Nirgrantha disciple Upāli (a common Indian name—this is not the same person as the Buddha's disciple, the Venerable Upāli) maintained that bodily punishment is the gravest. The Buddha replied plainly:

> Among these three karmas, which are similar in this respect, I designate mental karma as the gravest.

Of the three karmas—bodily, verbal, mental—the Buddha alone judges **the mental as the gravest**. This is not an abstract ranking of values; it is a direct statement of karmic ontology: an outward act constitutes karma precisely because it carries an inner *cetanā*; remove the *cetanā*, and the act is merely physical motion, no karma at all.

*Saṃyukta-āgama* 1288 carries this ontology further, into the scene of giving:

> One who can give purely from little wealth, while another with great wealth cannot give. Giving practiced with a pure mind—such giving's reward is equal.

Giving from little wealth with a pure mind, and giving from great wealth with a turbid mind, carry **equal merit**; indeed, little wealth with a pure mind even surpasses great wealth with a turbid mind. **Material quantity does not determine merit; the purity of mind determines merit.**

Taken together, these three passages give the Āgama ontology of karma: **the substance of karma is *cetanā*, not external form; the quality of mind determines the quality of karma, and material magnitude is irrelevant.**

### The Lay-Girl Muni: Neither Joy nor Giving Can Substitute for the Vow

But if one were to infer from this that "merely feeling a moment of joy in the heart produces merit," the Buddha himself would object.

The *Ekottarika-āgama*, fascicle thirty-eight, records a crucial counter-case. The princess Muni hears that an elder bhikṣu is begging through the lanes for lamp-oil with which to make an offering to the Tathāgata Ratnagarbha, and at the mere hearing of the Buddha's name she is "filled with joyful exultation"—precisely the inner movement that the word "rejoicing" later names—and there and then gives him the whole of the sesame oil and lamp-wicks herself. Yet the elder bhikṣu who receives her gift and makes the vow receives the prediction of buddhahood, while she, having made no vow, does not. The Buddha then says:

> If the elder bhikṣu does not make the vow, he will in the end not accomplish the buddha-path. The merit of vow-making cannot be calculated.

In plain terms: **even when the gift is actually made**, joyful exultation, if not joined to vow-and-dedication, **does not accomplish the buddha-path**. The Buddha, in his own words, denies the reading "rejoicing alone is meritorious."

This counter-case is decisive. Already at the Āgama level it distinguishes **true rejoicing** from **counterfeit rejoicing**: rejoicing in which *cetanā* genuinely arises and which is sealed by vow-and-dedication is true; joy that remains merely verbal or affective does not count.

The Āgamas do not teach the simple proposition "rejoicing produces merit"; the Āgamas teach the ontology "the substance of karma is volition"—**and true rejoicing carries merit precisely because it falls within this ontology, not because it bypasses it**.

The structure of this section directly answers the first two questions raised at the opening of the chapter.

**The answer to the first question, "why should rejoicing carry merit"**: because the substance of merit is *cetanā*, not external form. The single thought of true rejoicing is the purest face of mental karma—*cetanā* genuinely resonating in the presence of another's wholesome act—and this thought lands directly upon the karmic ontology the Buddha himself defined, and lands upon the gravest of the three karmas, the mental.

**The answer to the second question, "surely this is too easy"**: "easy" is the misreading of an ordinary person who looks only at outward form. True rejoicing is not easy. Joy without vow-and-dedication, without the genuine resonance of mental activity, is denied merit-status by the Buddha himself. This threshold is more interior than "the body performing a wholesome act," and harder to counterfeit—for its only witness is the practitioner's own mind in that very thought.

### Huayan: The Gate of Host-and-Companion in Perfect Luminous Endowment

The Āgamas have established the ontology at the base. Huayan, taking it up, presses this ontology to a dharma-realm scale.

The closing fascicle of the *Forty-fascicle Avataṃsaka*'s "Samantabhadra-caryā-praṇidhāna" unfolds "rejoicing in merit" thus:

> As for what is called rejoicing in merit: in all the buddhas and tathāgatas throughout the exhaustion of the dharma-realm, the realm of empty space, the ten directions and three times, and the kṣetras of all buddhas numerous as dust-motes—from the first arousing of the aspirational mind for the sake of all-knowledge … all their roots of goodness, I entirely rejoice in. And in all the worlds of the ten directions, among the six destinies and four modes of birth, in every kind of being, whatever merit there is, down to a single dust-mote's worth, I entirely rejoice in. … As the realm of empty space is exhausted, as the realm of beings is exhausted, as the karma of beings is exhausted, as the defilements of beings are exhausted—so too my rejoicing will have no exhaustion; thought after thought continuously, without interruption, with no weariness of body, speech, or mind.

This passage must be unfolded structurally.

First, **the cognitive object is all the roots of goodness throughout the dharma-realm**. What is rejoiced in is not "some particular wholesome act of some particular person," but the full array of wholesome roots of "all the buddhas and tathāgatas throughout the exhaustion of the dharma-realm, the realm of empty space, the ten directions and three times … all bodhisattvas … every kind of being among the six destinies and four modes of birth." The scope of the cognitive object *is* the entirety of the dharma-realm.

Second, **"down to a single dust-mote's worth, I entirely rejoice in."** Not even the most minute dust-mote of goodness is omitted. This is not "inclusive as far as possible"; it is "without a single omission."

Third, **"thought after thought continuously, without interruption."** The temporal structure is continuous thought-moments without break, not a special event at one particular moment.

Fourth—and this is the most critical—**between the scope of the cognitive object and the rejoicer's single thought there is no intermediary whatsoever**. The rejoicer does not need first to "know" which being is performing which wholesome act, and where, in order to rejoice; the cognitive object of this single thought of rejoicing is already, in the present moment, the entirety of the dharma-realm.

This absence of intermediation is precisely the scriptural origin of Huayan's "gate of host-and-companion in perfect luminous endowment."

Fazang, in his *Tànxuán jì* (Record of Searching Out the Profundities of the Avataṃsaka), defines this gate as follows:

> Because in this perfect-teaching dharma-realm principle never arises in isolation, it must arise together with its retinue. … Hosts among hosts and companions among companions do not see each other, while host-turning-to-companion and companion-turning-to-host are perfect-luminous and complete in virtue.

The definition has three layers, and must be unfolded in turn.

**Principle never arises in isolation; it must arise together with its retinue.** No single dharma in the perfect-teaching dharma-realm can stand alone—when it arises, it arises bearing its retinue. There is no such thing as "an isolated dharma."

**Hosts among hosts and companions among companions do not see each other.** When one dharma is host, you cannot see another dharma also as host (two hosts cannot share one frame); when one dharma is companion, you cannot see another also as companion (two companions cannot share one frame). For "host" and "companion" are positional roles relative to a perspective, not substantive properties—a single angle of observation can have only one host.

**Host-turning-to-companion and companion-turning-to-host are perfect-luminous and complete in virtue.** And yet "when I am host, you are companion" and "when you are host, I am companion"—these two angles can both be fully established at once. A single dharma can be host and can equally be companion, entirely by the switching of perspective. "Perfect-luminous" means "complete viewed from any angle"; "complete in virtue" means **"the companion in that moment already possesses, in full, the virtue of the host."**

Put this structure back into rejoicing: when you rejoice in someone's merit, they are host and you are companion. But **the companion possesses the host's virtue**: you are not "given a share of their merit"; your single thought of rejoicing already possesses the full virtue of that merit. And when the focus switches, when another rejoices in you, you are host and they are companion, and their single thought of rejoicing already possesses your full virtue.

Fazang, in his *Huányuán guān* (Contemplation of the Return to the Source), gathers this structure into a single formula of practice:

> Wherever a single dharma is raised, host and companion are immediately gathered together, layer upon layer without end.

The single thought of rejoicing is exactly this "single dharma raised." In that thought, host and companion are immediately gathered together—the dharma-realm in its entirety, as cognitive object, is the companion, while your single thought is the host; and at the same time, reverse the focus, and all rejoicers throughout the dharma-realm are hosts, while you are companion. Layer upon layer without end.

This is the literal realization, in rejoicing, of the four words "host and companion perfectly luminous."

⚠️ A necessary correction here—from "consciousness-centered" to "dharma-realm-centered." In certain earlier classifications, one of the ten profound gates was called "the gate of mind-only reversal accomplishing goodness"—all things turn upon mind, and mind is the center. Fazang renamed this gate "the gate of host-and-companion in perfect luminous endowment," and his intent was this: there is no fixed center. Every dharma can be center, and every dharma can equally be retinue. Rejoicing is not "this heart of mine reaching out to embrace another" (a mind-only structure, in which mind remains the center); it is "the other and I being reciprocally host and companion within the dharma-realm" (a host-companion structure, with no fixed center). The former still smuggles in a self/other duality with its hierarchy of subordination; the latter dissolves that subordination root and branch.

Now the third question, raised at the opening, can be answered.

**The answer to the third question, "why the correspondence with the gate of host-and-companion in perfect luminous endowment"**: because what host-and-companion perfect luminosity prescribes is precisely the structure "the companion at that moment already possesses the full virtue of the host." The rejoicer (companion) and the one rejoiced in (host) share substance at the level of *cetanā* and share virtue within the host-companion perfect-luminous structure of the dharma-realm. This correspondence is not the explicit text of any commentary—Fazang nowhere directly maps this vow onto this gate—it is a conclusion compelled from Huayan's own doctrine. Given that the dharma-realm's structure is host-companion perfect luminosity, given that the substance of merit is *cetanā*, and given that the cognitive object of rejoicing is already the entirety of the dharma-realm, the conclusion "rejoicing in merit = the cultivation-face of host-companion perfect luminosity" follows necessarily from these three premises. It is not arbitrary pairing.

### The Two-Tier Clamp: The Base Guards Against the Spurious, the Summit Breaks the Constricted

Now the Āgamas and Huayan can be drawn to the same table.

**The base tier (Āgama)**: the substance of karma is *cetanā*, not external form. True rejoicing requires *cetanā* genuinely arising; counterfeit rejoicing does not count. This tier guarantees that rejoicing cannot be read as the cheap free-riding of "verbal joy is meritorious."

**The summit tier (Huayan)**: once *cetanā* genuinely arises, then because of the dharma-realm's own host-companion perfect-luminous structure, the cognitive object of this thought is the entirety of the dharma-realm, and this thought already possesses the full virtue of the one rejoiced in. This tier guarantees that rejoicing cannot be read as an exercise in individual psychological feeling.

**The base guards against the spurious; the summit breaks the constricted.** Neither tier can be removed.

The reader may ask: are these not two independent propositions, and is bolting them together not a forced synthesis?

It is not. The two are one and the same teaching unfolded at two scales.

The Āgama *cetanā*-ontology is not the narrow proposition "the substance of karma lies in mind." Its real content is: **the establishment of karma does not depend on external form; it depends on the structure of the inner mind.** Once one accepts this content, "the structure of the mind" must inevitably press the further question—how wide is the scope of this structure? The interiority of a single individual? Or some larger structure?

The Āgamas themselves do not answer this question head-on. The Āgamas' concern is individual liberation, so it suffices for them to stop at the level of "the individual *cetanā*." When Huayan takes the question up, it presses it to its conclusion—if *cetanā* is the substance of karma, and the substance of karma does not depend on external form, then by what is the genuine scope of *cetanā* determined? **Huayan's answer: by the structure of the dharma-realm itself.** Since the dharma-realm is host-companion perfectly luminous and inexhaustibly layered, the genuine scope of *cetanā* is host-companion perfectly luminous and inexhaustibly layered.

In other words, Huayan does not add a dharma-realm scale onto the Āgama *cetanā*-ontology; Huayan **makes explicit a question of scope already latent within the Āgama *cetanā*-ontology but never unfolded there**. Base and summit are not two independent layers; they are a single ontology stated at two scales.

### Conduct-Buddha, Not Position-Buddha

The result of this two-tier clamp immediately invites a dangerous misreading—"a single thought of true rejoicing already possesses the full virtue of the one rejoiced in" sounds like "an ordinary being, in one thought, takes the seat of buddhahood."

This misreading must be handled on the spot. In his *Wǔjiào zhāng* (Treatise on the Doctrinal Divisions of the One Vehicle of Huayan), Fazang sets up a precise distinction:

> Because it is accomplished by faith, it is the conduct-Buddha, not the position-Buddha.

**Conduct-Buddha**, not **position-Buddha**.

- **Position-Buddha**: reaching the stage of Marvelous Awakening within the fifty-two-stage sequence (the Ten Faiths, Ten Abidings, Ten Practices, Ten Dedications, Ten Grounds, Equal Awakening, Marvelous Awakening). This is buddhahood as sequential accomplishment.
- **Conduct-Buddha**: at the level of conduct, the buddha-virtues are already complete. Though one's stage-position is still that of an ordinary being at the faith-stage, what one does is already a buddha's conduct.

This distinction cuts cleanly through the vulgarizing danger of "buddhahood at the fullness of faith." Buddhahood at the fullness of faith does not mean "believe a little and take the buddha-seat"; it means "the very thought in which faith is accomplished is, at the level of conduct, a buddha's act." The stage-positions still have to be traversed in sequence, but the conduct-virtue is already perfectly complete.

The bearing on rejoicing is direct: **the very thought of rejoicing is, at the level of conduct, a buddha's own act of rejoicing**—for buddhas everywhere rejoice in the merits of all sentient beings, and your single thought of rejoicing shares with the buddhas' rejoicing one and the same *cetanā*-substance. Though your stage is that of an ordinary being, your conduct is already a buddha's. There is no contradiction, because stage and conduct are two different dimensions.

⚠️ Two directions of slippage must be guarded against here at once.

One direction is **rushing ahead**: reading "a single thought of rejoicing is already a buddha's conduct" as "one thought of rejoicing seats me on the buddha-throne"—wrong. The stage remains that of an ordinary being; one does not leap to Marvelous Awakening on a single thought of true rejoicing. Whoever misreads conduct-Buddha as position-Buddha turns either to arrogance (imagining oneself already a buddha) or to disappointment (discovering one is not)—both extremes proceed from the same misreading.

The other direction is **dejection**: reading "the stage of an ordinary being" as "an ordinary being cannot possibly have buddha-virtue, so rejoicing is only going through the motions"—also wrong. This reads conduct-virtue as a by-product of stage-attainment: no stage, no virtue. But Fazang's distinction is made precisely to block this reading: **conduct-virtue is presently complete; stage-position is walked in sequence; the two do not conflict.**

Stage-position and conduct-virtue are two different dimensions. That you walk very slowly along one of them does not prevent you from arriving, in the present moment, along the other.

### The Practice Gate: From Jealousy to Indifference to Rejoicing

With the doctrine laid down, we enter the body—or rather, we enter your first response.

You hear of someone doing good—a friend takes up the precepts; an elder funds the building of a stūpa; a master enters a ten-year retreat; a stranger rushes into the flames in an earthquake to save a life. In the instant of hearing, what is your heart's first response?

There are three typical responses, corresponding to three entirely different inner structures.

**Jealousy** — **the theory of separated individuals**. You treat the other as a competitor; their merit seems to diminish your own position. This structure presupposes "merit is a finite resource—the more they have, the less I have." But the Āgamas have already broken this presupposition: the substance of merit is mind, not matter; there is no "conservation of total quantity." Jealousy's root error is that **it materializes merit**.

**Indifference** — **the pluralism of unrelated atoms**. You treat the other as someone with no bearing on you; their merit has nothing to do with you. This structure does not materialize merit as jealousy does, but it cancels the host-companion structure and reads the dharma-realm as a collection of atomized individuals—each person cultivating within a closed system, none touching another. Indifference's root error is that **it cancels the dharma-realm's native host-companion structure**.

**Rejoicing** — **the perfect teaching of host-and-companion mutual embrace**. You treat the other as a being who, within one and the same dharma-realm, is reciprocally host and companion with you; when their single thought of wholesome action arises, then because of the host-companion perfect-luminous structure, your single thought of rejoicing already, in that very moment, possesses the full virtue of theirs. This is not the training of a feeling; it is the transformation of an inner structure—**from "separated individual" into "node of a mutually-embracing host-and-companion dharma-realm."**

From jealousy to indifference is easy; one need only move the other outside one's circle of concern.

From indifference to rejoicing is hard; it requires drawing the other into the deeper structure of "another self within the same dharma-realm."

This "another self" is not metaphor; it is the literal realization of host-companion perfect luminosity—when you take yourself as host, the other is companion possessing your full virtue; when you take the other as host, you are companion possessing the other's full virtue. The duality of self and other temporarily dissolves within the genuine *cetanā*-resonance of true rejoicing, because from the standpoint of dharma-realm structure, **"I" and "the other" were always two positions within a host-companion angle, never two independent entities**.

### A Note for Cultivation

What you can practice today is utterly simple.

The next time you hear of someone doing good—a family member, a friend, a stranger in the news, some kind act online—pause for one second and ask yourself a single question:

In this moment, what is my first response? Jealousy? Indifference? Or rejoicing?

You do not need to force yourself to leap from jealousy to rejoicing. Forced joy is emotion-management, not true rejoicing. What is needed is **to see clearly the three inner structures behind the three responses**. The moment you see clearly, the structure loosens of itself.

⚠️ The mark of true rejoicing is not the high excitement of feeling, but a calm of "things being just as they ought to be." It is not that you have taken the other's merit into your account; it is that you have recognized a fact that was true of you all along, there in that dharma-realm. Rejoicing is not excitement; it is **recognition**.

Once a day is enough. Do it for a few months and watch whether your first response changes.

When it changes, you will not have changed it on purpose. On some morning, on some day, you will find that the thing welling up naturally in your heart when you hear of another doing good is no longer comparison, but **a sympathy as natural as breathing**. That sympathy is the genuine rising of *cetanā* within rejoicing—and it is the host-companion perfect-luminous structure of the dharma-realm unfolding, once, truly, at your position.

This event is not something you "do"; it is something you recognize.

### Closing

Here the chapter rests.

The Āgamas tell you why this single thought of rejoicing counts: because the substance of merit is *cetanā*, not external form; the Buddha himself judged mental karma the gravest, and the single thought of true rejoicing is the purest face of mental karma.

Huayan tells you how far this single thought of rejoicing reaches: because the dharma-realm is host-companion perfectly luminous from the first, your single thought of rejoicing already possesses, in the present moment, the full virtue of the one rejoiced in. This is not the sharing of a quantity; it is identity of virtue at the level of structure.

Read together, rejoicing truly stands: **the Āgama base keeps it from becoming free-riding; the Huayan summit keeps it from becoming emotion-management.**

Suchness, the original awareness, has never drawn a border between "you" and "the other." The barrier between you and the other is a covering, not the dharma-realm. The covering opens—this was the work of the last chapter—and host-companion perfect luminosity is present.

The next chapter will continue unfolding this host-companion structure from another angle. Host-companion perfect luminosity is a two-pole symmetry (I, the other); Indra's net is an endless layering (interreflection upon interreflection, light illumining light). Chapter Six takes "the gate of the realm of Indra's net" as its window and unfolds Samantabhadra's sixth vow—petitioning the turning of the dharma wheel. When you sincerely ask the buddhas, bodhisattvas, or a spiritual friend to turn the dharma wheel, you are not asking a distant person to do a particular thing; you are activating one node within Indra's net—and that activation resounds at once, simultaneously, throughout the layered eyes of the net.

> Every chapter in this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an omission; it is reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write to a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where the pen is set down—so the reader knows that, beyond this line, one must rely on one's own dharma-eye.

The pen of this chapter rests here.
