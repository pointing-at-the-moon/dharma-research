## 8.11 The Ten Great Vows Beneath the Ten Windows — One Is Ten, Ten Is One

The end of the previous chapter laid out the whole doctrinal skeleton of the Avataṃsaka's ten great vows. From chapter one through chapter ten, vow by vow and gate by gate, vows one through ten and gates one through ten were locked into place, pair by pair.

But you have, in fact, already walked past ten windows. Each chapter of this eighth part, from the first through the tenth, was itself a window. And each window failed in its own particular way — the gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence failed at "simultaneity" (the simultaneity that the linearity of time cannot express); the gate of unhindered freedom of broad and narrow failed at "broad and narrow" (the compatibility that the opposition of scale cannot express); the gate of mutual identity and sovereign freedom of all dharmas failed at "mutual identity"; the gate of hidden-and-manifest mutual completion failed at "hidden and manifest"; the gate of host-and-guest perfect clarity failed at "host and guest" —

What this chapter does is a **reflexive turn**. It does not open an eleventh window — there is no eleventh window. What this chapter does is to look at all ten windows **once more, as a single whole**.

Each window fails in one particular way. **Ten failures added together compose the complete shape of the dharma-realm** — not a shape written out directly, but the superimposed trajectory of ten failures, together tracing the outline of an object that cannot be written out directly.

### One-Totality and Nine-Differentiations: Not a Flat Row of Ten

The ten windows are not ten observational angles laid out flat in a row. This point must be pinned down first.

Chengguan states it plainly in fascicle eleven of the *Elaborated Exegesis of the Avataṃsaka*:

> Within the gate of simultaneity, the lower nine gates are contained; the lower nine gates do not contain simultaneity.

The gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence is the "totality"; the latter nine gates are the "differentiations." The totality contains the parts, but the parts do not contain the totality — this is **one-directional**.

But slightly later in the same passage, Chengguan says something that turns it around:

> Now, speaking in terms of non-separation, the nine gates likewise, following the example of simultaneity, each contain the nine gates.

"Speaking in terms of non-separation" — when the focus shifts from the totality-differentiation relation to the relation of mutual containment, each differentiation-gate **also contains the other nine gates in its own right**.

There are two grammars here.

**The totality-grammar of containment**: the gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence, as "totality," contains the latter nine gates. This is the position already shouldered in chapter one — the vow to revere all Buddhas was paired with the gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence, because in the physical instant of one act of reverence, all Buddhas of the ten directions and three times are simultaneously revered; this is the totality-gate's structural containment of the whole.

**The differentiation-grammar of containment**: each differentiation-gate, when you enter into its inner structure, **also** contains the other nine gates. This is performative containment — not a "natural" structural containment, but the discovery, when you actually open the gate and look, that "so the others are here too."

The first scale was installed back in chapter one. What this chapter takes up is the second scale — **each window contains the shadows of the other nine windows**.

This is not a linear summing of "ten times one." It is the stereoscopic mutual containment of "ten times ten."

### One Gate Contains Ten Vows: The Gate of Simultaneous Mutual Correspondence × the Ten Great Vows

The first pivot demonstration. Let all ten great vows stand beneath a single window — the gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence — and watch how each of them manifests itself simultaneously within that one window.

**Reverence for all Buddhas** within simultaneity: in the physical instant of one prostration, all Buddhas of the ten directions and three times are revered at once. This was fully unfolded in chapter one.

**Praise of the Tathāgatas** within simultaneity: the nine syllables of "Homage to the Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha" take only about three seconds, but in the very moment of those three seconds, every act of praising a Buddha throughout the ten directions and three times corresponds simultaneously — not "it will arrive later," but "it already is, right now."

**Extensive offering** within simultaneity: in the instant one stick of incense is lit, all offering instruments are established at once. Incense, flowers, lamps, water, fruit all arise together in this one stick of incense — not "offered in sequence," but "one offering is all offering."

**Repentance of karmic obstructions** within simultaneity: in the instant of one thought of repentance, the immeasurable karma accumulated through beginningless aeons is purified all at once. The "manifest" of karma (its phenomenal marks, vivid and distinct) and the "hidden" of karma (its ultimate emptiness of nature) are both established in this one thought — not the sequence of "first confirm the karma, then seek its emptiness," but two faces manifesting themselves simultaneously in the same one thought.

**Rejoicing in merit** within simultaneity: in the instant of one thought of rejoicing, the immeasurable merit that others have cultivated over immeasurable aeons is contained, all at once, within this one thought of yours. Position and practice are established together in this one thought — your position is that of an ordinary being, yet your practice is already that of a Buddha.

**Entreating the turning of the Dharma-wheel** within simultaneity: in the instant of requesting the Dharma in one place, the events of requesting the Dharma in all the Buddha-realms of the ten directions are established at once. Every jewel of Indra's net, in this one thought, simultaneously requests the Dharma, hears the Dharma, turns the Dharma — not occurring one after another, but manifesting themselves simultaneously.

**Entreating the Buddhas to remain in the world** within simultaneity: in the instant of one thought entreating them to remain, all the instants to the very limit of the future are contained at once. The most subtle thought of "entreating them to remain" and the most vast "limit of the future" are established together in this one thought. **Time unfolds from this one thought; it is not that this one thought happens within time.**

**Constantly following the Buddhas in study** within simultaneity: in the instant of studying under one Buddha, the teachings of all Buddhas of the ten directions are studied at once. The objects of study are compatible-yet-distinct in this one thought — one is many, many is one, yet the one does not become the many and the many does not become the one. In the very moment you study Śākyamuni, you are at once studying all the Buddhas of the ten directions, with no need to choose.

**Perpetually according with sentient beings** within simultaneity: in the instant of according with one sentient being, the event of according with all sentient beings is established at once. Every sentient being accorded with is the dharma-realm revealing the Dharma to itself by entrusting it to a phenomenon — and this revealing does not happen "after" according with this one; it is in the very same instant as "according with this one."

**Universal dedication of all merit** within simultaneity: in the instant of one thought of dedication, the ten times fold into one thought. Past's three times, present's three times, future's three times, and the totalizing three times all arise simultaneously in this one thought — dedication is not a transmission across time, but the unfolding of time out of this one thought.

When all ten vows stand beneath one window, a clear structure surfaces:

**The "action" of each vow is not an event within a temporal sequence; it is the dharma-realm manifesting itself simultaneously upon that action.**

Prostrating, praising, offering, repenting, rejoicing, requesting the Dharma, entreating them to remain, studying under the Buddhas, according with beings, dedicating — the physical instants of these ten actions are not "isolated points in time." They are the dharma-realm's simultaneous correspondence with itself across ten of its own facets.

The gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence is not "the one window that corresponds to reverence for all Buddhas." It is **the totalizing window for all ten vows**.

### One Vow Contains Ten Gates: Reverence for All Buddhas × the Ten Profound Gates

The pivot in the reverse direction.

Reverence for all Buddhas is not merely "the vow that pairs with the gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence." It is the one action among the ten vows that lets **all ten gates be seen at once**. This is why it stands first in the sequence of the ten vows — not first in temporal order, but first in structural order.

When you complete a single prostration before the shrine, this one prostration opens all ten windows at once:

**The gate of simultaneous mutual correspondence**: one prostration gathers the ten directions and three times. The one who reveres, the one revered, and the act of reverence — all three are simultaneously a single dependent origination.

**The gate of unhindered freedom of broad and narrow**: the physical single prostration is "narrow" (a finite bodily posture); all the Buddhas of the ten directions and three times who are revered are "broad" (pervading the inexhaustible dharma-realm). The narrow does not become the broad, the broad does not become the narrow — this one prostration is still a finite physical action, yet its function already covers the dharma-realm broadly.

**The gate of one-and-many mutual containment without identity**: to revere Śākyamuni is to revere all the Buddhas of the ten directions. Śākyamuni is still Śākyamuni — his thirty-two marks and eighty fine features remain his alone; the Buddhas remain the Buddhas, and do not melt into a fused mass. The one and the many are mutually contained yet not identical.

**The gate of mutual identity and sovereign freedom of all dharmas**: the you who reveres and the Buddha who is revered are mutually identical. When the inner grammar of this prostration is true reverence, "I am revering" and "the Buddha is revered" are not two separate matters but two positions in a single event.

**The gate of hidden-and-manifest mutual completion**: in prostrating, the "mark of self" (manifest) and "no-self" (hidden) are established together. The physical act of prostration requires the position called "I," but the inner grammar of prostration requires the original ground called "no-self." **"I" and "no-self" are established together in this one prostration — and this is precisely the literal realization of hidden-and-manifest mutual completion.**

**The gate of subtle containment and establishment**: the three-second lowering of the body is subtle; the "dharma-realm event of revering all Buddhas" that it corresponds to is the vastness of immeasurable aeons. The subtle and the vast are established together in this one prostration, without either's scale being destroyed.

**The gate of Indra's net**: your one prostration is reflected in the prostrations on immeasurable net-jewels, and the net-jewels' prostrations are reflected in yours. In the instant you lower your body, every jewel of Indra's net simultaneously displays an image of you prostrating; and within each jewel's reflected image of "you prostrating," the prostration of the entire net is again reflected. Layer upon layer, without end.

**The gate of dependence on phenomena to reveal the Dharma**: by the phenomenon of "lowering the body," the Dharma of "subject and object are not two" is revealed. The lowering is the phenomenal mark; subject-and-object-not-two is the Dharma. The phenomenon is the vehicle, the Dharma is what is carried — when you lower your body, the dharma-realm reveals, through this concrete phenomenal mark, the Dharma that "the one who reveres and the one revered are originally not two."

**The gate of ten-time distinct establishment**: this one prostration gathers all prostrations past and future. Your prostration today, your prostration yesterday, your prostration ten years ago, all your prostrations since beginningless aeons — all are gathered, at once, in the instant of this one prostration. And this one prostration gathers the future too — all the prostrations you will yet perform across inexhaustible aeons to come are also established together in this one prostration. The ten times fold into a single prostration.

**The gate of host-and-guest perfect clarity**: when you lower your body, the Buddhas are host and you are guest; simultaneously, you are host and the Buddhas are guest. The guest possesses the host's virtue — when you are the guest, you already fully possess the prostration-merit of all Buddhas.

The ten windows are opened at once upon this one action.

**This is why reverence for all Buddhas stands first in the sequence of the ten vows.** It is the totalizing vow that lets all ten gates be seen simultaneously.

### Ten Is Not Ten; Ten Is the Index of the Inexhaustible

Reading this far, a question may surface: why exactly ten? Why not nine or eleven?

Fazang, in the *Record of Investigations into the Mysteries of the Avataṃsaka*, gives a distilled answer:

> Ten is the round number, inexhaustible.

**The round number.** This concept must be read carefully. The round number is not a quantitative count, as in "ten items"; it is "the fullness of a structure from beginning to completion" — a cycle complete. And because of the cyclical nature of "roundness," ten naturally points toward the inexhaustible: the end of each cycle is the beginning of the next.

Zhiyan, in the *One-Vehicle Ten Profound Gates of the Avataṃsaka*, puts it more deeply still:

> If the one is established in its own nature, the ten cannot be accomplished. If the ten cannot be accomplished, neither can the one.

The establishment of the one depends on the ten; the establishment of the ten depends on the one. The one and the ten are mutually conditioning; neither can stand alone.

Applied to the structure of this book: the ten windows are not an exhaustive enumeration; added together they are a single "round" — one complete observational structure. But the moment this round is complete, **it immediately points to the next round** — the ten gates each contain ten gates, which is one hundred gates; the hundred gates each contain ten gates, which is one thousand gates; layer upon layer, without end.

Ten is an indicator of fullness, not a closed count.

If this book were to exhaust every direction, it would have to write one hundred pivot demonstrations, each containing one hundred finer correspondences, forming ten thousand sub-demonstrations. The sub-demonstrations would contain their own sub-sub-demonstrations — **and the length would spin out of control**.

And the spinning out of control is itself a proof of the ten profound gates. **Three-dimensional paper cannot write a ten-dimensional object.** This chapter sets the pen down here; the rest of the unfolding is for you to carry out, on your own, in your daily practice.

The place where the pen is set down is not an omission. It is an act of reverence.

### Three Places Where the Line Must Be Drawn Clearly

The first place is that **correspondence is not an equals sign**. To read "ten gates corresponding to ten vows" as the equation "vow = gate" is a mechanizing misreading. Correspondence is not merger — **the vow remains an action** (concrete movements the bodhisattva makes at the level of body and consciousness), and **the gate remains a doctrine** (the angle from which the Huayan patriarchs observe the structure of the dharma-realm). The position of the correspondence is this: the inner structure of each vow on the cultivation side is precisely the dharma-realm structure that some gate describes on the discursive side. The action does not become the angle of observation, and the angle of observation does not become the action — they work at different levels, but they point to the same object.

The second place is that **the windows are lenses, not buttons** — this is the greatest danger of the meta-level chapter: to finish this chapter or the previous ten and think, "I understand now; my daily practice has already been upgraded to the dharma-realm level."

It has not.

The profound gates are lenses, not buttons. Understanding the doctrine of a gate does not automatically upgrade the corresponding vow — understanding only lets you see a structure you could not see before. **The upgrade happens in the body's repeated practice, not in the mind's conceptual understanding.**

After finishing the previous ten chapters and this one, what you have seen is the **shape** of the ten windows, not their substance. The shape belongs to the textual layer; the substance belongs to the cultivation layer. Seeing the shape is not the same as entering the substance. This book brings you to the door; what is inside the door must be walked into, by you, in daily practice.

The third place is that **this chapter is not a practice manual**. This chapter looks most like a practice manual of all — it has two pivot demonstrations that make a reader feel "this is something I can follow step by step." But this chapter is still **descriptive** prose, not **prescriptive** instruction.

It describes: "When the inner grammar of prostration is X, this prostration realizes, on the cultivation side, the perfect-teaching structure that such-and-such a gate describes." It does not prescribe how you ought to think. You may choose to adopt this observation, or choose not to.

The value of this book does not depend on whether you adopt it — its value lies in the fact that it **describes a structure**. That structure is already there. Whether or not you adopt it, it is there.

### A Cultivation Pointer: Ten Questions, Once a Day

Starting tomorrow, in your daily practice, after completing each action, stop for one second and ask yourself one question — **what is the inner structure of this action?**

- **After prostrating**: was this prostration to one Buddha, or to the ten directions?
- **After praising**: were these few words narrow, or broad?
- **After offering**: was this stick of incense isolated, or mutually identical?
- **After repenting**: where is the karma? where is the emptiness of the karma?
- **After rejoicing**: was he the host, or was I the host?
- **After requesting the Dharma**: was this an individual's plea, or the dharma-realm's resonance?
- **After entreating them to remain**: was I requesting physical time, or the limit of the future?
- **After studying the Buddhas**: did I study one, or study all?
- **After according with a being**: what Dharma is this sentient being revealing?
- **After dedicating**: is dedication a transmission, or a folding?

Ten questions, once a day.

You do not need an answer to every question — **the question itself is the cultivation**. Once you have asked, the inner structure of the action will slowly shift from linear to interfusing.

This shift will not be completed in a day. It may take many years. But once a day, and after thirty years, looking back, you will see an utterly different practitioner.

**This is the concrete daily operation of the ten windows.** What this chapter can do is only draw the shape of the ten windows clearly; the work of walking into each window must be done by you.

### Drawing to a Close

The chapter ends here.

This chapter is itself a single demonstration of the ten profound gates. It is not "a discourse on the ten profound gates"; it is **a discourse on the ten profound gates carried out in the manner of the ten profound gates**. The first section, Chengguan's "one-totality and nine-differentiations," is simultaneous mutual correspondence; the second section, "one gate contains ten vows," is unhindered freedom of broad and narrow; the third section, "one vow contains ten gates," is mutual identity of all dharmas; "ten is the index of the inexhaustible" is subtle containment; the three corrections of the line are the mutual reflection of Indra's net; the ten daily questions are dependence on phenomena to reveal the Dharma. Having finished this chapter, you do not merely know what the ten profound gates are — you have undergone one complete passage through the structure of the ten profound gates.

But the place where the pen is set down must be marked clearly: a guide map is even more apt than a window to make one mistake oneself for having already seen the moon. This is the greatest danger of the meta-level chapter — to see the shape and think one has already walked through.

The original awareness of suchness is not within this chapter, nor within these ten windows. It is the object these ten windows together point toward but cannot contain. The failure of each window is an act of reverence; the unreachable center that the ten windows together trace — that is what it is.

Having finished this chapter, please set it down. Continue your daily practice — **this is the true window**.

> Ten gates are ten vows,
> Ten vows are ten gates.
> Where vow and gate become one,
> The perfect teaching is in body and mind.

The next chapter is the final chapter of the whole eight-part work. Stepping out of Part VIII, we return to the eight words this book began with — **trust, understand, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation**. The Huayan "manifestation" is the simultaneous manifestation of these eight words. The ten profound gates are Huayan's ten observational angles upon the dharma-realm; the eight-part work is Pointing-at-the-Moon's eight unfolded facets of the path of cultivation. The two are isomorphic — both are inexhaustible indices of the round number. The twelfth chapter will point out this isomorphism, and gather the whole book together, to see the single unreachable center they both point toward.

> Every chapter in this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an omission; it is reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot reach a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where the pen is set down — so the reader knows that, beyond this line, the rest depends on her own dharma-eye.

The pen of this chapter stops here.
