## 8.8 The Six Characteristics in Perfect Fusion—"Constant" Means Simultaneous, Not a Length of Time

At the close of the previous chapter, the seventh vow came to rest on one word: *acknowledgment*. Within your own mind, thought after thought, a buddha is always attaining full awakening. You do not need to petition some external buddha; you only need to acknowledge that the structure is already this way. The next natural movement is to **follow**.

The four words "constantly following the Buddhas in study" are most easily read as a matter of time—"following forever": I keep learning in this life, I keep learning in the next, I follow in the Buddhas' footsteps life after life. There is nothing wrong with this reading. But to stop at this layer is to miss the true weight this vow carries within the perfect teaching of Huayan.

If a buddha is present in thought after thought (and the seventh vow has already acknowledged this), then what you follow is not the footsteps of some particular buddha in history; it is the imprinting function itself, present in every thought. This "following" is not, in temporal terms, a linear continuation. In structural terms, it is the full, simultaneous presence of the present moment.

The work of this chapter is to open up the structure of this "simultaneous present moment." The tool that opens it is called **the six characteristics in perfect fusion**. Six facets of observation—totality, distinction, sameness, difference, formation, decay—fall simultaneously upon one of the simplest possible actions (say, bowing in reverence before an image of Śākyamuni), so that you can see clearly: at the very moment of learning from this one buddha, you are already learning from all buddhas; and yet Śākyamuni remains Śākyamuni, never melted down into an undifferentiated mass.

The corresponding profound gate is the *Mutual Containment Without Confusion*—the one and the many mutually contain one another, yet the one does not become the many, and the many do not become the one.

### The Six Characteristics Come from the *Daśabhūmika Sūtra*

The six characteristics are not a Huayan invention.

In the sūtra text of the "great vow" passage of the first ground in the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s Chapter on the Ten Grounds, these six terms are already listed out: **totality, distinction, sameness, difference, formation, decay**. Vasubandhu—the Indian patriarch of the Mahāyāna Yogācāra school—gave these six terms their first systematic commentarial treatment in fascicle one of his *Daśabhūmika-vyākhyāna*, and there fixed for them an utterly crucial principle:

> The verbal explanation here, one should know, *sets aside the particulars*; "the particulars" means the aggregates, the elements, the bases, and the like.

"Setting aside the particulars"—what the six characteristics discuss is not the *phenomenal-fact* level of objects such as the five aggregates, the eighteen elements, and the twelve bases. **It is the level of meaning.** From the very start, the six characteristics are a tool of the *modal-aspect* (how to view a thing), not a category of the *self-nature* (what a thing is in itself).

This distinction between "modal-aspect" and "self-nature" will, in a moment, become the single most important bridge for a Yogācāra reader of this book's Part Three entering Huayan. Hold onto this first: the six characteristics are not another set of eight consciousnesses, not another set of three natures, not another set of the hundred dharmas. The path it walks is fundamentally not the path of "self-nature."

From Vasubandhu's exegetical tool, through Jingying Huiyuan of the *Dilun* school (the early Chinese Yogācāra tradition of the sixth century), through the application of Zhiyan the second Huayan patriarch, the perfect-teaching doctrinalization by Fazang the third patriarch, and on to the systematization by Chengguan the fourth patriarch—the six characteristics are **a mature tool cultivated jointly across four centuries**, not the isolated creation of any single patriarch.

### What the Six Characteristics Are: The House and the Golden Lion

In fascicle four of his *Treatise on the Five Teachings of Huayan*, Fazang took an ordinary timber-frame house as his demonstration and unfolded the six characteristics one by one. One thing must be nailed down first: **the six characteristics are not the dismantling of the house into six parts; they are the simultaneous presentation of one and the same house along six observational facets**.

Fazang's first sentence is deeply counter-intuitive:

> The rafter is itself the house. Why? Because the rafter, fully and on its own, can make the house. Apart from the rafter, the house is not formed; when you obtain the rafter, you have already obtained the house.

A single rafter is itself a whole house. The assembling of rafters and tiles is not addition; it is mutual identity—apart from this rafter there is no this house; obtain this rafter and you obtain this house.

Unfolded characteristic by characteristic:

**Totality**—the house (the integral whole).
**Distinction**—the rafters and the other conditions (the individual constituents). Rafter, tile, beam, and column are each different. But "distinction" is not separation—distinction depends upon the root and fulfills it.
**Sameness**—the rafters and the other conditions, harmoniously together, make the house; not opposing one another, they are all called conditions of the house (they are homogeneous in this: that they are all "conditions of the house").
**Difference**—the rafters and the other conditions, considered with respect to their respective forms and types, are distinct from one another (a rafter is a rafter, a tile is a tile, each with its own form and type).
**Formation**—by means of these conditions, the meaning of the house is established (the conditions assemble into a house).
**Decay**—the rafters and the other conditions each abide in their own dharma and do not, on their own, make (each condition keeps its own position and does not go off to do something else).

The most counter-intuitive point is **the simultaneity of formation and decay**. Fazang's argument:

> It is precisely because they do *not* make—that the dharma of the house comes to be established. If they were to make—to go off and not abide in their own dharma—then the meaning of the house would not be established.

**It is precisely because the rafter does not go off to make the house (decay) that the house can be established (formation).** If the rafter went off to make the house, it would no longer be a rafter, and the house would no longer be able to stand—because the house is established precisely by "rafters that remain rafters" together with "tiles that remain tiles" together with the other conditions that each "remain themselves."

When Fazang taught Empress Wu Zetian, he took the golden lion in the palace as another demonstration and composed the *Treatise on the Golden Lion*. The golden-lion image is more directly intuitive:

- **Totality**—the lion (the whole)
- **Distinction**—the differentiation of the five organs (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, each different)
- **Sameness**—sharing one dependent origination (all made of the one gold)
- **Difference**—eyes and ears not reaching one another (each unmingled)
- **Formation**—the organs assembling together (the six organs assembling into the lion's form)
- **Decay**—each organ abiding in its own position (each keeping its original place)

The golden lion and the house convey the same structure, presented simultaneously upon different objects of experience. At the end of the golden-lion image Fazang added one crucial sentence:

> This displays that within the dharma-realm there is no isolated dharma. Whichever single characteristic one raises, the dependent origination of all six characteristics is contained within it.

**Within the dharma-realm there is no isolated dharma.** Any single thing, the moment you raise it up, brings the other six characteristics with it. The six characteristics are not six concepts; they are the very fact that "one and the same thing has six observational facets simultaneously."

### Eternalism and Nihilism: The Six Characteristics Are Not Hegelian Dialectic

Into the house parable Fazang inserts a crucial argument that blocks misreadings in two directions.

**The nihilism extreme**: if you say "each rafter contributes only a little effort, and the house is assembled out of the small efforts of all the rafters"—this disassembles the house into the sum of the small efforts of individual rafters, and what you end up with is not a complete house but many small portions.

**The eternalism extreme**: if you say "no matter what the rafters do, a complete house exists in any case"—this treats the house as a permanently-abiding substance independent of the rafters.

Fazang's conclusion:

> If a dependently-originated dharma is not formed, that is the end of it; once formed, it is mutually identical.

A dependently-originated dharma either fails to come into being at all; or, once it comes into being, the whole and the parts are necessarily mutually identical—neither assembled nor independent.

This is why the six characteristics must be **simultaneous** and cannot be read as a linear progression.

⚠️ Here a common misreading must be blocked. A reader who has studied Western philosophy will very likely think first of Hegel's dialectic—the sublating progression of thesis, antithesis, synthesis. The six characteristics seem to have three pairs of oppositions (totality/distinction, sameness/difference, formation/decay), and it is all too easy to read them as three Hegelian dialectics.

**But the six characteristics are not Hegel.**

Hegelian dialectic is *temporal*—the thesis appears first, the antithesis appears later, the synthesis at the end sublates the two predecessors. The six-characteristics dialectic is *synchronic*—totality, distinction, sameness, difference, formation, decay are all present simultaneously, with no before and after.

Hegel's central operation is *sublation* (*Aufhebung*)—the synthesis sublates thesis and antithesis, lifting their opposition to a higher level. The six characteristics have no sublation—distinction does not sublate totality, difference does not sublate sameness, decay does not sublate formation. Each characteristic remains itself "in its own position"—this is exactly what "decay" means, "abiding in its own dharma and not making."

**The six characteristics are not an ascending spiral; they are a lateral simultaneity.** To read the six characteristics as a Hegelian dialectical progression is to miss their core meaning entirely.

At the end of the six-characteristics section in the *Treatise on the Five Teachings*, Fazang gives an eight-line summary verse, whose final two lines are a structural Middle-Way correction:

> This is the realm of wisdom and not of eventful consciousness; by this skillful means, one encounters the One Vehicle.

The six characteristics are the realm of *wisdom* (the direct illumination of *prajñā*), not the realm of *eventful consciousness* (the conceptual discrimination of the sixth, mind-consciousness). The six characteristics are not an object for the consciousness to conceptualize; they are a realm for wisdom to behold directly.

### The Six Meanings and the Six Characteristics—A Bridge for the Reader of This Book's Part Three

In fascicle four of the *Treatise on the Five Teachings*, Fazang also gives a doctrinal distinction with decisive significance for the Yogācāra reader of this book's Part Three. The question and answer run like this:

> Question: How do the six characteristics and the six meanings divide their ranges?
> Answer: **The six meanings rest on the self-nature of dependent origination; the six characteristics rest on the modal-aspect of dependent origination.** As the dharma-essence enters the modal-aspect, differentiation thereby comes about.

The six meanings (empty, existent, having power, lacking power, dependent on conditions, independent of conditions) discuss the *self-nature* of the dependently-originated dharma—what the thing itself is within the structure of dependent origination. The six characteristics (totality, distinction, sameness, difference, formation, decay) discuss *how to view* the dependently-originated dharma—how the thing presents itself simultaneously along six observational facets.

One and the same dharma-essence, on entering the modal-aspect, comes to have the differentiation of six observational facets. The six meanings and the six characteristics are not two separate systems; they are **one and the same dependently-originated dharma unfolded along two paths**: the six meanings walk the "self-nature" path, the six characteristics walk the "modal-aspect" path.

The significance of this for the reader of Part Three is direct. The Yogācāra you learned in Part Three—the *ālaya-vijñāna*, the production of actualities and seeds from one another, the three natures, the five positions and hundred dharmas—walks the "self-nature" path. The six characteristics walk the "modal-aspect" path. The two paths discuss the same dependently-originated dharma, but they unfold in different directions.

The six characteristics are not another ontological model, not another set of eight consciousnesses, not another set of three natures. **The path it walks is fundamentally not the path of "self-nature."**

Historically, the formation of six-characteristics hermeneutics spanned two traditions, Yogācāra and Huayan—Vasubandhu (Yogācāra), Jingying Huiyuan (the *Dilun* school, the early Chinese Yogācāra tradition), Zhiyan (second Huayan patriarch), Fazang (third patriarch), Chengguan (fourth patriarch). Jingying Huiyuan's "four-gate exegesis" was inherited by Chengguan and expanded into a "seven-gate" structure. This is not a standoff between two schools; it is **the fact of one and the same tool being cultivated jointly by two traditions**.

You do not need to abandon Yogācāra when you enter Huayan. The two readings can be held simultaneously—use the self-nature discourse of Yogācāra to analyze the production of actualities and seeds in an individual mind-consciousness; use the modal-aspect discourse of Huayan to behold the simultaneous presentation of the six characteristics in an integral thing. **Complementary, not merged; tension preserved, not forced into unity.**

### Practice: The Simultaneous Six Characteristics of Reverence Before an Image of Śākyamuni

Now enter the body.

Choose one of the simplest possible actions: **before a buddha-hall, joining your palms and bowing your head in reverence to an image of Śākyamuni Buddha**. The physical duration of this action may be only a few seconds—but under the structure of the simultaneous presentation of the six characteristics, the following six characteristics operate at once.

**Totality-as-sameness (the same buddha-dharma).** What you revere is not merely "this one image of Śākyamuni" but the totality that is "the buddha-dharma itself." The image of Śākyamuni is the concrete present-form of the buddha-dharma, but the buddha-dharma is not only the image of Śākyamuni—it is the one same dharma jointly displayed by all the buddhas of the ten directions and three times. At the instant your forehead touches the ground, this "same buddha-dharma" totality is already present—not that you "think of" it, but that it was already there in the object of your reverence.

**Distinction-as-difference (different teaching skillful means).** Yet the image of Śākyamuni you revere is still the individual form of Śākyamuni—His thirty-two marks, His eighty subsidiary marks, His eight phases of the buddha-life, His forty-nine years of teaching: these are all His distinction as "Śākyamuni, this particular buddha." What you revere is not a composite fused from all buddhas; it is Śākyamuni, this concrete historical person, and His concrete teaching. **Distinction-as-difference does not dissolve totality-as-sameness, and totality-as-sameness does not erase distinction-as-difference**—both are established at once.

**Sameness-as-formation (joint disclosure of reality).** Śākyamuni and the buddhas of the ten directions are homogeneous in "jointly disclosing the same reality." At the very instant you revere Śākyamuni, this homogeneity of "jointly disclosing reality" is already present—not that you afterward infer that the buddhas of the ten directions are also disclosing the same reality, but that the concrete form of Śākyamuni and the universal form of the buddhas of the ten directions are, from the start, one and the same matter in this: the disclosing of reality.

**Difference-as-formation (each responding to capacities).** Yet the buddhas of the ten directions each respond to the differing capacities of beings with differing teaching skillful means. Śākyamuni's teaching for the beings of *Sahā* (the four noble truths of the *Hīnayāna*, the *prajñā* of the *Mahāyāna*, the three turnings of the dharma-wheel) differs from the Medicine Master Buddha's teaching for the beings of the Eastern Lapis Lazuli World, and from Amitābha's teaching for the beings of *Sukhāvatī*. Each buddha's difference—the particular teaching for a particular capacity—is established. **Difference-as-formation does not contradict sameness-as-formation; rather, it is the very concrete present-form of sameness-as-formation.**

**Formation-establishes (the teaching takes place).** This very action of revering Śākyamuni is established as a "dharma-learning event." This event is not vacuous—it has a concrete time, place, posture, and intention; it is a real action that genuinely happens. At the instant of this action, the matter of "revering the buddha" really does assemble together—the body's prostration, the gesture of joined palms, the contemplating thought, the form of the buddha-image: all these conditions assemble simultaneously, so that the event of "reverence" comes to be established.

**Decay-abides (the teaching dispels grasping).** Yet at the same time, this action of reverence itself dispels grasping at reverence. At the instant of your reverence, there is no congealed thought of "I am revering an image"—the body remains the body, the buddha-image remains the buddha-image, the action of reverence remains the action of reverence, each characteristic keeping its own characteristic and not turning into another. **It is precisely because each characteristic keeps its own characteristic (decay) that reverence can be genuinely established (formation)**—this is the concrete unfolding, on the plane of practice and realization, of Fazang's "it is precisely because they do not make that the dharma of the house comes to be established" in the house parable.

### The True Meaning of the Word "Constant"

When the above six characteristics operate simultaneously at the instant of this action of reverence, the vow of "constantly following the Buddhas in study" is already fully realized.

Not as the temporal commitment "from now on I will often follow Śākyamuni in study," but as—

at this instant of reverence, learning from Śākyamuni *is* learning from the buddhas of the ten directions (totality-as-sameness + sameness-as-formation), yet Śākyamuni remains Śākyamuni (distinction-as-difference + difference-as-formation); this dharma-learning event is genuinely established (formation-establishes) and simultaneously dispels grasping at the dharma-learning (decay-abides).

**The true meaning of the word "constant" is not "forever" (a length of time); it is "simultaneous" (the six-characteristics structure).** At every instant of revering Śākyamuni, these six characteristics operate simultaneously—and so every instant is already constantly following the Buddhas in study.

Constantly following the Buddhas in study is not a future goal you must work toward; it is the present-moment event already underway each time you revere a buddha.

The corresponding profound gate, the *Mutual Containment Without Confusion*, and the six-characteristics version of this chapter are two facets of the same doctrine. Mutual containment—learning from one buddha is learning from all buddhas; yet the one does not become the many, and the many do not become the one—Śākyamuni remains Śākyamuni, and His concrete teaching skillful means remain His alone. The profound-gate angle is a structural statement; the six-characteristics angle is an observational unfolding. The two converge upon the same fact: **the instant of learning from one buddha is not a personal psychological event; it is the simultaneous presentation of the dependent origination of the dharma-realm.**

### The Perfect Fusion of the Six Characteristics and "At the Moment of First Aspiration, Full Enlightenment Is Already Accomplished"

At the end of the six-characteristics section in fascicle four of the *Treatise on the Five Teachings*, Fazang concludes by citing the famous line from the Chapter on Pure Conduct of the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, and attributes the standing of this line, structurally, to the perfect fusion of the six characteristics:

> The universal and the particular are jointly fully-present, beginning and end uniformly even. **At the moment of first arousing the aspirational mind, full enlightenment is already accomplished.** This is precisely because the six characteristics of dharma-realm dependent origination are perfectly fused, causes and effects simultaneously inter-pervading and self-mastered in both forward and reverse direction.

The meaning is: "at the moment of first aspiration, full enlightenment is already accomplished" is possible *precisely because* (this is the force of "precisely because") the perfect fusion of the six characteristics of dharma-realm dependent origination guarantees that first aspiration and full enlightenment already possess the same structure within a single thought-moment.

The two events of arousing the initial aspiration and full enlightenment are, in their six-characteristics structure, entirely isomorphic—totality-as-sameness (the integral whole of *bodhicitta* / buddha-awakening), distinction-as-difference (the concrete content of this one thought), sameness-as-formation (homogeneous with all bodhisattvas / all buddhas), difference-as-formation (the individual form), formation-establishes (genuinely present), decay-abides (remaining as aspiration / awakened illumination). The difference is not a structural difference (the six-characteristics structures of the two are the same); it is a difference of position (one is at the position of aspiration, one at the position of buddhahood).

⚠️ This line is all too easily cheapened—read as a personal psychological declaration: "I aroused the initial aspiration, so I am already a buddha." Especially in the context of modern instant-results spirituality, this misreading happens almost automatically.

The key to the antidote lies in Fazang's own original phrase: "**This is precisely because the six characteristics of dharma-realm dependent origination are perfectly fused.**" The standing of this line has a structural condition—it stands only under the dharma-realm structure of the perfect fusion of the six characteristics. Apart from this condition, it is mere rhetoric.

"At the moment of first aspiration, full enlightenment is already accomplished" does not say "some person aroused the initial aspiration and so became a buddha." It says "under the structure of the perfect fusion of the six characteristics of dharma-realm dependent origination, the event of arousing the initial aspiration itself possesses the entire structure of the event of full enlightenment." The person who arouses the aspiration becomes a buddha not because "he aroused the aspiration," but because "the very action of his arousing the aspiration participates in the perfect fusion of the six characteristics of dharma-realm dependent origination" and so shares the same structure as the event of full enlightenment.

The point is not "him"; it is **the structure of the event**.

This also returns to the distinction drawn in Chapter Five—"buddha-in-practice, not buddha-in-position." "At the moment of first aspiration, full enlightenment is already accomplished" speaks of the buddha-in-practice, not the buddha-in-position. The very action of arousing the aspiration is already the action of a buddha (buddha-in-practice), but this does not mean the person has already arrived at the position of buddhahood (buddha-in-position). Position and practice-virtue are two different dimensions—you stand at the position of an ordinary being, your practice is already that of a buddha, and the two do not conflict.

### Drawing In

This chapter ends here.

Dharma-realm dependent origination is, from the start, the structure of the perfect fusion of the six characteristics. You do not need to achieve the simultaneity of the six characteristics—the six characteristics are simultaneous from the start. All you need to do is, at the instant of revering a buddha, reciting the buddha's name, chanting a sūtra, or sitting in meditation, open those six windows and let the six characteristics present themselves simultaneously.

The "constant" of constantly following the Buddhas in study is simultaneity, not a length of time. Every act of reverence is one simultaneous opening of the six characteristics; every simultaneous opening of the six characteristics is one present-moment realization of constantly following the Buddhas in study.

The original awakening of suchness naturally discloses itself within this simultaneous fusion of the six characteristics. It is never covered by any single observational facet—It is beheld directly in the **integral present moment** in which the six characteristics open simultaneously.

The next chapter will push this integral present moment toward another action—**constantly according with sentient beings**. Constantly following the Buddhas in study (the eighth vow) looks at the object you follow; constantly according with sentient beings (the ninth vow) looks at the object you take up. If what you follow is the imprinting function present in thought after thought, then who is the object you take up? The answer, in a single phrase first: **all sentient beings**. But this taking-up is not looking toward beings from a distance; it is walking in among them. Chapter Nine takes the *Entering the Dharma-Realm* chapter of the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* as its window, opening from the prologue to Sudhana's southward journey of fifty-three visits—a young man leaving the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī and walking toward every being of the dharma-realm, disclosing the dharma within each concrete person and event.

> Every chapter of this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an omission; it is an act of reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write to a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where it is set down—so that the reader knows that, beyond this line, one must rely on one's own dharma-eye.

The pen of this chapter stops here.
