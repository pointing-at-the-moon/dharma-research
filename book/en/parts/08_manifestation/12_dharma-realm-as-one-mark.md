## 8.12 Trust, Understanding, Practice, Inquiry, Cutting, Illumination, Gathering-In, Manifestation—The Eight Movements as the One Mark of the Dharma-Realm

The previous chapter finished drawing the ten windows. Arriving here, Part Eight is in fact already complete.

What this chapter does is not to open a ninth chapter—there is no ninth chapter at all. This chapter steps out of Part Eight and returns to the eight words the whole book opened with: **trust, understanding, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation**.

The eight movements are not a linear stacking of eight independent series, not a staircase of "first trust, then understand, then practice…" **The eight movements are the dharma-realm unfolding simultaneously across eight facets.** That sentence sounds like a slogan, but in the very structure of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's chapter "The Manifestation of the Tathāgata," the answer was written long ago. The work of this chapter is not to establish a new thesis; it is only to take this thesis out of the sutra, set it before you, and then lay down the pen.

This is the last chapter of the book's academic-treatise layer. The discussion stops here. What remains is not in the words; it is in your own dharma-eye.

### One Thing That Happened in a Translation Hall

Before walking the diagonals of the eight movements, look first at something the translation history of "The Manifestation of the Tathāgata" has already demonstrated on its own.

The eighty-fascicle Avataṃsaka was translated by Śikṣānanda, and there this chapter is titled "**The Manifestation of the Tathāgata**"; the sixty-fascicle Avataṃsaka was translated by Buddhabhadra, and there the same chapter is titled "**The Nature-Arising of the Jewel-King Tathāgata**." The ordinary way to handle this would be to say: different translations, different rendered titles, the reader need only take note.

But this difference is itself a miniature proof of "the dharma-realm as one mark."

The same chapter of the same sutra yields, under two translators' brushes, two entirely different titles—"**nature-arising**" and "**the manifestation of the Tathāgata**." If each of these two titles expressed an independent doctrine, it would mean the original teaching had been cut in half. But if the two titles point to the same object, each translator merely identifying one of its faces from a different angle—then it means **the two facets of a single doctrine were present simultaneously from the start**, and the two translators, each entering the sutra from his own angle, each pointed out one of them.

Buddhabhadra pointed out the "nature" (the arising of the tathāgatagarbha's own nature); Śikṣānanda pointed out the "mark" (the Tathāgata's manifestation within the dharma-realm). Neither was wrong, neither omitted anything; only the two together constitute one complete chapter title. This is exactly the ancient demonstration of "one is all, all is one": the same chapter of sutra automatically generates two names at the level of the translation language, proving that the object itself carries a structure of "one meaning, two faces."

This book's Part Eight is named "**manifestation**" (顯), and that was no arbitrary choice of character. Within the Chinese language, 顯 happens to cover at once both "making the nature manifest" (the hidden-becoming-revealed sense) and "appearing as a phenomenon" (the manifestation sense)—the Chinese character itself has already fused the two translators' perspectives into a single glyph. The literary history of the Avataṃsaka long ago contained one "eight-movements-style" demonstration of mutual inclusion: one chapter of sutra has lived in Han-Chinese Buddhism under two translation-names for thirteen hundred years, each glossing the other, neither able to replace the other. This is what the dharma-realm as one mark looks like in the life of the literature.

The eight movements of the eight words walk the same structure. The four diagonal pairs below merely do this same thing once more, on a larger scale.

### Why Walk the Diagonals, Not the Straight Line

If we were to review "trust, understanding, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation" one by one, speaking of each in turn, we would in form be denying the thesis itself—because to cut "the dharma-realm as one mark" back into eight independent items is to say that these eight words are independent of one another.

So this chapter takes the **diagonals** instead. Not a review, but a demonstration.

From among the eight words, choose the two poles that look the most distant, pair them into four groups, and point out how these surface opposites are in fact the most mutually implicative within the structure of the dharma-realm. Chengguan, in his *Sub-Commentary Elaborating the Meaning of the Commentary on the Huayan Sūtra*, in his classification of the "one-totality, nine-differentiations" relation of the Ten Profound Gates, said it long ago—the mutual identity between the two most distant gates is the strictest test of "one is all." The compatibility of two adjacent poles easily misleads one into reading them as a temporal sequence; only the compatibility of the diagonal can force the matter of "simultaneity" into the open.

The four diagonal pairs are as follows:

- **Trust ↔ Manifestation** (S1 ↔ S8): the same disclosure at the temporal cause-position and at the fruit-position
- **Understanding ↔ Cutting** (S2 ↔ S5): the same occurrence at the theoretical layer and at the practical layer
- **Practice ↔ Gathering-In** (S3 ↔ S7): the same action at the analytical layer and at the integrative layer
- **Inquiry ↔ Illumination** (S4 ↔ S6): inquiry in its incomplete and its consummated form

When these four pairs have been walked, what you will have experienced is not "a summary of eight series" but a single complete passage, in reading-order, through the thesis of "the dharma-realm as one mark."

### Trust ↔ Manifestation: The First Thought and the Manifestation of the Tathāgata

The first movement of the eight is "trust" (the initial arising of bodhicitta), the eighth is "manifestation" (the manifestation of the Tathāgata). In time, these two points are the most distant—one is the first stirring of the cause of the cause-position, the other the perfect accomplishment of the fruit of the fruit-position; one is the day the reader has just stepped through the Buddhist gate, the other this present moment, after the reader has walked through the preceding seven movements.

But the *Worthy Leader Chapter* says:

> Trust is the primordial source of the Way, the mother of virtues; it grows all good dharmas, cuts off the net of doubt, brings one out of the stream of craving, and opens the supreme path of nirvāṇa.
> …
> The forms of beings are each different, their actions and voices likewise measureless; all of these can be made to appear — through the awesome power of the ocean-seal samādhi.
> …
> All of these are sovereign, through the Buddha's Huayan-samādhi power.

Fazang, in his classification of this passage in the *Treatise on the Five Teachings of the One Vehicle of Huayan*, advances the thesis of "**becoming a buddha at the completion of trust**"—at the moment the bodhisattva's stage of trust is complete, her mind-practice has already become identical to the buddha-fruit, without needing to wait for the sequential ascent through the Ten Abodes, Ten Practices, Ten Dedications, and Ten Grounds. Fazang's own words are: "Because it is by trust that the accomplishment is had, therefore this is the **practice-buddha, not the position-buddha**." From the standpoint of the structure of action, the moment trust is complete is already buddhahood—not in the sense that one has arrived at the buddha-position on the ladder of bodhisattva stages, but in the sense that, in the inner grammar of the action, the structure of a buddha is already present.

This is the thesis of the whole Huayan perfect teaching most easily misread. It is not "after the cultivation of trust is complete one then becomes a buddha"; it is that **the very structure of completed trust is the structure of a buddha**. The two readings differ only by the tense of a single verb, but the doctrinal difference is the whole difference between Huayan and ordinary path-theory—the former is the non-duality of cause and effect in the perfect teaching, the latter still rests in linear sequence.

Place this thesis into the framework of "inceptive awareness, original awareness, inceptive-and-original non-dual," and the diagonal of trust and manifestation becomes wholly visible. Fazang, commenting in his *Record of Investigations into the Mysteries of the Avataṃsaka* on the great-sūtra-scroll-in-a-dust-mote parable of the Nature-Arising Chapter, dissects the "knowledge-of-all, self-arising knowledge, unobstructed knowledge" of the sutra's three knowledges thus:

> The knowledge-of-all is inceptive-awareness wisdom; the unmastered knowledge is original-awareness wisdom; the unobstructed knowledge is the wisdom in which inceptive and original are non-dual.

**Inceptive awareness** is the first response of sentient beings, on the cause-ground, to original awareness—this is "trust"; **original awareness** is that awareness which has always been in place at the dharma-realm layer—this is "manifestation"; **inceptive-and-original non-dual** is what the Huayan perfect teaching truly wishes to point out—the non-duality of trust and manifestation.

This book's first chapter wrote "a single aspiration"; the last chapter (this one) points out the manifestation of the Tathāgata—the two are separated by more than thirty chapters in the book's actual page count, and by no distance at all at the layer of dharma-nature. Trust and manifestation are not the two ends of a temporal sequence; they are the dharma-realm as one mark disclosing itself simultaneously at the cause-position and the fruit-position.

⚠️ Here one misreading must be refused: it is not "because trust is manifestation, therefore one need not cultivate." The word "completion" in "becoming a buddha at the completion of trust" is the key—trust truly completed is buddhahood, but trust before its completion is still on the road. Fazang uses the word "practice-buddha" rather than "buddha" precisely so as to leave this boundary standing. The diagonal does not extinguish difference; it makes the compatibility within difference manifest.

### Understanding ↔ Cutting: Simultaneous, Not Sequential

The second movement, "understanding" (the *Heart Sūtra*), and the fifth, "cutting" (the *Vajracchedikā*), both belong to the prajñā lineage. The reader's easiest misreading is: first understand emptiness in "understanding," then put it into practice in "cutting."

The *Vajracchedikā* says:

> One should not dwell on anything, yet let the mind arise.

This is not "first not-dwelling, then letting the mind arise," a temporal sequence. The Chinese word 應 ("should") here is not a temporal adverb but a **simultaneity adverb**. Not-dwelling and letting-the-mind-arise are two faces of a single action: the very moment of dwelling the mind is the very moment of arising the mind; the very moment of arising the mind is the very moment in which dwelling is absent. Understanding emptiness and cutting off clinging occur at the same time, not one before the other.

The *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra* and the *Vajracchedikā* share a common structure: the doctrinal "understanding of emptiness" and the practical "cutting of clinging" were never set up as a sequence of before-and-after. The sutra texts point this out repeatedly in formulas like "giving without dwelling on marks," "neither grasping nor rejecting," "letting the mind arise without dwelling"—every one of these is a "simultaneity-adverb" sentence, not a "temporal-adverb" sentence.

Map this structure back onto the eight movements: the "understanding" of S2 is not a stage of comprehension preceding the "cutting" of S5; it is another name for that very blade in S5. The "cutting" of S5 is not a stage of practice following the understanding gained in S2; it is the dynamic face of that very understanding in S2. Not two actions, but two faces of a single action.

This is exactly why this chapter lists "understanding" and "cutting" as the two ends of a diagonal rather than as two adjacent items. Adjacency means "one after another"—which would mislead you back into a temporal sequence; the diagonal forces the point: the distance between them is not a temporal distance but a difference of angle. The same "one should not dwell on anything, yet let the mind arise," viewed from the angle of static doctrine, is "understanding"; viewed from the angle of dynamic cutting-off, is "cutting."

Fazang, in his *Contemplation of the Profound Meaning of Huayan: Exhausting the Deluded and Returning to the Source*, defines the Ocean Seal Samādhi thus:

> The ocean-seal is the original awareness of suchness. When delusions are exhausted and the mind is clear, all phenomena appear together.

"Delusions exhausted" is the cutting; "all phenomena appearing together" is the understanding-disclosed; and the two occur in the same moment of "clarity of mind." Understanding and cutting are one body.

### Practice ↔ Gathering-In: Analysis Carried Through Is Gathering-In Carried Through

The third movement, "practice" (the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas*), and the seventh, "gathering-in" (the *Lotus Sūtra*), are the two movements in the eight that most look like opposites. The hundred dharmas is the finest analysis, cutting the dharma-realm into a hundred grains; the one buddha-vehicle is the grandest gathering-in, drawing all dharmas into one.

The two look opposite in direction—one divides, one unites; one is a microscope, one a wide-angle lens.

But the title of the *Mahāyāna Hundred Dharmas Gate of Clarity Treatise* has already given the answer. Why are the hundred dharmas called a "**gate of clarity**"? Not in order to set them apart, but in order to let the position of each dharma **be clearly pointed out**. If a dharma is truly "made clear" to its place, then this dharma's coordinate within the totality of the dharma-realm is established—which is to say, it is **gathered in** by the totality. Analysis is not for the sake of pulling apart; it is for the sake of letting each analyzed object enter the totality at the position that is properly its own.

Look in reverse at the *Lotus Sūtra*: "The buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, manifest in the world solely for one great cause-and-condition." "In the buddha-lands of the ten directions there is only one vehicle of the Dharma; there is no second, no third, save where the Buddha teaches by skillful means." This "one" can stand not because it excludes multiplicity, but because every single pathway is, by this "one," individually pointed out, individually gathered in. If each being could not be seen individually and clearly (each with her own path, her own faculties, her own affliction-structure), the one buddha-vehicle would hollow out into a slogan; only when every pathway is individually seen does the one buddha-vehicle become a structure that truly gathers all in.

So: **carried to the extreme inward is completed outward; carried to the extreme outward is completed inward**. The analysis of the hundred dharmas and the gathering-in of the one buddha-vehicle are not two actions of opposite direction; they are two faces of a single action—one turns inward and makes a fine pointing-out of each dharma, one turns outward and makes a comprehensive gathering-in of all dharmas.

Fazang, commenting in his *Record of Investigations into the Mysteries* on the "gate of defilement-and-purity" of the Nature-Arising Chapter, has a passage of decisive importance:

> Sentient beings and their afflictions are all nature-arising. Why? Because they are what is to be saved, what is to be cut off, what is to be known.

To be saved is gathering-in, to be cut off is cutting, to be known is analysis. The three belong together to nature-arising—not three actions set side by side, but three facets of the same nature-arising structure. S3 is the complete demonstration of the "known" layer (the hundred dharmas are a hundred in order to be known down to the finest detail); S7 is the complete demonstration of the "saved" layer (the one buddha-vehicle is one in order to save in the widest extent). In Fazang's classification, both are gathered into nature-arising and cannot be separated.

This book places S3 in the third movement and S7 in the seventh, with four movements in between—an interval that looks like four steps in the linear sequence, and like zero steps in the diagonal structure. They are, from the beginning, two names for the same event.

### Inquiry ↔ Illumination: To Inquire Back Is to Hear Back

The fourth movement, "inquiry" (AI × Buddha-Dharma), and the sixth, "illumination" (the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*), look the most asymmetrical—one is the only contemporary original series in the eight, the other one of the most classical mind-nature treatises of Han-Chinese Buddhism. One new, one old; one inquiry, one answer; seemingly impossible to set side by side.

But the very structure of the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* is a demonstration of "inquiry → illumination."

The seven places searched for the mind: the Buddha asks Ānanda "where is the mind?", and Ānanda answers seven times (the mind is inside the body, outside the body, hidden in the faculty, in the opening and closing, arising wherever there is conjunction, in the middle, nowhere at all), and the Buddha refutes each in turn. Every "inquiry" points toward a deeper "illumination," until, after the seventh, Ānanda discovers—the so-called "mind" is in none of the positions he is able to answer. That moment of "nowhere to answer" is exactly the starting point of "illumination."

The central argument of the *Śūraṅgama*, the "twenty-five perfect penetrations," is structurally also twenty-five demonstrations of "inquiry → illumination"—each of twenty-five bodhisattvas starts from one sense-base (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; or form, sound, smell, taste, touch, dharma), interrogates the essence of that base, and at the limit of the interrogation turns back to recognize the self-nature. The most famous line of Avalokiteśvara's ear-base perfect penetration is:

> Turning the hearing to hear the self-nature; the nature becomes the supreme Way.

To turn the hearing is not to hear a fainter sound; it is to let **the structure of hearing itself speak**. When "the inquirer, the inquired, and the act of inquiry" are all placed within the question mark, the structure of inquiry collapses into illumination. Structurally, this line says exactly: **when inquiry returns to inquiry itself, it is illumination**.

This is precisely the boundary that S4 keeps striking against in its handling of AI—AI pushes us to a place where "what is inquiry" and "who is the inquirer" return at the same time to the inquirer herself. Every discussion in S4 stops somewhere, and the place where it stops is "language reaches to here and no further"; this posture of stopping, and the word "look" when, in fascicle 2 of the *Śūraṅgama*, the Buddha says to Ānanda "look now upon this assembly," are the same action in two epochs.

Inquiry and illumination were, in the context of Han-Chinese Buddhism, originally of a single source. The "illumination" of the *Śūraṅgama* is not the "silent illumination" of later Chan; it is a **seeing that carries a question with it**. Inquiry is the unconsummated form of illumination; illumination is the inquiry in which no question remains. The inquiries of S4 all became, in the end, illuminations of the S6 kind; the illuminations of S6 all began as inquiries of the S4 kind.

### The Four Pairs Walked: Eight Words Pointing at One Thing

With the four diagonal pairs walked, one clear fact surfaces: **trust, understanding, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation—these are not eight independent fields, but the dharma-realm disclosing itself simultaneously across four most-distant pairings.**

- Trust = manifestation at the beginning of the temporal cause-position; manifestation = trust at the completion of the temporal fruit-position
- Understanding = cutting in its action at the theoretical layer; cutting = understanding in its occurrence at the practical layer
- Practice = gathering-in completed at the analytical layer; gathering-in = practice redeemed at the integrative layer
- Inquiry = the unconsummated form of illumination; illumination = the inquiry in which no question remains

Fazang, commenting in his *Record of Investigations into the Mysteries* on the Nature-Arising Chapter, has a line of utmost concision:

> The arising has no other arising; it takes the nature itself as its arising.

All dependent-arising phenomena ultimately return to the self-disclosure of the "nature," not to some external arising-cause. The "arising" that all four diagonal pairs of this chapter point out takes, in the end, one and the same "nature" as that from which it arises—**a nature that is not called trust and not called manifestation, not called understanding and not called cutting, not called practice and not called gathering-in, not called inquiry and not called illumination. It is simply the dharma-realm itself, which is also the tathāgatagarbha-nature already fully present within your own body.**

⚠️ Here again one misreading must be refused: mutual inclusion is not sameness. Trust is still trust, manifestation is still manifestation; the two are never to be conflated. But precisely because each strictly holds its own facet, they can be compatible at the dharma-nature layer in the manner of "inceptive and original non-dual." A monism that extinguishes difference destroys the very core structure of the Huayan perfect teaching—the "one" of Huayan is not the extinguishing thesis "one and many non-dual," but the structural thesis "one and many mutually identical." Fazang: "The partial and the complete are without limit; therefore, at each partial place everything is complete." The "partial place" must be truly retained as a "partial place," and at the same time each partial place must be "everything complete." Not "the partial place disappears into the whole," but "the partial place is, precisely in being the partial place, simultaneously the whole."

Such is the mutual inclusion of the eight words. Whichever part, whichever chapter of this book you turn to hereafter, do not read it as "a precursor, an extension, or a lesser version of the other seven"—each part is the whole dharma-realm as one mark, the complete self-disclosure that takes that part's name as its facet.

### From Āgama to Avataṃsaka: A Dual Track That Was Always There

Now this chapter has one thing to do—to set down, formally, the last stroke of this book's academic-treatise layer.

But before that marking, an ancient thread must first be connected. For the fact that "the discussion stops somewhere" is not the invention of this book, of the Huayan school, or of Han-Chinese Buddhism. In the oldest Āgama sutras, the Buddha himself had already drawn this line.

The *Madhyama-āgama*'s *Sūtra on Omniscience* records a dialogue between the Buddha and King Pasenadi of Kosala. Pasenadi, trusting a rumor, had come to believe the Buddha claimed to be "one who knows all and sees all," and sought the Buddha's confirmation. The Buddha's answer came in two steps:

First, the Buddha expressly denied ever having said "I know all and see all";

Second, the Buddha acknowledged having said something else: "There has not been, there will not be, and there is not now, any other recluse or brahmin who **knows all at one time, sees all at one time**"—this is impossible.

Set the two statements side by side, and the Buddha's position is exceedingly clear: **he does not deny being "omniscient" (in principle capable of knowing all); what he denies is "knowing all in a single moment"—all knowledge made manifest at once in any single instant**. The completeness of omniscience is disclosed through time, through dependent arising, through what is asked; it is not a static totality already fully unfolded.

This passage is the ancient-stratum legitimacy for the closing of this book's academic-treatise layer. The thirty-some chapters of the eight movements do not add up to "all of the Dharma," do not constitute a closed, omniscient system. For even the Buddha himself never said "knowing all at one time." The boundaries, failure-points, and unfinished places of Pointing-at-the-Moon are not the writer's incapacity; they are a faithfulness to the Āgama-stratum principle that "omniscient does not equal omniscient in a single moment." The closing of the pen carries no obligation to "finish saying everything"—because "finishing" was never, from the beginning, the Buddha's way of describing his own omniscience.

Another ancient-stratum thread is Brahmā's entreaty. In the early period after his awakening, the Buddha sat in solitude reflecting that "the dharma I have attained is extremely profound, subtle, hard to comprehend and hard to see," and resolved to remain silent and not to preach; the lord Brahmā descended and entreated him, three earnest entreaties, and only then did the Buddha resolve to open the Dharma. This episode is recorded in the *Dīrgha-āgama*'s *Mahāvadāna-sūtra* in the posture of the common track of the seven past buddhas, and in the *Ekottarika-āgama*'s *Chapter on Entreaty* in the setting of Śākyamuni himself. Reading the two versions together, one can jointly establish a single thesis: **the Buddha's preaching was never, from the beginning, "speaking the whole for everyone"; it was "speaking for those who trust and delight in hearing, and not for those who would be disturbed to no benefit."** Preaching is by nature selective, by nature reserved, by nature built with the internal limit "this is not the whole."

From Āgama to Lotus to Avataṃsaka, this dual track of "silence / opening" has always been present. The "profound, silent, thrice-entreated, opening-the-Dharma" of the moment the wheel was first turned was enlarged, in the age of the *Lotus Sūtra*, into "the five thousand withdrew, the Tathāgata remained silent"; in the age of the Avataṃsaka's "The Manifestation of the Tathāgata," it was organized by Samantabhadra into a more structured statement. The "Tathāgata's Turning of the Dharma Wheel" chapter of "The Manifestation of the Tathāgata" has a parallel passage:

> He turns the dharma wheel without any speech, for he knows that all dharmas cannot be spoken; … he turns the dharma wheel through every word and every language, for the Tathāgata's voice reaches everywhere; … the true reality of voice is itself the dharma-wheel.

The key to this passage is not the dialectical rhetoric of "the Buddha both speaks and does not speak." It is the revelation that: **"speech" and "non-speech" are not two options of one dharma-wheel; they are the two-faced structure that the single dharma-wheel inherently carries.** The very nature of the dharma-wheel is "the true reality of voice"—and the reason this is "true reality" is that it simultaneously carries the emptiness face of "entering all voices yet entering nothing."

This is the scriptural ground on which this book's academic-treatise layer closes here: **the closing of the pen is not the Dharma ceasing to flow; on the contrary, it is the official presentation of the other face of this dharma-wheel.**

### Here the Book's Treatise Layer Sets Down the Pen

The thirty-some chapters together, this book has not answered these questions:

- What is the essence of mind? (The true answer to the *Śūraṅgama*'s seven places searched for the mind can only appear inside your own sitting.)
- What is seeing-the-nature? (Eighteen hundred years of Chan recorded sayings have been circling this term; this book has no standing to give a better definition.)
- What actually happens at the moment of awakening? (Every description in the sutras and treatises is a marking, not the scene.)
- What is the single thought at the moment of death? (Pure Land's recollection of the Buddha, the *Śūraṅgama*'s hearing-and-reflecting, the Vajrayāna's *tummo*—the three traditions each have their own path; this book has no standing to adjudicate among them.)
- Does AI have buddha-nature? (S4 walks to its eighth paper, and the place where the pen is set down is right at the edge of this question.)

These five questions are not omissions; they are questions that **the academic-treatise layer should not, in principle, write**. The true answer to each must occur in "a place that is not a treatise"—in a meditation hall, on a deathbed, in the instant of a single thought's correspondence, on the day your own dharma-eye ripens. The entire posture of this book is, across the words of thirty-some chapters, to circle repeatedly around the edges of these five questions, draw a circle around them, and then point it out to you: "What is inside this circle is not in a place a treatise can reach."

This is the literal redemption of the two characters "pointing at the moon." The moon is inside the circle, the finger outside; on the rim of the circle are written more than three hundred thousand characters, and inside the circle is written silence. The completion of this book's academic-treatise layer is not because the rim has been filled with characters; it is because **the circle itself has been drawn closed**.

⚠️ This posture of setting down the pen is not pessimism, not giving up, not "we cannot do it so we are stopping." Quite the opposite—it is this book's most confident utterance: **this book trusts that your dharma-eye can do what the treatise cannot.** To mark the boundary clearly is the highest honor one can pay the reader. A book that tries to finish saying everything is neither honest nor respectful of the reader—because it is pretending to be the moon, rather than the finger that points at the moon.

⚠️ One more misreading must first be refused. The closing of this book's academic-treatise layer is not "the end of the Pointing-at-the-Moon project." Pointing-at-the-Moon still operates on three other layers: the YouTube spoken layer, the online joint-practice and offering platform, and the sub-series of Sudhana's fifty-three encounters. The moment the treatise layer closes its pen is in fact the very moment the other three layers truly begin—just as Samantabhadra, at the end of the *Samantabhadra-caryā-praṇidhāna*, having pronounced the ten great vows, did not "end" the Avataṃsaka but handed its whole dynamic back into the daily practice of each of its readers. **The closing of the pen is a passing of the baton, not a curtain fall.**

Fazang's *Record of Investigations into the Mysteries*, commenting on the Nature-Arising Chapter, has one more line, the last citation of the discursive part of this chapter:

> Since this true nature pervasively fuses everything, what arises from it likewise fully contains everything. **The partial and the complete are without limit; therefore, at each partial place everything is complete**, nothing not containing the entire inexhaustible dharma-realm.

True-nature pervades every place, so whatever dharma arises from any place fully contains everything. Each place is complete; no place is partial. The moment you close this book, you yourself at that instant are "one place," and this one place **already fully contains everything**; no place is in any way insufficient.

The moon is at your own position. It always has been.

### How Extraordinary! These Beings

The great-sūtra-scroll-in-a-dust-mote parable of "The Manifestation of the Tathāgata" contains a line quoted again and again from the first patriarch of Chan down to today. At the close of this book, this line must appear once more—but this time not as a scriptural citation, but as a pointing-out:

> How extraordinary! How extraordinary! How is it that these beings possess the Tathāgata's wisdom, yet in foolishness and confusion do not know it, do not see it?

The "how is it" in this line is not an interrogative; it is a declarative. The Buddha is saying: **you are already like this, you have always been like this—why do you still not know it?**

From Chan's "this very mind is the Buddha" to Huayan's "the mind itself, thought after thought, always contains a buddha accomplishing perfect awakening," the deepest place of Han-Chinese Buddhism's mind-nature doctrine is, all of it, a response to this single cry, "how extraordinary." It is not astonishment, not compassion; it is the Buddha's direct pointing at a structural fact—**within these beings, the Tathāgata's vast wisdom has always been present, not different from the Buddha's**. This "always" is not a past-tense always; it is the always already in place at this very moment, as you turn to this page.

The whole posture of this book's thirty-some chapters aims, in the end, at this "how is it." It does not set up a new thesis for you to learn; it points out a fact that has long been present, only not yet seen by you—**the tathāgatagarbha-nature already fully present within your own body is not different from the Buddha's**. At the moment when, having finished this chapter, you close the book, if these two characters "how is it" appear in your heart even for a single instant, then the more than three hundred thousand characters of this book have not been written in vain.

### Universal Dedication

The tenth vow of the closing fascicle of the *Samantabhadra-caryā-praṇidhāna*, "universal dedication," says:

> From the first vow of Homage down through Accommodation, all the merits produced, I dedicate universally to all sentient beings of the boundless dharma-realm and empty-space realm. … To the limit of empty space, to the exhaustion of the realm of beings, to the exhaustion of the karma of beings, to the exhaustion of the afflictions of beings: my dedication shall be without limit. Thought after thought continuing without interruption; in body, speech, and mind, without weariness.

This book began with the first chapter, "a single aspiration," and finishes here at the last chapter of Part Eight, spanning several years and more than thirty chapters, some three hundred thousand characters. Whatever roots of goodness, whatever merit, arose in the course of this writing—every act of deliberation, consideration, writing, proofreading, organizing, publishing, responding, and revising—are here, all of them, dedicated:

Dedicated to all sentient beings throughout the boundless dharma-realm and empty-space realm: may they all attain peace and ease, all hear the true Dharma, all see within themselves the marks and qualities of the Tathāgata's wisdom that have always been their own.

Dedicated to every reader of this book: may wisdom arise in the moment you stop at each middle-way correction, and may you find your home in the single breath drawn after you set down your inquiry.

Dedicated to the good friends who, over two and a half thousand years, have written Buddhist commentary in every language: may every marking they set down be seen by those who come after, and may every "the pointing reaches to here" they left be walked further by those who come after.

### Thus Does It Manifest

The final four lines of the verse of the great-sūtra-scroll-in-a-dust-mote parable:

> The great compassion of the buddhas causes them to remove their delusion—
> **thus does it manifest**, benefiting the bodhisattvas.

"Manifestation" is not a coming-forth from a hidden place, nor an appearance of being from where being already was. "Manifestation" is the natural self-disclosure, after the breaking of the dust mote (deluded thinking and clinging), of that great sutra-scroll already fully present, equal in extent to the great trichiliocosm.

From this book's first chapter, a single aspiration, to here, the dharma-realm as one mark has already manifested countless times on the fingertips that turn your pages. Every turn of a page, every stopping, every setting of the book back on the table—it has been there. **Your tathāgatagarbha-nature has never once left its position; it has only been covered by thirty-some chapters of words.**

The words are finished now, and the covering has been lifted to its very end.

> Every chapter of this book stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an oversight; it is reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write to a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where it is set down—so that you know that, beyond this line, you must rely on your own dharma-eye.

Pointing-at-the-moon points to here and no further. The moon is at your own position. It always has been.

Thus does it manifest.

Here Part Eight of this book ends.
