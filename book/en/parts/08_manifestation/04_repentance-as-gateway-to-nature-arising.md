## 8.4 From "Concealment" to Nature-Arising — Repentance of Karmic Obstructions as the Gate of Practice into the Huayan perfect teaching

The previous chapter left a question hanging at its end: if the dharma-realm is, by its own nature, everywhere unobstructed, phenomenon to phenomenon — if a single stick of incense, here and now, already holds all things complete — then why do you feel a layer between yourself and that original nature? What is that layer? How do you deal with it?

The answer of this chapter is direct: that layer is called **concealment**. To deal with it, you must walk to the Buddha.

### One Word, "Concealment"

There is something you have not told anyone for twenty years.

Not because it is large. Sometimes it is even small — small enough that you cannot say why you still remember it. But every time it surfaces from some corner of your mind, you push it back down at once, switch to another thought, open your phone, or do something else.

That act of "pushing down" is what Buddhism calls **concealment**.

Part Three, Chapter Seven of this book discussed this mental factor. The *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* lists concealment among the twenty minor derivative afflictions; the *Cheng Weishi Lun*, Fascicle Six, further sets out its structure: "Concealment: with regard to offenses one has committed, fearing the loss of gain and reputation, its nature is to hide; it obstructs non-remorse, and its function is vexing heat… This concealment is a portion of craving and a portion of delusion."

Concealment is not lying — when you lie, you still know you are lying. Concealment covers up even the fact that a cover-up is happening. Beneath it lie two conjoined roots: an attachment to one's own image (a portion of craving), and an avoidance of reality (a portion of delusion). To conceal one thing is necessarily to conceal the existence of these two as well.

What this chapter has to say is simple, but it requires the *Āgamas*, the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*, and the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* together to say it clearly:

**so long as there remains even one concealment in you that has not been opened, the Huayan doctrine of nature-arising is, for you, a beautiful and empty phrase.** Not because it is untrue — it is profoundly true — but because the handle of that door is in your own hand, and you have been holding it shut.

### The Āgamas' Three-Limb Program

The Buddha gave the method for dealing with concealment as early as the Āgama period. The *Madhyama Āgama*, Fascicle Four, in the *Pāṭaliya Sutta*, records a sentence spoken to the Buddha by a householder named Pāṭaliya — a sentence that can almost serve as the canonical formula of Āgama repentance:

> I repent, Gautama! In the past, O Well-Gone One! I was as a fool, as one deluded, as one unsteady, as one unwholesome… May the Blessed One accept my repentance. Seeing the offense and disclosing it, having repented, I shall guard against recurrence.

Three actions in one sequence — **seeing the offense, disclosing it, guarding against recurrence**. This is not moral exhortation; it is an operational program.

**Seeing the offense.** The original does not use emotional vocabulary like "admitting fault" or "feeling remorse"; it uses the verb *to see*. Seeing is an epistemic act — seeing a thing as it is. Seeing the offense as an offense, not as "an understandable constraint," not as "I too was a victim," not as "I was young then." Seeing the offense as offense is the first step of the whole program, and the hardest. Because the way concealment operates is precisely to make you **unable to see it as it is**. Concealment never says to you "you did not do it"; it says "you did it, but actually…" — and everything that follows that "but actually" is a disguise of concealment.

**Disclosing it.** Seeing is still only inside your head; disclosure is what makes it come out. The Vinaya preserves a technical term, *paṭidesanīya* (disclosure), for the act by which a monk confesses an infraction face-to-face to another monk or to the Saṅgha. The point of disclosure is not ritual; it is **giving it voice** — letting the thing leave the heart, leave the body, and enter common space. Why must it be voiced? Because an acknowledgment that remains in the head always keeps a back door open: "I have not really said it yet, so I can still take it back." Voicing closes that door. Seeing without disclosure slowly fades, and within days concealment quietly covers the ground again.

**Guarding against recurrence.** The first two limbs deal with the past; this one deals with the future. *Saṃvara*, often rendered as "restraint," is an active guarding of future conduct. If repentance consists only of seeing and disclosing, without guarding against recurrence, it becomes the most tragic figure in the *Water-Simile Sutta*: "having risen, he sinks again" — the one who has just lifted his head above the water and pushes it back down himself. Repenting and re-offending, re-offending and repenting, becomes a self-consoling loop in which concealment grows ever denser — because each new offense now has "but I already repented last time" as its alibi. Guarding against recurrence is not a vow never to err again (the Āgamas are not that naïve); it is the construction of an active warning system, so that the first stirrings of the next occurrence are recognized for what they are.

How grave an offense can this program address? The Āgamas' answer is startlingly direct: **patricide can.**

The *Dīrgha Āgama*, Fascicle Seventeen, *Śrāmaṇyaphala Sutta*, recounts that King Ajātaśatru killed his own father, King Bimbisāra, to usurp the throne. Patricide is one of the five gravest offenses, the heaviest evil karma the Āgamas acknowledge. Ajātaśatru later went to the Buddha and repented on the spot. The Buddha accepted his repentance, then said to Ānanda this verdict: "This King Ajātaśatru's offenses have diminished; **he has uprooted the grave offense**."

*Uproot* is a horticultural verb — to pull a thing up by the roots. Not to cover, not to offset, not to transfer, but **to tear the karma out from its very growing point**. If even patricide, of that magnitude, can be uprooted, then nothing in your twenty-year burden is beyond uprooting.

But there is also an honest tension in the Buddha's words that must be faced: "**had King Ajātaśatru not killed his father, he would have attained the pure Dharma-eye on this very seat**." Had he not killed his father, he would have seen the path and attained the fruit then and there. In other words, repentance uprooted the grave offense, but it did not make what happened into something that never happened. This is the Āgamas' sober posture on the question: **repentance is really effective, but it is not magic.** What it uproots is the continuing momentum of the karmic stream, not the historical fact of the act.

For you, the reader, the meaning is this: do not expect that after repenting, that thing will become as though it had never occurred. It did occur. You will remember. But the *way* you remember it will change — from a thing still living, still growing, still influencing every decision you make, into a completed past you can look back upon calmly.

### Prostration Before the Buddha: The Body Must Be Present

There is a danger in reading this far. You may draw the following conclusion: "So Āgama repentance is this plain. Then I can sit quietly for three minutes, see the thing once in my heart, disclose it once in my heart, decide once in my heart not to do it again — and that will suffice."

**This conclusion is wrong.** And it is, precisely, the most sophisticated form of concealment.

Why? Because the inner monologue **is concealment's most comfortable hiding place**. In the moment you say to yourself "I admit I did that thing," the one speaking and the one listening are the same person — which is to say, it is still the one who was doing the concealing who chairs this act of confession. He can decide at any moment "all right, the confession is finished"; he can adjust the wording at any moment; he can press pause whenever it gets too uncomfortable.

This is not seeing the offense and disclosing it. This is **a performance with a presiding officer**. The officer is you, the audience is you — and the concealment that needs to be opened is the very identity of the officer himself.

In the Āgamas, Pāṭaliya, Ajātaśatru, Aṅgulimāla — these three penitents share a feature we have, until now, intentionally left un-stressed: **every one of them did it in the presence of the Buddha, face to face.** The Buddha is not literary background to the scene; he is the *field* in which the "seeing" can truly take place. There is an eye that is not your eye, watching — so you cannot quietly swap in a cleaner version. There is an ear that is not your ear, listening — so you cannot press down on your own voice halfway through the disclosure. Guarding against recurrence is no longer a private promise you can revise on your own tomorrow; it is set down before a presence you dare not deceive.

After the Buddha's *parinirvāṇa*, this "toward-whom" structure was carried forward by images of the Buddha and by the liturgies of repentance. This is not cultural ornament; it is the necessary condition for turning an epistemic act back into a **bodily act**. If, having read this chapter, you decide to begin truly repenting of one thing, the following is heartfelt counsel. Please do not treat it lightly.

**Walk to the Buddha.** Not imagine the Buddha in your heart — the imagined Buddha is a product of your own making, still the same presiding officer. Let the body physically go. If there is a shrine at home, go to it; if there is a temple nearby, go to it; even an image of the Buddha on your phone will do, but turn to it in earnest.

**Join your palms.** Not as politeness, but as the body entering a posture in which it can no longer scroll its phone, no longer drift, no longer escape. The gesture itself draws two outward-reaching hands back to the centerline — it is a **bodily arresting of concealment**. Before you have even opened your mouth, concealment has already been blocked in half by the posture itself.

**Read a long repentance text.** The *Repentance of Emperor Liang*, the *Water Repentance of the Compassionate Samādhi*, the *Great Compassion Repentance* — these long liturgies are not for skimming quickly. They are long because the reader needs that length. A short self-acknowledgment is easily dispatched by concealment with a single "all right, that's enough." A long text is like a corridor you must walk all the way through, and at every section it closes another route by which concealment might escape. As you read, at some section a sentence will suddenly strike you — a sentence that points precisely at the thing you have been covering. Let that strike land. Do not move around it.

What follows is heavy.

**The thing you are going to repent of today before the Buddha is a tumor that has been quietly growing inside you.** ⚠️ This is not appropriating a real disease as a metaphor; it is borrowing this medical fact to describe the dynamics of concealment — **a thing that has never been brought into the light does not pause in the dark; it grows.** You believed that something twenty years ago was already over; in truth it has gone on dividing, every day, in the places you could not see. It has shaped the way you choose a partner, the degree to which you are severe with your children, the pattern of your anxiety about money, the voice that rises in you when you wake in the middle of the night. You think these are parts of your personality; in truth some of them are branches that grew from the thing that never saw light.

When you really walk to the Buddha, join your palms, and bring it out as sound — not whispered secretly inside, but letting the voice truly leave your throat — three things will happen.

**First, the Buddha will hear you.** This is not myth. It is a description of the deepest structure of "seeing" in your body-mind being activated. In ordinary states, that structure is blocked by your own concealment; you cannot hear it, you cannot feel it. In the moment of voiced disclosure before the Buddha, the obstruction between you and that structure temporarily vanishes, and so you experience *being heard*. The experience is real, whatever vocabulary you use to describe it.

**Second, the gods and Dharma-protectors rejoice and praise.** To a reader familiar with Buddhist culture this phrasing is entirely natural; to one less familiar, a note is needed. On the web of causes and conditions there are countless threads of wholesome force at work, which ordinarily cannot reach you — because your concealment works like a shield, sealing you off from the entire field of conditions. In the moment you truly open that concealment before the Buddha, the shielding drops, and those forces flow in at once. Traditional Buddhism describes this experience as "the gods and Dharma-protectors rejoice and praise," in anthropomorphic language; but what it points to is a real structural event — **you have reconnected to the field that was running all along.**

**Third, you will feel light.** The body grows light — not as a figure of speech. The thing that had been growing in the dark begins, in the very moment of being seen, to dissolve — just as water rinses away mud. The *Partial Translation of the Saṃyukta Āgama* has a verse: "The waters of Dharma wash the dust of defilement; here, in this place, one should bathe." Those words describe exactly this. Some readers weep in that moment. That is not emotional collapse; it is the body expelling a weight it has carried for a long time. Let it weep. Do not rush to control it.

When all this is done, sit a while. Attend to your own breath. You may find that you have not breathed this way in a long time — a breathing that does not need to pause halfway, that does not need to grow shallow on purpose to avoid feeling something. That is what "lightness" means.

This is not the lyrical aside of the chapter. This is the action the chapter asks you to perform today. What follows is the doctrinal ground beneath the Āgama program — but however clear the ground may be, if you do not actually perform this action, the chapter has failed.

### The Āgamas Give the Method, Huayan Gives the Grounds

The Āgama program of repentance works, but the Āgamas do not fully explain **on what grounds** it can work. Why should so simple an act as seeing and disclosing be capable of uprooting a grave offense? The Āgama answer is pragmatic: "Because it works. Do it and you will see." — This is fully sufficient to sustain the practice; but the reader who reaches this point will ask: on what grounds?

The answer to that "on what grounds" belongs to Huayan.

The eighty-fascicle *Avataṃsaka*'s *Chapter on the Manifestation of the Tathāgata* (T0279, Fascicle 52) gives, in its description of the Tathāgata's attainment of the path, the most astonishing passage in the whole sūtra:

> When the Tathāgata, Worthy, Perfectly Enlightened One, attains Perfect Enlightenment, he sees within his own body all sentient beings attaining Perfect Enlightenment… all the same nature, namely: no nature.

At the moment the Tathāgata attains Perfect Enlightenment, "within his own body he universally sees all sentient beings attaining Perfect Enlightenment." Not "the Buddha is able to liberate sentient beings," but "in the very moment of the Buddha's attainment of Perfect Enlightenment, all sentient beings are already, within the Buddha's body, simultaneously enlightened." And the ground of this "simultaneous enlightenment" is "all the same nature, namely, no nature" — it is not that some shared substance binds Buddha and sentient beings together, but that "no nature" (the absence of self-nature) is the deepest sameness.

The same chapter contains an *originally-present* declaration. The sixty-fascicle version reads:

> Marvelous! Marvelous! How is it that the Tathāgata, possessed of wisdom, is within the body, and yet is not known, not seen? I shall teach these sentient beings to awaken to the noble path, making them forever depart from the bondage of deluded thinking and inverted defilements, so that they may wholly see the wisdom of the Tathāgata within their own bodies, no different from the Buddha.

The eighty-fascicle version reads: "There is not a single sentient being who does not possess the wisdom of the Tathāgata, but because of deluded thinking, inverted views, and grasping, they do not realize it; if they depart from deluded thinking, omniscience, spontaneous wisdom, and unguided wisdom will at once become manifest."

Here appear verbs the Āgamas, all along their way, never once used: **become manifest**, **no different from the Buddha**, **departing from deluded thinking, at once attained**. The Āgama path attains its fruit by passing through "removing, severing, extinguishing, attaining," one stage at a time; the verb here in Huayan is **disclose** — it is already there, merely covered; lift the cover, and there it is.

This difference of verb is not a choice of translation; it is the base of the entire soteriology shifting underneath.

Will Huayan's "disclosing," then, immediately turn into a creationism of "*tathatā* producing the myriad dharmas"? Fazang addresses this directly in his *Questions and Answers on the Avataṃsaka Sūtra*:

> Nature-arising refers to what is itself so, not arising from conditions... As for nature-arising, the very Dharma-nature takes non-arising as its nature, and thus takes non-arising as arising.

The key is that one phrase: **"takes non-arising as arising."** The "arising" of nature-arising is not arising in the sense of birth-and-cessation (that is dependent arising, which has arising, abiding, changing, perishing), but the manifestation of "so by its own nature." Taking "non-arising" as "arising" — Huayan's definition of the word "arising," from the very start, refuses any generative reading.

Fazang, in his *Exploring the Mysteries Record*, uses a more intuitive analogy: "As when gold is made into a ring: the ring is empty, the gold is real; only the gold arises." Gold made into a ring — the ring is an apparent, posited form; the gold is the real substance. The "arising" of the ring is not the production of a ring from elsewhere; it is the manifestation of "gold" in this particular shape. **The form is merely posited, the substance is real; what is called "arising" is only the substance manifesting through the form.**

⚠️ A necessary correction. Nature-arising sounds like "*tathatā* originally possesses all merits and virtues," and is easily misread as treating *tathatā* as a substance. The *Chapter on the Manifestation of the Tathāgata* itself seals off this reading — "all the same nature, **namely: no nature**." The sameness of sentient beings and buddhas is not because they share an entity called "buddha-nature," but because both equally lack self-nature. **No-nature is the deepest sameness, and also the most thorough refusal of substantiation.** Huayan has never treated *tathatā* as a substance. This is the same grammar of emptiness as Part Two's *Heart Sūtra* — "the five aggregates are all empty" — and Part Five's *Diamond Sūtra* — "A → not-A → therefore named A": every affirmation is immediately accompanied by a cancellation by emptiness.

### The Āgamas' "Removing" and Huayan's "Disclosing" Are Two Faces of One Act

Now the question from the beginning can be answered: are the Āgamas' "removing, severing, attaining" and Huayan's "disclosing, manifesting, originally present" two mutually exclusive paths of cultivation?

No. They are **two descriptive layers of one and the same instant.**

The Āgamas speak from the side of *event*: there is a concealment, lift it off. Huayan speaks from the side of *nature*: what appears once it is lifted is not newly made, it was always there.

The Āgamas' "removing" and Huayan's "disclosing" are not before-and-after; they are **simultaneous**. The instant you remove is the instant it discloses. The Āgamas' "uprooting the grave offense" and Huayan's "cultivation arises from nature, and cultivation in turn merges back into nature" are the same action described at two doctrinal layers — what is uprooted is the continuing momentum; what is merged into is the originally-present nature; and these two things happen in the same instant, in the same body, in the same act of voiced disclosure.

This is why this chapter, before its doctrine advanced into Huayan, first set out "walk to the Buddha" clearly. Huayan's "disclosing" is no castle in the air — the place where it discloses is the very place where the Āgamas' concealment is pried open; the moment it discloses is the very moment you voice your disclosure.

### The Śūraṅgama and Huayan Shake Hands in Repentance

Part Six of this book went as far as the tathāgatagarbha. When the fifty skandha-*māras* are exhausted and the five delusive thinkings dissolved, what is disclosed is exactly "the wondrous true nature of the original Tathāgata-store." The *Śūraṅgama* closes its brush on the three tathāgatagarbhas — Empty, Non-Empty, Empty-and-Non-Empty.

But the *Śūraṅgama* sub-commentator Jiaoguang Zhenjian (of the Ming dynasty) long ago pointed out a critical incompleteness. In his *Zhengmai shu on the Śūraṅgama Sūtra* he wrote: "Again, this speaks purely of suchness, showing only the Empty tathāgatagarbha; **it has not yet clarified the arising of its function** — this is why it is classified under the true limit." The *Śūraṅgama* discloses only the substance of suchness; "how it arises," "why it arises at all," "what relation the arisen myriad dharmas bear to the substance" — what the *Śūraṅgama* gives is a skeleton, not a complete doctrine of nature-arising.

Zhenjian then built a bridge, joining the *Śūraṅgama*'s three tathāgatagarbhas directly to Huayan's contemplation of the dharma-realm:

| Śūraṅgama Three Garbhas | Huayan Dharma-Realm |
|:---:|:---:|
| Empty tathāgatagarbha | *li*-dharma-realm (principle) |
| Non-Empty tathāgatagarbha | *shi*-dharma-realm (phenomena, form arising from nature) |
| Empty-and-Non-Empty tathāgatagarbha | principle-phenomena unobstructed → phenomena-phenomena unobstructed |

The Empty tathāgatagarbha discloses the substance of suchness, corresponding to the *li*-dharma-realm; the Non-Empty tathāgatagarbha speaks of suchness following conditions to manifest the myriad dharmas, corresponding to the *shi*-dharma-realm — and this is precisely nature-arising expressed in the *Śūraṅgama*'s voice: "the one mind originally contains in principle, and follows conditions in its functioning." The Empty-and-Non-Empty tathāgatagarbha speaks of "nature wholly form, form wholly nature," corresponding to principle-phenomena unobstructed moving toward phenomena-phenomena unobstructed.

Zhenjian has one sentence that closes all dispute: "**To reach that dependent arising is itself nature-arising — all manner of transformations arise from the wondrous, perfect, true mind.**" The *Śūraṅgama*'s "all manner of transformations" are not mere dependent arising; in their essence they are nature-arising — born of the wondrous, perfect, true mind. And again: "Cultivation arises from nature, and cultivation in turn merges back into nature. Outside the true nature, not a single dharma can be obtained." Every one of the *Śūraṅgama*'s twenty-five perfect penetrations is "cultivation arising from nature"; at the utmost of cultivation, each is "cultivation merging back into nature." This closed loop is the *Śūraṅgama* version of Huayan's "the cause embraces the sea of fruit, the fruit penetrates the source of the cause."

Part Six of this book stopped at the tathāgatagarbha, because that is where the *Śūraṅgama* itself ends. This chapter of Part Eight re-positions that endpoint as the starting point of the Huayan perfect teaching.

### The Gate of Simultaneous Completion of Hidden and Manifest: The Perfect-Teaching Direct Perception of Repentance

This chapter corresponds to the fourth of the Ten Mysterious Gates — the **Gate of Simultaneous Completion of Hidden and Manifest.**

Chengguan, in his *Sub-Commentary*, uses the half-moon simile to explain this gate:

> Like the waxing and waning of the moon: when hidden it is not non-existent; when manifest it is not newly produced.

The hidden and the manifest of the half-moon obtain simultaneously. The moon's unseen portion (hidden) is not the absence of moon; the moon's seen portion (manifest) is not newly produced moon. Hidden and manifest do not deprive each other; they are two faces of one body.

Apply this structure to repentance —

**Phenomena (manifest):** you did such-and-such; the fruition stands before you vividly. You do not evade it; you take it fully upon yourself.

**Nature-empty (hidden):** beneath this deed and its fruit there is no independent, self-existing "karmic entity"; it is the manifestation of dependent flow, empty in that very moment.

**Simultaneous completion:** the two are not before-and-after; they are simultaneous. The moment of shouldering is the moment of release; the moment of release is the moment of shouldering.

Sincere repentance is neither "denying the karmic obstruction" (that is annihilationism) nor "being crushed by the karmic obstruction" (that is eternalism); it is completing the transformation by **shouldering, simultaneously**, both "the karmic obstruction is empty by nature" and "the phenomenal appearance is vivid and distinct."

Now we can return to the three things spoken of earlier in "Prostration Before the Buddha," and re-read them once through this structure.

**"The Buddha will hear you"** — that experience of being heard is not some distant Buddha stretching out his ear to listen to you. It is the **originally-present structure of "seeing" in the deepest place of your body-mind** (what Huayan calls "the Tathāgata's wisdom complete within the body") re-manifesting in the moment concealment is opened. What is originally present discloses itself in the act of repentance (manifest). But you did not newly receive a Buddha — you were always within this structure, merely covered before now (when hidden, it is not non-existent). Manifest and hidden obtain in the same instant.

**"The gods and Dharma-protectors rejoice and praise"** — this is precisely the realized face of that line in the *Chapter on the Manifestation of the Tathāgata*, "all the same nature, namely no nature." You have reconnected to the field of causes and conditions that was running all along. That field is not something coming in from outside; it is something you have never actually left. Your concealment did not seal you outside the field; it caused you to **temporarily mis-recognize the fact of your own continuous presence within it**. The moment concealment is opened, the nature-arising structure of "when one attains, all attain" immediately becomes directly perceptible at the level of your experience.

**"The body grows light"** — this is the most direct case of simultaneous completion of hidden and manifest. In the instant the thing grown in the dark dissolves, it does not "become non-existent" (that would be annihilationism, reading nature-arising as "the karma was never there"). It did happen — the phenomenon stands vividly (manifest); but beneath it there was never an independent, self-existing "karmic entity" that could go on pressing upon you, and the momentum of its continuing stream has been cut — the weight your body remembered releases its grip (hidden). The Āgamas' "the waters of Dharma wash the dust" and Huayan's "the wondrous true nature of the original Tathāgata-store" are, in this instant, the same thing — no water comes from outside, no dust is carried away; it is the originally-present purity disclosing itself in the moment concealment is opened.

### The Āgamas: Non-Concealment; Huayan: Hidden and Manifest Completed Together

Finally, a balance must be drawn.

The root principle of the Āgamas' instruction on repentance is "see the offense as offense, repent according to the Dharma" — the principle of non-concealment. This does not contradict the Perfect-Teaching reading of simultaneous completion of hidden and manifest: the Āgamas' "see the offense as offense" is honesty on the phenomenal side, and Huayan's "nature-empty, phenomena-vivid" is its deepening on the doctrinal side. **Non-concealment is the entry; simultaneity of hidden and manifest is the completion.**

A practitioner who understands Huayan but not the Āgamas easily reads "the karmic obstruction is empty by nature" as "the karmic obstruction does not exist," and loses honesty. Concealment then returns in an even subtler form — concealment that uses "emptiness" as its alibi is harder to uproot than the original concealment. A practitioner who understands the Āgamas but not Huayan easily reads repentance as "there is a real sin waiting to be washed off," and falls into anxiety, repenting and repenting until the burden grows heavier.

Holding both at once — phenomenal honesty in seeing the offense as offense, and the deep contemplation of hidden-and-manifest simultaneity on the doctrinal side — is complete repentance in the sense of the perfect teaching.

### Closing

The chapter ends here.

The Āgamas tell you the method: seeing the offense, disclosing it, guarding against recurrence — nine characters that are one complete operational sequence, capable of uprooting even patricide. The *Śūraṅgama* tells you the depth: what is disclosed when the fifty skandha-*māras* are exhausted is nothing other than the tathāgatagarbha you always had. Huayan tells you the grounds: what is disclosed is not newly made but originally thus — "takes non-arising as arising." You are not constructing a new self; you are recognizing again the self that never left.

The three scriptures converge perfectly upon this single act of repenting karmic obstructions. This is the true weight, within the perfect teaching, of the fourth of Samantabhadra's ten great vows.

The original awareness of suchness does not diminish by a hair's breadth because of your concealment; it only waits, beneath the concealment, for you to speak.

But before you move on to the next chapter, please do not read this one merely as a chapter. **Today, walk to the Buddha.**

The next chapter continues from the state that follows repentance — when that concealment has been opened and your body has reconnected to the field of conditions, the next action to surface naturally is rejoicing. To see another's wholesome roots and feel gladness, without giving rise to envy — this is not a moral precept; it is the inevitable manifestation, at the level of emotion, of the dharma-realm's structure of host and guest perfectly interfused. Chapter Five takes the Gate of the Host and Guest Perfectly Luminous and Endowed with Virtue as its window, and unfolds Samantabhadra's fifth vow: rejoicing in merit.

> Every chapter of this series stops somewhere. The place where it stops is not an oversight; it is reverence. A three-dimensional pen cannot write its way to a ten-dimensional object; all it can do is mark clearly the place where it is laid down — so that the reader knows that, beyond this line, one must rely on one's own Dharma-eye.

The pen of this chapter is laid down here.
