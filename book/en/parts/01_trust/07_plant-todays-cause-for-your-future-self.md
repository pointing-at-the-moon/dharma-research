## 1.7 Planting Today's Cause for Your Future Self

"I've donated so much, done so many good deeds—surely that one thing can be cancelled out by now?"

Almost everyone who studies the Dharma has asked this once, somewhere inside. The form varies—some want to cancel out a mistake from their youth, some a relationship in which they hurt someone, some a habit that is still going on—but the structure of the question is always the same: use good to offset evil, balance the account, and then breathe a sigh of relief.

The arithmetic does not work.

Not because the Dharma is harsh and unwilling to give you a chance, but because—seeds do not operate that way.

The last chapter spoke of *manaskāra* (attention) as the act of reaching a hand into the field of consciousness. Once the hand reaches in, what gets planted? Once planted, can it be cancelled by something else? Wholesome seeds and unwholesome seeds—how exactly do they coexist within the field of consciousness? This chapter opens the field up and looks inside.

### Karma → Seed → Fruit: Six Steps Pressed Into a Single Line

Yogācāra describes cause and effect with extraordinary precision. From a single thought creating karma to the final experiencing of the fruit, there are in fact six steps in between.

First, **creating karma**. Body, speech, and mind set an action in motion in the present moment—a flash of aversion, a flash of giving, a slanderous remark, a word of praise: each is creating karma. Second, **perfuming into a seed**. The action ceases the very moment it occurs, but it leaves a functional imprint within the ālaya-vijñāna, called a seed; the scriptures use the verb "perfume," meaning it is like fragrance permeating clothing—invisible yet genuinely present. Third, **continuity**. Once the seed is stored, it transmits continuously within the stream of consciousness; it does not vanish on its own. Fourth, **affliction-moistening**. For a seed to bear fruit, it needs afflictions to moisten it; without the nourishment of afflictions, the seed "withers and perishes"—it is still there, but cannot grow. Fifth, **ripening** (*vipāka*). When conditions are complete, the seed matures and attracts the fruit of the next stretch of life—which destiny you are reborn into, what you look like, whom you meet, all are determined at this step. Sixth, **cycling**. One stretch of fruit is fully experienced, the next batch of ripened seeds takes over, and so it circles on.

The whole mechanism requires no external "judge"; it is purely an internal, dynamic system of seeds and consciousness. Buddhism does not need to say "heaven keeps the ledger"—the ālaya-vijñāna is itself that ledger. But this ledger is not for tallying a total score. It is a **field of consciousness**.

The seed is in the field, and the field will water it or let it run dry, give it sun or shade, nourishment or neglect. Every point of leverage in practice is on the side of the "field," not on the side of the "total count of seeds."

### Of the Six Characteristics of Seeds, the Two You Can Use Right Now

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* sets out six conditions for a "seed," called the six characteristics of seeds. To the scholar, all six matter; to the practitioner, the two that are usable in the present moment are chiefly these:

**Determinacy of nature**—a wholesome cause attracts a wholesome fruit, an unwholesome cause an unwholesome fruit; they cannot be exchanged.
**Dependence on conditions**—a seed must wait until conditions are complete before it can bear fruit.

Put these two together, and they answer that opening line, "I did good deeds, surely that one thing can be cancelled out now?"—

No. Because of **determinacy of nature**. A wholesome seed can only grow into a wholesome fruit; an unwholesome seed can only grow into an unwholesome fruit. Within the field of consciousness they are two different kinds of seed, and they do not cancel each other out—just as rice and weeds do not merge into a single plant. No matter how well the rice grows in one plot, the weeds in the plot beside it will not vanish of their own accord because of it.

But **dependence on conditions** tells you something else: although the unwholesome seed is there, for it to grow out it needs conditions—it needs the corresponding affliction to water it, the corresponding environment to activate it. If the conditions change, so that the unwholesome seed never meets that condition, it stays right where it is, yet cannot grow into fruit. The scriptural phrase for this is "the karmic seed withers and perishes"—the seed is still there, but it has dried out, and can put forth no sprout.

Cutting through afflictions is the work of depriving the unwholesome seed of water.

### Good and Evil Cannot Cancel Each Other—But You Can "Stop the Feeding"

Stated plainly: **doing good deeds does not cancel out the bad deeds you have done, but it does two more important things.**

First, the wholesome seed will itself attract a wholesome fruit in the future—this is its own track, not dependent on cancellation. Second, while you continually perfume wholesome seeds, the entire structure of your mind-consciousness is changing—afflictions are decreasing, attention is turning toward the right, the *manas*'s grasping at "self" is loosening—and this amounts, at the same time, to cutting off the water supply of the unwholesome seeds.

So the correct strategy is not "do a few more good deeds to balance the account," but to walk two roads at once. One is called **continually perfuming wholesome seeds**: every thought of wise attention, every act of giving, every compassionate word, adds a new wholesome seed to the ālaya-vijñāna. The other is called **stopping the feeding of unwholesome seeds**: cutting off the afflictions that nourish those unwholesome seeds; aversion waters the seed of aversion, miserliness waters the seed of miserliness, and the moment a single thought of aversion is stopped, that moment waters it one time less.

These two are not the settling of accounts; they are the reordering of the whole structure of the field of consciousness.

Once the structure of the whole field has changed, even if certain past unwholesome seeds remain, they can no longer find the environment that would let them sprout. They have not been "cancelled out"—they have been "set aside."

For someone still carrying the past, this is a way out; for someone in the midst of making a choice, this is a map.

### ⚠️ A Structural Analogy: Genes Are Not Destiny

Modern biology has an idea called **epigenetics**—the same stretch of DNA, under different environments, behaviors, and stresses, has certain genes "switched on" and certain ones "switched off." The DNA sequence itself cannot be changed, but which genes get expressed can be influenced by what comes later.

In **structure**, this is very much like the "determinacy of nature plus dependence on conditions" of seed theory: the DNA sequence cannot be altered, corresponding to "determinacy of nature"; gene expression can be changed by environment, corresponding to "dependence on conditions." Borrowing this analogy is meant only to break a common intuition—"the past is the past, it cannot be changed." Epigenetics tells the modern person: the foundation is fixed, the expression is variable. Seed theory speaks of the same structure, but at the level of mind-consciousness rather than the level of molecules.

⚠️ Hold the boundary firmly: **this is only a structural correspondence, not an identity of essence.** Seeds are not genes, karmic force is not DNA. Epigenetics is a molecular mechanism at the material level; seed theory is a causal mechanism at the level of mind-consciousness—the two are, in essence, two different things. To take "seed theory equals genetics" literally is to demote the Dharma into a footnote of biology, and so to lose the very level it can genuinely reach into.

### The Three Easiest Roads to Go Astray

Once seed theory is misunderstood, it heads toward three extremes, each of which does real harm.

The first extreme is **fatalism**—"my fate is just like this, there's nothing to be done."

Some, having read seed theory, draw this conclusion: since all of this is past seeds attracting their fruit, whatever I do now is in vain.

This conclusion steps straight onto the opposite of "dependence on conditions." For a seed to bear fruit, it must await conditions—and conditions can be changed. A person whose constitution is quick to anger (this is a past seed manifesting) begins now to practice attention, to practice śamatha-vipaśyanā, to practice compassion—these are all changing the conditions. The seed will not be eliminated, but the speed, the intensity, and the form of its fruition will all change.

The greatest harm of fatalism is that it makes a person **give up on turning**. But the whole point of seed theory is precisely to teach a person how to turn.

The second extreme is **omnipotence of will**—"as long as I practice hard enough, any karma can be changed."

This is the opposite of fatalism, but its harm is no smaller.

"Determinacy of nature" sets an irreversible boundary: karma already created, fruit already ripened, must still be experienced if it is to be experienced. Even Śākyamuni Buddha, late in life, suffered headaches (the scriptures record this as karma from some past life); Maudgalyāyana, foremost in supernormal power, was in the end beaten to death by heterodox practitioners. If even the Buddha and the great arhats could not use practice to cancel out their own old karma, on what grounds does an ordinary person imagine that reciting a few sūtras and holding a few ceremonies can "wipe it all away"?

The greatest harm of omnipotence of will is that, without one's noticing, it feeds the "self"—"my practice is forceful enough, so I can change everything." After all that practice, what grows is the *manas*'s grasping at self, not the mind of renunciation.

Practice is not about letting a person jump ship from receiving their due; it is about letting a person, in the very midst of receiving it, no longer create new karma and no longer be swept away by old karma.

The third extreme is **the abuse of cause and effect**—"he got this illness, it's his karma."

This one is the most dangerous.

Cause and effect is a tool for **observing oneself**, not a weapon for **judging others**. Seeing a person suffer—losing a job, falling ill, a family breaking apart—if the first thought in your heart is "this is his karma," and then you let it go, that moment is not using the Dharma; it is using the Dharma to justify your own coldness.

When the Buddha taught cause and effect, it was always so that a person would ask, "What cause am I planting right now?"—never so that a person would calculate about others, "He must have planted such-and-such a cause." **Cause and effect is an endoscope, not a microscope.** Turned upon yourself, it sees clearly; turned upon others, what it sees is mostly your own inner contents projected outward.

When you see another suffer, the first action the Dharma teaches is compassion, not analysis. If, having read this chapter, you become more alert to your own seeds and more tender toward the suffering of others, you have read it rightly; if it makes you colder toward another's situation and quicker to "attribute," you have read it backward.

### One Thought, One Seed

Back to that opening line.

"I've done so many good deeds, surely that one can be cancelled out now?"—the very way this question is asked is still using the worldly merchant's abacus to keep the Dharma's account. In the field of consciousness there is no offsetting of positive against negative; there is only the accumulation of seeds and the turning of conditions.

But this is not bad news. On the contrary, it is enormously good news.

If good and evil could cancel each other out, then every unwholesome karma you have created in this life would be waiting there to settle accounts against the wholesome—the pressure too great to go on practicing at all. But seed theory tells you: the past is laid out on the track of the past, and what you can do now lies in **this very thought, right now**.

This thought of wise attention is a wholesome seed.
This thought that stops aversion is one less watering of an unwholesome seed.
This thought of meeting the person before you with compassion is a wholesome seed—and, in passing, it loosens the "self" a little too.
This thought, deciding how tomorrow morning will begin, sets a direction for the whole field of consciousness.

The time of a seed is measured in instants; but the accumulation of seeds is measured in lifetimes—one, and many. The things you do today are not so you can sleep well tonight, nor so your luck improves next month—they are for **that future you who has not yet grown out**, so that there may be a field of consciousness to stand upon.

The next chapter takes up a more fundamental question: since the causes of fruition are all in the field of consciousness, all within one's own mind, why does a person still keep seeking outward without end? Seeking a better job, seeking someone who understands them better, seeking a more efficacious method—where does this road of seeking outward end, when walked to its limit?

The answer is: a dead end. But to see this dead end clearly, one must first see clearly what happens within the field of consciousness. Only once it is seen clearly can one walk out.
