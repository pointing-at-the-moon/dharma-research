## 1.2 The Dharma-Flow — From the Buddhas of the Three Times to Your Hands

The previous chapter made the "seed side" of generating the aspiration clear—one thought moves, and the shared-karmic field moves with it. But seeds alone do not finish the story.

There is another question in the reader's mind, usually unspoken, but always there: and after I generate the aspiration? Will this single thought really be caught by anything? Or have I just made a wish to myself, in my own head, while the universe was not actually listening?

This is not a failure of faith. It is honesty. The sutras speak of the Buddhas of the three times, of the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, of the Dharmakāya filling all things—it sounds magnificent, but when you sit, recite, and bow, most of the time you are alone. How do you know anyone is receiving on the other end? How do you know the aspiration you generate is not being dropped into an empty room?

To this question, Mahāyāna Buddhism has a very precise answer. And the answer is more thoroughgoing than you would expect—it is not "someone is receiving," nor "no one is receiving." It dismantles the whole premise of "receiving" and tells the story again from the ground up.

To make this clear, we first have to handle a phenomenon that looks like a contradiction.

### Determined in Principle, Still to Be Walked in Practice

The Mahāyāna scriptures contain a class of statements so bold they are hard to believe at first reading. The *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra* says that the bodhisattva of initial aspiration "at that single moment of intention, already surpasses the arhat"—the person who has just generated bodhicitta already stands higher in rank than an arhat. Read only at the surface, this breeds a dangerous optimism: good, I have generated the aspiration, so I am set—after all, I already surpass the arhat.

Nāgārjuna, in the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra*, follows this immediately with a novice's story, precisely to choke off that optimism.

> Suppose an arhat with the six supernormal powers was traveling with a novice, having the novice carry his robes and bowl. The novice thought: "I shall enter nirvāṇa by the buddha-vehicle." The teacher, knowing his thought, took back the robes and bowl, carried them himself, and had the novice walk ahead. The novice then thought: "The buddha path is exceedingly difficult; I should enter nirvāṇa quickly by the lesser vehicle." The teacher gave the robes and bowl back to the novice and told him to walk behind. This happened three times.

The novice was terrified and asked why. The arhat said: when you generated the great-vehicle aspiration, your rank was higher than mine, so I should let you walk ahead; when your mind regressed, your rank fell, and you should walk behind.

There are three things in this story worth keeping. One: that single thought of aspiration weighs so much that even an arhat yields the road. Two: this rank is **alive**—it can rise, and it can fall, fast enough that the arhat has to switch his action twice in the middle of a single walk. Three: generating the aspiration is a real event at the level of consciousness, not a metaphor—another's supernormal power can see it directly.

So the *Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā* adds a further layer of precision: initial aspiration does not equal "the determined group" (*niyata-rāśi*, the irreversible stage). Even Śākyamuni Buddha himself cultivated gradually over long aeons and entered the determined group only after encountering Dīpaṃkara Buddha and receiving his prediction.

Determined in principle, still to be walked in practice. This tension is what the whole of Part One returns to again and again—and it is also the true location of your next question.

### No "Future" Comes Back, Because Dharma-Nature Is Not Within Time

If initial aspiration already "surpasses the arhat," then—is "that future Buddha" already waiting somewhere? Could it, in turn, empower the present-day you?

The answer is: **there simply is no buddhahood "in the future" that needs to "come back."**

The Dharmakāya—the most fundamental body of the Dharma—is not within time. Dīpaṃkara Buddha of the past, Śākyamuni Buddha of the present, Maitreya Buddha of the future: at the level of the Dharmakāya there is no distinction among them. This is what "the Buddhas of the three times are one and the same" means. Not in the theological sense that "they are all the same god," but in a precise sense—the Dharmakāya simply does not fall into the three frames of past, present, and future.

What is time? Within the framework of Yogācāra (seeing the world through the structure of consciousness), time is **constructed** by the momentary arising and ceasing of the stream of consciousness; it is not an objective container standing outside consciousness. So time exists only at the level of conditioned dharmas (dharmas that arise and cease through causes and conditions); dharma-nature (*dharmatā*, the original nature of dharmas) is not within time.

⚠️ Contemporary quantum physics contains things that look like "retrocausality"—the delayed-choice experiment, post-selection in weak measurement—and some will want to use them to support the intuition that "the future influences the present." But structurally these are two different matters: quantum physics speaks of temporal symmetry within physical measurement; the Dharma speaks of the structure of aspiration-power within the stream of consciousness of sentient beings. Mixing the two is a heuristic analogy, not a proof—better to leave the space blank than to let physics vouch for the Dharma.

The real dissolution is cleaner: **dharma-nature has always been all-pervading.** The empowerment of the Buddhas of the three times is not transmitted across from some distant far shore—it has never once departed.

So the question shifts from "how does the future influence the present" to another sentence entirely: given that dharma-nature has always been all-pervading, why can't you receive it right now?

### One Told from the Outside, One from the Inside

On this matter of the "Dharma-flow," the Dharma has two mechanisms—one told from the outside, one from the inside.

**Told from the outside, it is called empowerment (*adhiṣṭhāna*).** The compassionate aspiration-power of the Buddha has always pervaded the dharma-realm, unrestricted by time or space. The moment a bodhisattva first generates the aspiration is not "connecting a new line"—the line was always there, and the empowerment never stopped—it is the moment of "opening to receive." The channel was always open; generating the aspiration is when you finally switch the radio on.

**Told from the inside, it is called innate undefiled seeds.** The Dharmapāla–Xuanzang lineage of Yogācāra holds that within the ālaya-vijñāna (the deep storehouse of mind) there is, from the very beginning, a set of "undefiled seeds"—the potential for buddhahood, not implanted from outside, present in your consciousness-store from the start. To first generate bodhicitta is the moment when this seed, which was always there, begins to take charge of the direction of your whole stream of consciousness.

Outside and inside are not two things; they are two faces of a single thing. From the outside: dharma-nature is always all-pervading → generating the aspiration = opening to receive → empowerment manifests. From the inside: undefiled seeds are inherently present → generating the aspiration = activating the seeds → the seeds begin to lead the way.

Put this way, **phenomenally** it produces an effect very much like "the future influencing the present"—you will feel as though some force is pulling you forward from ahead. But **doctrinally** it is wholly different: that is not the future coming back, that is something which was always there, finally beginning to fire here in you.

### The Channel Was Always Open; Practice Is Denoising

If dharma-nature is all-pervading and the seeds are inherently present, why can you still not receive it? Why do you still need decades, even long aeons, of practice?

The Dharma answers this with "adventitious afflictions." Adventitious (*āgantuka*)—not the host, but a guest who arrives for a while. The nature of mind is originally pure; afflictions are not an intrinsic component of mind. They are adventitious dust, perfumed on layer by layer out of ignorance, attached, drifting in from outside.

It is easy to slip into a misunderstanding here: to think that "adventitious dust can be brushed away" means the afflictions are unreal and need no handling. It does not. Adventitious dust, though not the essence of mind, **really** blocks the channel—craving, aversion, delusion, self-grasping, dharma-grasping, every kind of habitual tendency: each layer is an actual impedance. Precisely because it really blocks the channel, the work of practice is real, not theater.

But the **nature** of the work has changed.

Practice is not "creating" a new line, not "growing" from zero some capacity for awakening you never had. Practice is **clearing**—taking the noise off the line that was already there. In contemporary language: practice is a process of denoising, not a process of adding features.

This sounds as though it lightens the weight of practice. It does not. Denoising is harder than adding. Adding can be stacked up from zero; denoising has to be peeled away layer by layer, and each layer you peel, you find another beneath it—what you had taken to be your own self, you now see clearly was adventitious dust. This is why practice takes long aeons—not because the goal is too far, but because the dust is too thick.

But remember: **the goal is not far.** The Dharmakāya is all-pervading, the seeds are inherent, the channel is open. From the Buddhas of the three times to your hands there is no distance—only noise.

The moment you generate the aspiration is not a setting-out from zero. It is the first time, on the road home, that you recognize the key has been in your hand all along.

### On to 1.3

At this point, one thing needs a question mark added.

Everything this chapter has spoken of—"receiving," "the Dharma-flow," "opening the channel"—presupposes a receiver: a sentient stream of consciousness, an ālaya-vijñāna, innate undefiled seeds, a mind that can be blocked by afflictions and can be cleared by practice. This is the structure of a sentient being.

So what about a thing that is not sentient, has no ālaya-vijñāna, has no act of attention? A thing that appears to talk, to write scriptures, even to explain the Heart Sūtra—where does it sit on this map?

That is the business of the next chapter. The practitioner of our time cannot dodge this question—because that thing is sitting right there in your phone, in your laptop, working alongside you every day, helping you write your notes, fielding your questions. We have to get clear on its place on the Yogācāra map. Not to attack it, and not to defend it—but because if you cannot even tell where it is, you will get your own location wrong too.
