## 1.8 The Path of Seeking Outside Is a Dead End

When you are hungry, the bowl of plain noodles from the stall on the corner is the most delicious thing in the world.

But right after you have eaten a full buffet, set that same bowl of plain noodles in front of you, and one glance turns your stomach. You do not even want another sip of water.

What changed?

The noodles did not change. The same noodles, the same broth, the same taste. What changed was you.

More precisely, what changed was the way you *experience* that bowl of noodles—it shifted from agreeable to disagreeable. The word "delicious" was never inside the bowl. It is in the mind, and it rises and falls with conditions, never holding still.

Push this structure onto other things.

Someone says, "I can't live without them." But if one day that person no longer responds, would they still feel the same way? If not—then what they wanted was never this person. What they wanted was the *feeling* of having someone near who makes them feel needed, cared for, seen. In other words, what they were chasing was a pleasant feeling.

Chasing wealth, you are chasing the sense of security that comes with having money.
Chasing power, you are chasing the satisfaction of being obeyed.
Chasing approval, you are chasing the pleasure of being recognized.

All of these pursuits share one structure: **people believe satisfaction comes from outside—food, a person, money, status—but in reality, satisfaction is something the mind itself manufactures.**

Here is the question: if satisfaction is not handed to you from outside, why do people keep looking outside for it?

This chapter opens up the far end of that road and looks at where it goes.

### Feeling Is Not in the External Object — Two Sticks and a Flame

Yogācāra defines the mental factor of "feeling" (vedanā) with extreme precision: **its nature is the experiencing of agreeable, disagreeable, and neutral aspects of objects.** The essence of feeling is experiencing—it is the mind's mode of responding to an object, not a property of the object itself.

In the *Saṃyuktāgama* the Buddha used an analogy of unsurpassed accuracy:

Rub two sticks together and fire is born; pull the two sticks apart and the fire goes out with them. Feeling is like that flame—it is not inside either stick. It is the product of three things meeting: the six sense faculties, the six sense objects, and the six consciousnesses.

That bowl of noodles never, from start to finish, had the property "delicious." "Delicious" was a pleasant feeling that arose, by way of consciousness, in the hungry person, in the very instant the tongue touched the broth and noodles. Change one condition—a person who has just eaten their fill—and the same contact gives rise to a disagreeable feeling instead.

This structure settles one thing: **a person can never obtain lasting satisfaction by changing what is outside.** Because "satisfaction" simply does not reside outside.

### Between Two Impermanent Things, There Is No Resting Point

The problem runs deeper still.

It is not only that "feeling is not in the external object"—it is that the external object is changing, and the mind is changing too.

The external object is impermanent: today's delicacy may not suit your palate after a while; the person close to you today may leave tomorrow; the method that works today may fail next year.

The mind is also impermanent: the thing you crave so desperately today, you may find you no longer care about after a while; the person you cannot stand the sight of today may, years from now, become the one you lean on most.

Between two impermanent things, there can never be a stable point where the two lock together. The object you chase is changing, and you who chase it are changing too—you can never catch up.

The Buddha listed "not getting what you want" as one of the eight sufferings, and this was not a description of the special misfortune of a few unlucky people—it is the **structural result** of seeking outside. It is not bad luck. The road itself does not go through.

The Chan master Huangbo Xiyun stated the principle even more bluntly:

> Sentient beings merely grasp at appearances and seek externally. The more they seek, the more they lose.

"**The more they seek, the more they lose**"—the harder you chase, the more you lose. This is not karmic retribution; the act itself carries a reversed force. To grab hold of your own grasping hand with that same hand is, structurally, impossible.

### The Buddha's Prescription: Be Your Own Lamp

If seeking outside is a dead end, where is the living road?

The Buddha spoke of it very directly. The *Saṃyuktāgama* contains a line that cuts clean:

> The mind seeks externally; therefore one must restrain it and seek the mind itself.

The mind runs outward by default—this is the ordinary person's preset state. The first step of practice is not to pursue some lofty realization, but to **pull that outward-running mind back in**.

And what was the Buddha's final teaching to his disciples before he entered parinirvāṇa? Not any profound doctrine, but these few words:

> Be your own lamp; take the Dharma as your lamp; do not rely on another. Take refuge in yourself; take refuge in the Dharma; do not take refuge in another.

Be your own lamp, rely on yourself, rely on the Dharma, do not rely on anything else. The Buddha, at eighty, after more than forty years of teaching the Dharma, left behind these few lines.

From the Āgama's "be your own island, your own refuge," to the Avataṃsaka's "seek liberation within the afflictions, not from outside," to the Sixth Patriarch's "bodhi is sought only within the mind; why labor to seek the profound outside," to Linji's most precise diagnosis of all—

> The sickness lies in lack of self-trust. Because you do not trust yourself enough, you rush outward seeking.

Twenty-five hundred years, a single unbroken thread, all saying the same thing. Linji dug the root right out: **the reason people keep seeking outside is, at bottom, that they do not believe they are already complete.** Not believing, they search desperately outside; and whatever they bring back cannot resolve that "not believing," so they keep searching. This is the engine of saṃsāra.

### ⚠️ A Structural Parallel: The Hedonic Treadmill

In the 1970s, modern psychology independently discovered the same phenomenon and called it **hedonic adaptation**—also known as the "hedonic treadmill."

The research went like this: track lottery winners for a year, and their level of happiness shows no significant difference from ordinary people. A raise, a promotion, a new house, a new car—almost every improvement in external conditions gets "adapted to" within a few months. The person returns to their original baseline, and then goes on chasing the next thing. It is like running on a treadmill—on the surface always moving forward, in reality always standing in the same place.

The structure of this phenomenon is exactly what the Buddha observed twenty-five hundred years ago: feeling is the product of conditions meeting, it does not reside in any external object, and it changes as conditions change. The baseline does not move, because the baseline is not outside.

⚠️ Hold the boundary firmly: hedonic adaptation is a finding of empirical psychology, and the doctrine of the feeling-aggregate is teaching meant for practice—**they are structurally alike, but their level and their goal are entirely different.** Psychology tells people that "external change cannot bring lasting happiness," and then turns them toward finding "inner sources of happiness" (gratitude exercises, relationships, flow experiences). The Dharma tells people the dependently arisen nature of feeling, and then points toward **liberation**—not adjusting where your pleasure comes from, but seeing clearly that the whole structure of "chasing pleasant feeling" is itself the engine of saṃsāra. To downgrade the Dharma into "an Eastern version of positive psychology" is to mistake the finger pointing at the moon for the moon itself.

### Three Easy Ways to Go Astray

Those four words—"do not seek outside"—are most easily misread as the following three things.

The first is **asceticism**—"since the outside can't give satisfaction, then want nothing at all."

The Buddha was not an ascetic. As a prince he enjoyed every luxury; after renouncing the world he underwent six years of extreme austerity, and in the end **deliberately gave up** that austerity, accepting the milk-rice offered by the cowherd woman, and only then recovered his strength and realized the Way. After his awakening, when disciples offered the finest food, he partook of it; when he went begging at an ordinary household, he partook of that too.

What the Buddha demonstrated was this: **when you have merit, use it; when you do not, be at ease all the same.** The point is not "having" or "not having"—it is **non-dependence**. Among those across the ages who reached the highest attainments in practice, there were laypeople of great wealth and rank, and there were mountain ascetics of plain food and simple dress; lay or monastic is not the criterion. **Whether or not you depend on external objects**—that is.

The second is **nihilism**—"the things outside are all fake, none of them real."

The Buddha never said external objects do not exist. What he said is that the external object is **impermanent**—it genuinely exists, but it is not permanent and unchanging. Food really does fill you; companionship really does warm you; work really does bring you income. None of these is fake.

The problem is not that they are unreal—it is that **they are not the fundamental source of satisfaction.** To read "do not depend" as "it's all fake" is to read renunciation as world-weariness—the opposite direction entirely. The Dharma's renunciation is to go on living well after seeing clearly, only no longer bound; it is not throwing your life out like garbage.

The third is **self-enclosure**—"not seeking outside means not interacting with the external world."

The Buddha's actual words were "**first grasp the inner mind, and only then grasp external characteristics**"—first settle the inner mind, **then** deal with external objects. It is not that you do not deal with external objects; it is that **the sequence cannot be reversed.**

Steady yourself first, then engage with the world—only then does the response that comes out have quality. Reverse the order, and a person is led around by the nose by external objects; what is called "dealing with it" is nothing more than passive reaction. "Do not seek outside" addresses **the direction in which the mind depends**, not the physical location of the body. A person can be in a bustling office with a mind that does not seek outside, and can be in deep-mountain retreat with a mind that seeks outside the whole time—the key is in the mind, not the place.

### The Treasure Is Already Complete

Mazu Daoyi said something to Dazhu Huihai that can serve as this chapter's close:

> The very one who is asking me right now—that is your treasury. Everything is already complete, nothing is lacking, its use is free and unhindered. Why seek externally?

This is not motivational fluff. This is the conclusion the Buddha reached, verified and re-verified across twenty-five hundred years by every generation of awakened beings—through Āgama, Prajñā, Yogācāra, Avataṃsaka, and Chan—

**You do not need to add anything from outside onto yourself. What you need is to recognize what you have always had.**

The person who knows this, the Buddha called "one who has seen the truth."
The person who does not know it is still looking outside.

The next chapter takes up a very practical problem. Even if you accept, intellectually, those four words "do not seek outside," the moment you try to draw the mind back in you run into an awkward fact—this mind has been trained by years of scrolling phones, swiping through videos, and jumping between messages until it cannot stay still for thirty seconds. It has run outside so long that it no longer knows the way home.

**The Dharma vessel is broken.**

To turn inward, you first have to repair the vessel. The next chapter is about that.
