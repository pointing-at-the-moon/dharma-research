## 1.4 AI Generates No Merit — The Contemporary Wrong View of Mistaking a Tool for a Seed

In the last few years a new story about getting rich has swept the globe: AI can make you money. AI trading bots, AI prediction markets, AI that runs your account for you. Social media overflows with screenshots of "passive income," and influencers parade their results from "making money automatically." The logic of this story is extremely simple — **owning the right tool equals obtaining wealth.** AI is the most powerful tool in human history, therefore AI equals wealth.

And yet one plainly obvious fact is systematically ignored: **people using the very same tool get radically different results.** Institutional investors profit from AI trading; retail traders lose money with the same AI. Same strategy, same market, same tool — where does the difference lie?

The mainstream explanation is technical: different strategies, different amounts of capital, different execution speeds. These explanations are not wrong, but they stay at the surface, and they sidestep a more fundamental question: **what is the true source of wealth?**

The Buddha gave a precise answer two thousand five hundred years ago. And this contemporary "get rich with AI" talk, seen through the lens of the Dharma, is a structural inversion of cause and effect — it mistakes the tool for the seed.

This chapter is about seeing that inversion clearly. The next chapter (1.5) will turn back and deal with the other side: since AI is not a seed, how exactly should it be used as a tool.

### The Direct Cause of Wealth: Long-Night Generosity

The Saṃyuktāgama contains one of the Buddha's most direct teachings on the causality of wealth. Someone had questioned whether the Buddha, leading his assembly of monks on alms rounds, was harming the welfare of householders. The Buddha replied:

> I recall ninety-one kalpas into the past, and have never seen a single person who gave to a single bhikṣu experience any exhaustion or diminishment from that act. Village chief! Observe those families today who are greatly wealthy, with abundant money, many dependents, many servants — you should know that such families have been generous through the long night, truthful and serene. It is for this reason they enjoy such blessings.

The doctrinal meaning of this passage is utterly clear. The Buddha did not say, "Observe those wealthy families today — you should know they used their tools well." He did not say, "you should know they were skilled in commerce." He said "generous through the long night" — the **direct cause** of wealth (the cause itself, the seed) is giving, an inner seed of wholesome karma, not any external instrumental condition.

"Long night," in the Āgamas, is an idiom for "since beginningless time," pointing to an exceedingly deep past. "Truthful and serene" qualifies the manner of the giving: not for fame or gain, not for display, but given with a sincere and tranquil mind. "It is for this reason they enjoy such blessings" is a verdict on cause and effect — today's wealth is the *result* of generosity sustained through the long night, not the product of any present condition.

In the same passage, the Buddha goes on to list eight causes of wealth's diminishment: oppression by kings, plunder by thieves, fire, flood, the natural depletion of stores, debt, enmity, and squandering by unworthy heirs — with "impermanence" added as the ninth. Nine causes in all, and **not one of them is "lacking tools."**

The acquisition of wealth lies in past wholesome seeds. The diminishment of wealth lies in external calamity and inner impermanence. The tool — whether the farming implement of antiquity or the AI of today — is simply not a key variable in the arising and perishing of wealth within the Buddha's framework of cause and effect.

### Direct Cause and Auxiliary Condition: The Difference Between Seed and Soil

Yogācāra divides "cause" into four kinds (the four conditions). Here we need only look at the two that matter most:

**The direct cause** — the seed itself, the immediate cause that produces the fruit. The wholesome karmic seeds accumulated in the ālaya-vijñāna are the direct cause.

**The contributing condition** — the condition that gives a push from the side. Not the seed, but something that helps the seed mature, or that at least does not obstruct it.

An analogy: to harvest rice you need rice seed (the direct cause), and you also need soil, rain, and sunlight (the contributing condition). However fertile the soil, without a seed no rice will grow; however good the seed, fallen on concrete it will grow nothing either. Both are needed, but **their roles cannot be swapped.** The seed is primary, the soil assists.

What is AI within this framework? Chapter 1.3 already argued it: an AI chatbot is part of the **vessel world** (*bhājana-loka*), a static cross-section of humanity's collective karmic consciousness. It is not a sentient being, it creates no karma, it generates no merit. The most it can do is play the role of a **contributing condition** — to be a patch of soil, and it can even be very fertile soil.

But it is absolutely not the direct cause. **A person without merit seeds will grow no fruit of wealth, no matter how precise the tool.** With one and the same AI trading system, some profit and some lose — and the difference is not in the tool, but in the structure of the seeds in the user's ālaya-vijñāna. Behind the institutional investor there are often several generations of accumulated capital, relationships, and knowledge; behind the retail trader there is often nothing at all. Same soil, different seeds dropped into it — of course what grows is different.

The inversion in a single sentence: **the words "AI makes me money" mistake the soil for the seed.**

### Similar Fruit Does Not Mean Identical Cause

This inversion is hard to dislodge because it has a piece of **surface evidence**: some people really do make money with AI. That is not false.

But "some people made money with AI" does not mean "AI is the cause of the money."

In the Madhyamāgama the Buddha used the Ganges as a metaphor: once merit begins to mature, "whether going or coming, whether standing or sitting, whether sleeping or waking, whether by day or by night, their merit constantly arises, ever increasing and expanding. Just as the waters of the Ganges, flowing from their source into the great ocean, grow ever deeper and broader along the way." Merit is **self-amplifying** — once a wholesome seed is set in motion, its fruit, like the waters of the Ganges, naturally grows ever broader and deeper.

A person with merit seeds will make money. In an age without AI he would profit in business; in the age of the internet he would profit running an online store; in the age of AI he profits using AI too. The tool keeps changing, but the making of money never stops — because the cause is in the seed, not the tool.

A person without merit seeds will not make money. In the agricultural age his crops fail; in the industrial age he is laid off from the factory; in the age of AI he loses money trading with AI. It is not AI that ruined him — it is that the seed was never in his ālaya-vijñāna to begin with.

To see a person with merit make money using AI and conclude "AI made him money" is to **mistake identical cause from similar fruit.** He would have made money before he ever touched AI. AI is merely the tool in his hand at the moment; it is not the cause of his wealth.

### What Is Being Refuted Is the Inversion, Not AI

This section has to be said plainly enough, because it is easily misread. "AI generates no merit" is not opposition to AI, not opposition to technology, not nostalgia. AI as a contributing condition is good — fertile soil is always good, never bad. The problem was never the soil; the problem is mistaking the soil for the seed.

Some readers will reach this point and think the chapter is attacking AI. It is not. AI has done nothing wrong — the vessel world is in itself neither good nor evil; what is wrong is treating the vessel world as the direct cause of merit. Some will think the chapter is urging people not to use AI. It is not. The next chapter (1.5) will lay out precisely how to use AI well as a contributing condition for accumulating merit — used rightly, a contributing condition can indeed help wholesome karma to mature. Some will think the chapter is preaching some retro, hand-made, anti-technology Dharma. Even less so. The Buddha's age did not oppose the plough, did not oppose the loom, did not oppose the boat — the Dharma has never opposed tools; it opposes only the cognitive error of **mistaking a tool for a seed.**

What is refuted is the inversion, not AI. AI is the neutral vessel world: used rightly it helps you generate merit; used wrongly it merely amplifies the karma you already have.

### The Ekottarāgama's Forecast: The Miser Hears the Teaching on Giving Like a Knife on a Wound

Two thousand five hundred years ago the Buddha already described the reader-reaction this chapter is about. The Ekottarāgama says:

> Teaching the Dharma of giving to a stingy person — this too is exceedingly difficult... When a stingy person hears the teaching on giving, they give rise to anger and harmful intent, like a knife cutting into an unripe abscess — the pain is unbearable.

The psychological structure of the pursuit of "getting rich with AI" matches the Buddha's "stingy person" closely — what they pursue is the shortcut of reward without effort, the fast lane that substitutes a tool for one's own wholesome karma. When someone points out that "AI generates no merit; the cause of wealth is in giving," their reaction is exactly the "anger" and "harmful intent" the Buddha foresaw — like a knife cutting into an unripe abscess, the pain unbearable.

If reading this far you feel offended, that is precisely the reaction the Buddha forecast long ago. There is no need to push the reaction away — it is itself a doorway into practice, letting you see the very seed in your own ālaya-vijñāna. To see it is the beginning of turning.

### Closing: We Refute in Order to Establish

This chapter is a **refutation.** It refutes the causal inversion of "getting rich with AI"; it refutes the contemporary wrong view of mistaking a tool for a seed.

But **refutation is not the goal; establishment is the goal.**

The Buddha never negated for the sake of negating. When he says "generosity through the long night" is the direct cause of wealth, it is not to make you despair of AI — it is to turn your attention from "finding a tool" to "planting a seed." From the question "how much money can AI make me" to the question "is there even a money-making seed in my ālaya-vijñāna."

Since AI is not the direct cause, how should it be used rightly as a contributing condition? The tool itself is neutral; it is the user's mind that decides whether it aids the wholesome or aids the unwholesome. The next chapter unfolds this "establishing" — beginning from the graded path the Buddha taught his lay disciples, looking at how the three things of giving, moral discipline, and rebirth in the heavens can go on being practiced in the age of AI.

The tool has changed; the seed has not. The only thing that can plant a seed has always been your own mind.
