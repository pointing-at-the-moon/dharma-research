# General Preface
## The Eight Movements — From the First Aspiration to the Ocean-Seal Samādhi

> The true nature of all dharmas: it has always been just so.

---

## I. How It Began: One Question, Asked from S1 All the Way to S8

The starting point of this book was not a book. It was a question.

In the winter of 2025 I began writing the first paper of the *Pointing at the Moon* series, "The Butterfly Effect of the First Aspiration." At that time there was no conception of eight movements, no premonition of ninety-one papers, no book. There was only one question I could not get around: **from the first aspiration to its fulfilment, the road that runs between—what does it actually look like, within the framework of Yogācāra?**

This was not an academic question. It was a question of practice—I myself was on the road, and I needed to know where I stood, where to step next, and how I would know if I had stepped wrong. The papers were a by-product. By the time the by-products had accumulated to the tenth, "The Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening," I found that the first series had already finished saying what it meant to "set out on this road"—from the first aspiration, to one's position in the age of AI, to the chain of cause and effect, to becoming a vessel for the Dharma, to the thirty-seven factors of awakening as a complete methodology already prepared twenty-five hundred years ago. The series had closed itself. I called it S1, *Trust*.

Then came S2. Once one has set out on the road, the next question rises of its own accord: **after trust, by what do we see clearly?** The answer is prajñā. And so the eleven papers on the *Heart Sūtra* began from a fresh re-parsing of "prajñā · heart · sūtra," and ran all the way through to the three kinds of practitioner and the perfect interfusion of the exoteric and the esoteric. S2, *Understand*, also closed itself.

The question of S3 was: **once we see clearly, by what do we walk?** The answer is the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* and the *Verses on the Structure of the Eight Consciousnesses*—Yogācāra takes the ground of mind apart to its finest grain, and only then is a workable practice possible. The question of S4 was: **walking in this year of 2026, in the age of AI, with the vessel world grown into this shape, what is a practitioner to do?** This is *Inquiry*—not the Dharma putting questions to AI, but the practitioner putting questions to the Dharma, finding one's footing in an environment without precedent.

S5, the *Diamond Sūtra*, is *Cutting*—the blade of prajñā, severing every notion of self, person, being, and life-span that can be severed, including the notion of "practitioner" itself. S6, the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*, is *Illumination*—lighting up and breaking through the fifty skandha-māras in the depths of the ālaya-vijñāna, one by one, all the way to "the final recognition between the two pillars." S7, the *Lotus Sūtra*, is *Gathering-in*—every skilful means, every provisional teaching, every sentient being, all gathered into the one buddha-vehicle. S8, the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, is *Manifestation*—the dependent arising of the dharma-realm, the ten profound gates, the ten great vows of Samantabhadra, the Ocean Seal Samādhi.

**Trust · Understand · Practice · Inquiry · Cutting · Illumination · Gathering-in · Manifestation.**

These eight words were not an outline I planned in advance. They are a shape I discovered only when, having written from S1 through S8, I looked back. The seams between the eight series were not designed by me; they are the Dharma's own logic—each time I finished a series, the next question rose up by itself, and that question turned out to be the very eye of the next series. At the moment I finished writing "The Ten Great Vows Beneath the Ten Windows" in S8, I sat at my desk for a long while, stunned. For I saw that these eight words, strung together, form a complete circle—from the gesture of dedication in Huayan, back again to the first aspiration of S1.

**This is a book that was being written all along, and I simply did not know it.**

The ninety-one papers are ninety-one stones along the road, picked up one by one, until at the end I found that they had always been a single road. That road now needs to be joined together. The footnotes and the academic apparatus were written for researchers; those stay in `papers/`. But the people who are truly walking the road—who want to generate the aspiration, who want to see clearly, who want to walk out of the fog of the AI age, who want to take up the blade of diamond prajñā, who want to see through the modern mutations of the fifty skandha-māras in the depths of their own ālaya-vijñāna, who want to understand why the Lotus and the Avataṃsaka are two sides of one and the same thing—these people do not need papers. They need a book. A book that is continuous, readable, and that, turned from the first page to the last, truly arrives somewhere.

This book exists for exactly that.

Not new doctrine, but an ordering. Not a rewriting, but a stepping-down. Not a compendium of academic results, but the joining of ninety-one stones into a road—**a road that runs from the first aspiration to the Ocean Seal Samādhi.**

## II. Why This Sequence: The Inner Logic of the Eight Movements

Someone may ask: why trust, understand, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation? Why not trust, understand, practice, realization? Why not follow the traditional doctrinal classifications (Tripiṭaka, shared, distinct, perfect; or small, beginning, final, sudden, perfect)? Why bring AI in as S4?

The answer has three layers.

**The first layer: the practitioner's actual line of movement.** Traditional doctrinal classification looks from the angle of the Buddha's teaching (which dharmas he taught across a lifetime, into how many periods they divide, which are provisional, which are real). The Eight Movements look from **the angle of the practitioner walking the road**—I stand here, where do I step next? This vantage point fixes the sequence of the eight parts so that none can be skipped. Trust is the starting point, because without trust there is no setting out at all. Understanding follows trust, because to trust without seeing clearly is blind faith. Practice follows understanding, because to see clearly and not act is mere lip-service. Inquiry follows practice—and this step is the original contribution of the Eight Movements: **when you truly begin to walk, you collide with the problem peculiar to your own age, and our age happens to have collided with AI.** If inquiry is not resolved, practice stalls where it stands.

Cutting follows inquiry, because the final resolution of the AI problem is not technology but the blade of prajñā—the *Diamond Sūtra* cuts away even the notion of "myself as practitioner." Illumination follows cutting, because what remains after the cutting is not a blank emptiness but the capacity to see more deeply. The illumination of the Śūraṅgama is not a beginner's seeing; it is a seeing that has passed through the diamond cut, able to discern the exceedingly subtle self-disguises of the fifty skandha-māras. Gathering-in follows illumination, because at the end of all that seeing one discovers: everything you thought you had cut away, everything you thought you had broken through, is—in the view of the Lotus's one buddha-vehicle—one single thing, one single sentient being, all walking through the same conjured city toward the same place of treasure. Manifestation follows gathering-in, because the terminus of gathering-in is not to be sealed inside the one buddha-vehicle and stop there; it is **the entire dharma-realm unfolding all at once beneath the ten profound gates**—the Ocean Seal Samādhi, the myriad images appearing together.

And then manifestation returns to trust. For the terminus of Huayan is the ten great vows of Samantabhadra, and the first vow is "to revere and worship all Buddhas"—that is the posture of trust. The tenth vow is "to dedicate all merit universally"—that is the posture of sending the whole of one's practice outward, keeping nothing for oneself. To return from Huayan to trust is not to retreat to the starting point; it is the structure of the perfect teaching, in which **the starting point was the terminus all along, and the terminus was the starting point all along.** This is why, when S8 came to its close, what I saw was a circle and not a straight line.

**The second layer: Yogācāra as the skeleton.** The underlying grammar of the *Pointing at the Moon* research is Yogācāra—seeds, actualization, the ālaya-vijñāna, the *manas*, the five universal mental factors, the four afflictions, the three natures, the turning-of-the-basis. Why? Because Yogācāra is Buddhism's most precise system for describing "how the mind operates," and practice is, in essence, **an operation of the mind.** Without a precise psychological description, practice degenerates into slogans.

But Yogācāra is not the whole. Yogācāra is the bone; **the eight schools are the flesh.** Each part of the Eight Movements has a principal sūtra or a core set of texts: S1 is the Āgamas and the stages of the path to awakening; S2 is the *Heart Sūtra*; S3 is the *Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas* and the *Verses on the Structure of the Eight Consciousnesses* (with the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra*); S4 is the cross-disciplinary work and Yogācāra's positioning of AI; S5 is the *Diamond Sūtra*; S6 is the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra*; S7 is the *Lotus Sūtra*; S8 is the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*. This is not setting the eight schools side by side; it is **using Yogācāra as a layer of translation, so that the eight schools converse on one and the same map of the mind.** The *Diamond Sūtra*'s "let the mind arise without dwelling anywhere" and the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s "the gate of the mutual containment of the one and the many"—spoken in Yogācāra's language of seeds and actualization—are two angles on one and the same turning-of-the-basis. This cross-school conversation is not for the sake of comparative religion; it is so that the practitioner does not lose the way while walking—so that, no matter which sūtra you happen to be reading today, you can find its corresponding place on the map of the mind.

**The third layer: modernity as the skin.** Yogācāra for the bone, the eight schools for the flesh—and there is still one layer short: **the modern for the skin.** Every paper in the *Pointing at the Moon* research carries a "cross-disciplinary convergence" passage, setting the structures of the Dharma against the modern disciplines: cognitive science, neuroplasticity, CBT/ACT, predictive processing, quantum physics, complex systems, AI and machine learning. This is not to have modern science vouch for the Dharma—the Dharma has needed no one to vouch for it these twenty-five hundred years. It is to **give the modern reader a familiar way in.** A modern mind raised on Darwin, Freud, Kant, and Turing needs a modern interface in order to enter the Dharma. Cross-disciplinary convergence is that interface.

But cross-disciplinary convergence must be strictly marked as **heuristic analogy**, and must not slide into "physics proves the Dharma" or "the Dharma predicted quantum mechanics." This is a discipline the *Pointing at the Moon* research established from its very first paper, and the book preserves it strictly. Every cross-disciplinary passage is condensed in the book, but the ⚠️ marker and the middle-path correction are kept, to guard against misreading.

So the complete formula of the Eight Movements is this:

> **Yogācāra for the bone, the eight schools for the flesh, the modern for the skin, and the practitioner's actual line of movement for the spine.**

Trust, understand, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation—that is the spine.

## III. For Whom: The Reader This Book Has in View

This book is not written for everyone. Let me first say clearly what it **is not**, and then say what it is.

**This book is not:**
- Not an introduction to Buddhism. If you have had no contact with the Dharma at all, this book will be too dense; I would suggest first reading a modern selected translation of the Āgamas, or any basic introduction to Buddhist teaching.
- Not an academic monograph. The academic version lives at github.com/pointing-at-the-moon/dharma-research, where all ninety-one papers are open-sourced, with complete CBETA indexing and full footnotes—researchers are welcome to help themselves.
- Not a work of sectarian apologetics. *Pointing at the Moon* belongs to no modern school, and defends no school. Yogācāra, prajñā, tathāgatagarbha, Huayan, Lotus, Chan, Pure Land, esoteric—in the view of *Pointing at the Moon* these are all one and the same Dharma unfolding along different facets.
- Not spiritual comfort food. This book will not tell you that "everything will be fine," nor will it hand you "daily positive vibes." The core of the Dharma is **knowing and seeing things as they are**, not the soothing of emotion.

**This book is:**
- A **route map for practice**. If you are already on the road, this book offers an overall map of the terrain, so that you know where you stand, where to walk next, and how to tell when you have walked wrong.
- A book on **applying Yogācāra in the modern context**. Yogācāra is not an antique in a museum; it is an operating manual that can precisely diagnose contemporary mental phenomena—from information overload, fractured attention, and algorithmic anxiety, to the question of existential footing in the age of AI, Yogācāra can give a precise reading.
- An **experiment in cross-school conversation**. The *Diamond Sūtra* and the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*, the *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* and the *Lotus Sūtra*, the Āgamas and Yogācāra—this book attempts to let them converse within one shared language, rather than each speaking past the others.
- A book **for those with the good fortune** to have lived a full life, to carry some cultural grounding, and to have time—people whose deepest need is a book they can sit down with and read slowly, pausing at every chapter to think. The pace of this book's language is calibrated for these readers: no smoke screen of jargon, no translation-ese Chinese; clear where clarity is needed, and silent where silence is needed.

**The ideal reader:** someone in the second half of life who seriously wants to spend the remaining time on practice, but who has been left a little disoriented by the modern information environment. You may have read the *Heart Sūtra* but never finished the *Śūraṅgama*; you may know that "all is made by mind alone" but not how the eight consciousnesses operate; you may feel anxiety about the age of AI but not know how the Dharma views the matter; you may have heard a teacher's discourse at a temple but found you could not connect it back to daily life once you got home. **This book exists precisely to mend that break.**

**Suggestions for reading:**

- **The first reading:** straight through, from beginning to end. The sequence of the eight parts has its inner logic, and reading out of order loses the spine. Skip past a chapter you do not understand—do not get stuck.
- **The second reading:** choose one part and go deep. You may find that one part in particular moves you (for some it is S2, *Understand*; for some S5, *Cutting*; for some S6, *Illumination*); then return and read that part over and over, alongside the original sūtra.
- **The third reading:** cross-check against the paper version. If you want the academic detail of an argument, a CBETA source, or the full literature of a cross-disciplinary convergence, go to the GitHub repo and find the corresponding paper—every chapter carries a paper-number reference.
- **At any time:** when you meet a technical term you do not understand, turn to Appendix C, "Glossary of Terms," at the back of the book. When you meet a passage of scripture you want to verify further, turn to Appendix A, "Academic Footnotes." When you want to see the full version of a cross-disciplinary correspondence, turn to Appendix B.

## V. Dedication

> May it be, through the causes and conditions of this book,
> that every reader, within the prajñā of the written word, sees the prajñā of contemplative illumination; and within the prajñā of contemplative illumination, accords with the prajñā of true reality;
> seeing the finger and thereby knowing the moon, seeing the moon and thereby forgetting the finger—finger and moon not two, grasper and grasped both fallen away.
>
> May whatever merit this book accomplishes
> be dedicated to all sentient beings throughout the dharma-realm—
> that those who have not heard the Dharma may hear it; that those who have heard it may practise it; that those who practise it may realize it; and that those who realize it may ferry others across.
>
> May the road of the Eight Movements,
> from the single thought of the butterfly's wing-beat at the first aspiration,
> walk all the way to the myriad images appearing together in the Ocean Seal Samādhi—
> thought after thought, all of it a universal dedication.

**The true nature of all dharmas: it has always been just so.**
