## I. The Lay of the Land: The Eight Movements at a Glance

This book is made of eight parts, plus a general preface, a reading map (the one you are reading now), seven transition chapters, and two appendices. If you imagine the whole book as a road that climbs from the foot of a mountain to its summit, the eight parts are eight passes along the way—each pass with one central question, one central scripture, one central stance. The sketch below lets you take in the whole road at a glance, without first having to read the general preface through.

**Part One · trust: setting out on this road**

Central question: where does a person begin? Central scripture and treatise: the Āgamas and the Stages of the Path to Enlightenment. Central stance: aspiration. From "The Butterfly Effect of Initial Aspiration" to "The Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening," ten chapters lay out "the matter of setting out"—why a single thought of aspiration is enough to change the whole field of shared karma, why AI generates no merit, why awareness is itself the turning, why seeking outward is a dead end, why the thirty-seven factors of awakening were already a complete methodology two thousand five hundred years ago. When you finish Part One, you will know that you are "standing at the crossroads."

**Part Two · understand: seeing clearly by prajñā**

Central question: once on the road, what lets you see clearly? Central scripture: the Heart Sūtra. Central stance: contemplation. It begins by re-parsing the three words "prajñā · heart · sūtra," moves through Avalokiteśvara's closed loop of awakening, the emptiness of the *five aggregates*, the *twelvefold dependent arising*, "no wisdom and no attainment," and the mind without obstruction, and closes with the integration of exoteric and esoteric across three kinds of practitioner. The full meaning of the Heart Sūtra's two hundred and sixty characters unfolds across eleven chapters—but this is not a word-by-word commentary; it is a practitioner's map of contemplation.

**Part Three · practice: walking by Yogācāra**

Central question: once you have seen clearly, what lets you walk? Central scripture and treatise: the Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas, the Verses Delineating the Eight Consciousnesses, the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra. Central stance: precision. Fourteen chapters plus one supplementary chapter take mind-consciousness apart to its finest grain—the selflessness of all dharmas, the three transformations, the *ālaya-vijñāna*, *manas*, the sixth mind-consciousness, the first five consciousnesses, the fifty-one mental factors, form, the dissociated formations, the unconditioned, and the transformation of consciousness into wisdom. This is the part that most resembles a "toolbox," and it reads a little dense—but once you finish it, you will know that practice is no longer a matter of feeling your way; it has coordinates.

**Part Four · inquiry: practising in the age of AI**

Central question: walking through this era, the year 2026, what is one to do? Central theme: the encounter between AI and the Dharma. Central stance: honesty. These eight chapters are not a Yogācāra verdict handed down upon AI; they are a practitioner relocating themselves within an unprecedented environment—the equality of the dependent nature, mutual illumination, shared practice, the person in the mirror, the projection of *manas*, how the Buddha would use AI, the living eye, sentient beings that are not sentient beings. Part Four is the original contribution of The Eight Movements—the other seven parts have all been walked by the ancients, but "inquiry" is the one part the contemporary practitioner has to collide their own way through.

**Part Five · cutting: wielding the diamond prajñā**

Central question: after seeing clearly, walking, and inquiring, what lets you cut? Central scripture: the Diamond Sūtra. Central stance: the blade's edge. Ten chapters begin with "the is-not three-part formula," and move through the kōan mirror, the first cut into appearances, the parable of the raft, the weighing of merit, "let the mind arise without abiding anywhere," the self's recurrence, "the Tathāgata cannot be seen through appearances," and the six similes "like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow," closing with "from the hundred dharmas to the diamond"—lifting Part Three's toolbox up a dimension into illusory designation. Part Five's most dangerous misreading is the view of annihilation, and so every chapter comes with its middle-path correction.

**Part Six · illumination: diagnosing with the Śūraṅgama**

Central question: after the cutting, what lets you see the deeper disguises? Central scripture: the Śūraṅgama Sūtra. Central stance: diagnosis. Thirteen chapters begin with an introduction to the Śūraṅgama, and move through the seven locations probed for the mind, the ten demonstrations of seeing, the four categories and seven elements, the twenty-five means to perfect penetration, the three gradual steps, the four pure and clear instructions, and finally the five sections—form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness—of the fifty skandha-māras. This is the hardest part of the whole book, because the Śūraṅgama is itself the demon-revealing mirror of the age of the Dharma's decline. Read Part Six a little more slowly, and skip for now any chapter you do not follow.

**Part Seven · gathering-in: drawing together with the Lotus**

Central question: when illumination reaches its end, are all the distinctions still real distinctions? Central scripture: the Lotus Sūtra. Central stance: gathering the three back into one. Twelve chapters begin with the "true mark of all dharmas" from the "Skillful Means" chapter, and move through the seven parables (the burning house, the poor son, the medicinal herbs, the conjured city, the jewel in the robe, the jewel in the topknot…), the Devadatta chapter, Never-Disparaging Bodhisattva, the "Universal Gate" chapter of Avalokiteśvara, the immeasurable lifespan, and close by foreshadowing the Lotus–Avataṃsaka "star–black hole" pairing. Part Seven will let you see that all the cutting and judging of the first six parts, seen from the vantage of the one buddha-vehicle, are a single thing.

**Part Eight · manifestation: unfolding with the Avataṃsaka**

Central question: what does the *dharma-realm* originally look like? Central scripture: the Avataṃsaka Sūtra. Central stance: the *Ocean Seal Samādhi*. Twelve chapters take Samantabhadra's ten great vows as their spine and the ten profound gates as their windows, unfolding the full panorama of dharma-realm dependent arising—the Ocean Seal Samādhi, the place of Yogācāra within the fivefold doctrinal classification, the four dharma-realms, repenting karmic obstructions, rejoicing in merit, Indra's net, requesting the Buddhas to remain in the world, the perfect fusion of the six characteristics, Sudhana's southward journey, universal dedication, the ten great vows beneath the ten windows, and closing with chapter twelve, "trust-understand-practice-inquiry-cutting-illumination-gathering-in-manifestation—the single mark of the dharma-realm in The Eight Movements." This chapter itself carries the function of the book's final chapter: Samantabhadra's tenth vow, universal dedication, returns to the first vow, paying homage to all Buddhas, and returns to the initial aspiration of Part One, chapter one. The whole road bends into a circle, walking from Part Eight back to Part One.

**The seven transition chapters**

Between each pair of parts is wedged one short transition chapter—from trust to understand, from understand to practice, from practice to inquiry, from inquiry to cutting, from cutting to illumination, from illumination to gathering-in, from gathering-in to manifestation. Each runs about 700–1,300 characters, much shorter than the main chapters. Their function is not to be a bridge—a bridge is a load-bearing structure, spanning two banks. They are **a seam**—two pieces of cloth that already line up, needing only a thread to draw the join tight.

How to read them: when you finish a part, turn to the transition chapter; take no effort over it, treat it as one breath between two parts. But the transition chapters are not decoration—each one is doing a single thing: pointing out the misreading peculiar to that seam, making one middle-path correction, and then passing the baton. To skip a transition chapter is to lose none of the main content; but to read one is to see clearly the invisible seam between two parts.

## II. Three Reading Paths: Pick One and Start Walking

This book can be read many times over. The first reading and the second are not read the same way, and the second and third differ again. Below are three paths that others have already walked through to the end; you can pick one to begin with, or mix them.

### Path One: Straight Through—for the First-Time Reader

**How to walk it**: Begin with the general preface, turn through the reading map, enter Part One trust, and go down chapter by chapter; do not skip the transition chapters, and read the appendices last.

**Why**: The sequence of the eight parts has an internal logic—trust, understand, practice, inquiry, cutting, illumination, gathering-in, manifestation: every word follows from the one before it. Skipping around loses the spine—you might, reading the Śūraṅgama in Part Six, with no edge of the Diamond Sūtra from Part Five laid down beneath you, misread illumination as a mystical experience.

**What to do when you hit a chapter you cannot follow**: **skip it outright, do not go back**. There are usually two reasons a chapter resists you: one is that its meaning has not yet reached where you stand at this moment (then skipping is right, and you will come back to it later); the other is that your spirits are simply low today (then read it again tomorrow and it is fine). But "not following one chapter" is never a reason to stop the whole book.

**A note on pace**: One chapter a day. Rest a day or two when you finish a part, let the meaning settle, then enter the transition chapter and the next part. Reading the whole book through once takes roughly three to four months. Please do not rush—this book is not for finishing, it is for walking through.

### Path Two: By Theme—for the Reader Who Comes In with a Question

**How to walk it**: Do not begin with Part One; begin with the one question you most need today. Below are some common questions and the entrances that match them.

**"I have AI anxiety—I do not know what meaning practice still has in this era"** → all of Part Four (inquiry), paired with chapters three, four, and five of Part One (the Yogācāra placement of the LLM, AI generates no merit, making good use of AI as a contributing condition). After that, if you want to go deeper, the fifty skandha-māras of the Śūraṅgama in Part Six will show you where the most dangerous subtle misrecognitions of the AI age lie.

**"A loved one has just died, and my heart cannot let go"** → all of Part Two (understand), and especially chapter ten, the mind without obstruction—passing through terror to ultimate nirvāṇa. After that, look at chapter eight of Part Five, "the Tathāgata cannot be seen through appearances"—the dharma-body is neither form nor mind.

**"My attention has collapsed—I cannot get through any long text, and I am even afraid I will not finish this book"** → Part One, chapter nine, the preparation of the vessel. Read that chapter, then decide whether to keep going. If you decide to continue, go back and read straight through from Part One.

**"I want to understand what 'everything is mind-made' really means, but I get stuck every time I read the scriptures"** → all of Part Three (practice). Fourteen chapters plus a supplementary chapter looks daunting, but this is the map that takes the two words "mind-made" apart to their finest grain. Once you finish Part Three you will no longer feel that "everything is mind-made" is a slogan.

**"I have strange states in my sitting meditation, and I do not know whether I am possessed"** → the fifty skandha-māras of the Śūraṅgama in Part Six (chapters eight through thirteen). Before you read them, please read chapters one through seven of Part Six first as groundwork; do not start straight from the skandha-demons—that would frighten you.

**"I want to know why The Eight Movements walks back to Part One at the end, why the Avataṃsaka joins back to trust"** → all of Part Eight, especially chapter eleven, the ten great vows beneath the ten windows, and chapter twelve.

**"I only want to read one part—pick the most essential"** → I would suggest Part Two, understand (the Heart Sūtra). The Heart Sūtra is the shortest, but its meaning covers the widest ground; once you finish it, every one of the other seven parts becomes easier to enter. If you finish Part Two and want to pick one more, then Part Seven, gathering-in (the Lotus Sūtra)—the vantage of the one buddha-vehicle will let you read every preceding part over again.

### Path Three: Scholarly—for the Reader Who Wants to Verify the Sources

**How to walk it**: The book is the entrance, the papers are the depth. Each chapter opens by marking the corresponding original paper number (for example, "this chapter corresponds to S2-P04"); when you want to pursue the scholarly detail of an argument, the CBETA Taishō canon source, or the full literature of an interdisciplinary synthesis, turn to the scholarly footnote index in Appendix A, or go straight to the `papers/` directory on GitHub for the corresponding paper in its Chinese or English version.

**The division of labor between book and papers**: The book is a continuous path; the papers are discrete nodes. For the sake of readability, every passage in the book has cut a great deal of scholarly footnotes, CBETA fascicle and page numbers, and the full paragraphs of interdisciplinary synthesis—all of which are in the papers. If you are a researcher, if you want to cite or to critique, please use the paper version, not the book version.

**For English readers**: The book's v1.0 is in traditional Chinese. The English v2.0 is not planned to begin until after v1.0 is published. But the 88 papers already have English scholarly versions, which can be found on GitHub—`S{N}-P{NN}_{kebab-case-title}.md` is the English filename.

## IV. Term-Forerunner Table: Meet These Twelve Words Before Reading the Main Text

Every term in the main text is explained on the spot, in brackets or with a dash, the first time it appears (BOOK_STYLE discipline two); this is each concept's "in-person introduction." But twelve words run through the whole book, and if you have a rough impression of them before entering Part One, the rest will read much more smoothly. The list below is not a dictionary of terms (the full definitions are in Appendix C); it is only the "minimum-threshold" seatbelt.

| Term | Impression in One Line |
|------|-----------|
| Yogācāra | Buddhism's most precise system for describing "how the mind works"—the underlying grammar of The Eight Movements |
| the eight consciousnesses | mind taken apart into eight layers—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind, manas, ālaya; the first six handle perception, manas handles the sense of self, ālaya handles memory and karma |
| ālaya-vijñāna | the deepest storehouse of mind-consciousness, holding the seeds left behind by all past physical and mental activity |
| manas | the ever-active but unseen sense of "I," which grasps the perceiving aspect of the ālaya-vijñāna as "self" |
| seed / actualization | the latent force left behind by an action is called a seed; a seed coming into operation is actualization; the actualization perfumes new seeds in turn, and so the cycle goes |
| the three natures | three angles for contemplating one and the same object—the imputed nature (what I forcibly add), the dependent nature (what arises from conditions), and the perfected nature (what is originally so) |
| prajñā | not knowledge, but the capacity to see reality directly—first of the six perfections |
| emptiness | not "nothing," but "no independent, unchanging self-nature"—often misread as nihilism |
| the middle path | neither grasping at existence nor grasping at emptiness—the precise position that leans on neither side |
| tathāgatagarbha | the awakened nature originally complete in all sentient beings—sometimes equated with the ālaya-vijñāna, sometimes the pure aspect of the ālaya-vijñāna |
| the ten profound gates | the ten windows the Huayan school uses to describe the dharma-realm, each looking at the same object from a different angle |
| Samantabhadra's ten great vows | the ten great vows of practice the Buddha himself speaks in the "Practices and Vows of Samantabhadra" chapter of the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, from "paying homage to all Buddhas" to "universal dedication" |

If, having read this far, all twelve of these words feel unfamiliar, please do not panic—the work of this book is to make them clear. Unfamiliarity is the starting point, not the obstacle. If you already have an impression of more than half of them, then you already have a foundation, and can go straight into Part One.

## V. How to Use the Appendices

The two appendices at the end of the book are tools for "stopping to verify," not chapters for "reading straight on through":

**[Appendix A · Scholarly Footnote Index](/view/book/appendix/A/)**. For the sake of readability, the main text compresses T numbers (Taishō canon numbers), fascicle counts, and page numbers into a chapter-level index. When a reader, on reaching a passage, wants to verify the source of a quotation, [turn to Appendix A](/view/book/appendix/A/) and find the corresponding chapter row (for example, "6.13"); there you will see the corresponding paper number (S6-P13) and the principal scriptures that paper cites (T numbers); to obtain fascicle counts, page numbers, and exact translations, go to the [`papers/`](https://github.com/pointing-at-the-moon/dharma-research/tree/main/papers) directory of the GitHub repo for the corresponding paper version, which contains the full CBETA-collated footnotes. Researchers and those making scholarly citations should take the paper version as authoritative.

**[Appendix C · Glossary of Terms](/view/book/appendix/C/)**. When you meet a word you do not recognize in the main text, turn here. Each entry is arranged in the format "Chinese (Sanskrit / Pali) — one-line definition — first scriptural source — chapters in this book where it unfolds." Sometimes a word has different uses in different scriptures (for example, "tathāgatagarbha" is not entirely the same in the Śrīmālā Sūtra and the Awakening of Mahāyāna Faith), and Appendix C will list these in separate entries. The v1.0 edition includes 62 core terms, and future editions will add new terms in step as the other axes of Waken unfold them (no quantity target is set; the sole criterion for inclusion is "the words the reader actually meets in this book").

> **Why is there no separate appendix for the interdisciplinary paragraphs?** The interdisciplinary synthesis paragraph in each chapter of the main text (quantum physics, complex systems, neuroscience, CBT/ACT, predictive processing, behavioral economics, AI and machine learning) is compressed to a third (BOOK_STYLE discipline four), and is strictly marked "⚠️ a heuristic analogy, not a physical proof of the Dharma." The full interdisciplinary argument, literature citations, and the boundaries of its assumptions are already unfolded as separate sections in the corresponding paper version (papers/)—a separate "Appendix B" would duplicate the papers. Readers who want the full interdisciplinary synthesis should read the corresponding paper directly.

## VI. What to Do When You Get Stuck

One last thing. This book is not light reading, and there are certain chapters where you are bound to get stuck. This is not your problem, nor the book's problem—it is simply the way the matter of practice is. The three pieces of advice below are written for the times you get stuck.

**First, if you cannot read on, skip**. The eight parts come to ninety-one chapters in all; skipping one chapter will not lose you the whole road, and neither will skipping a part. The most common case is the Yogācāra precision-analysis of Part Three (practice) leaving the first-time reader dizzy, and the fifty skandha-māras of Part Six (illumination) leaving the first-time reader afraid. Both parts can be skipped for now—come back after reading the parts around them, and you can usually read your way in. **The only things that cannot be skipped are the general preface and Part One, chapter one**—these two are the spine, and without them the whole road collapses.

**Second, repeatedly failing to follow one part may be a hint from fate**. If you have read the same part three times and still cannot follow it, yet you keep being drawn to it—this is often not the book's problem; it is that the scripture matching that part is your work for this lifetime. Please set this book down and go straight to the original scripture of that part (for Part Two, read the Heart Sūtra; for Part Five, the Diamond Sūtra; for Part Seven, the Lotus Sūtra). Once you have spent some time with the original, come back and read the corresponding part of this book, and you will find that the passages you could not follow before come clear all at once. The book is a map, but the feet must walk on the ground.

**Third, never deny the whole book because of one passage you cannot follow**. This is the trick manas most often plays—reading a passage it does not follow, manas instantly translates "I cannot follow it" into "this book is badly written" or "this teaching has a flaw," and thereby protects the sense of self that "I am clever." Please notice this move, and then set it gently down. The true meaning of "cannot follow" is usually only "the time is not yet"—not that the book is wrong, nor that you are dull. Part Three, chapter four, on manas, will make this mechanism clear, and when you read it you will laugh out loud.

One final reminder: this book is for being read through, but even more for keeping you company along a stretch of road. Finishing is not the end. There are passages you may not truly understand until ten years from now—and through those ten years the book waits quietly on the shelf for you to come back. The pointing finger is not the moon, and the book is not the road—but if, while you are walking, it can now and then hand you a reliable staff, then this book has already done what it was meant to do.
